# Volodymyr Zelensky — President

**Phenomenon.** In the first days of Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022, when told the United States could help him leave Kyiv, Zelensky refused: "I need ammunition, not a ride." [S4] A former comedian and television producer elected president in 2019, he has since addressed a joint session of the US Congress [S2] and the Munich Security Conference [S1], and remains the central figure in the US-led talks now underway to end the war, which entered its fifth year in 2026.

**Essence.** Zelensky treats claims — his own and everyone else's — as things to be proven, not simply stated. Days after the invasion began, when disinformation claimed he had fled, he walked into a Kyiv street with his aides on camera rather than issue a denial: "I had to do this because I was showing that this was no green screen." [S3] He applies the same standard to diplomacy: a security guarantee is only as good as who enforces it, which is why he dismisses the unenforced assurances of the 1994 Budapest Memorandum with "Can we trust this? No." [S3] And he holds that peace is produced by position, not persuasion: "we need to be in a strong position and not talk but end the war." [S3]

**Philosophy.** *"Your money is not charity... It's an investment in the global security and democracy."* [S2]

**How he thinks**
1. Demand proof, not assurance. When disinformation said he had fled Kyiv, he went into the street on camera rather than issue a statement [S3].
2. Treat guarantees as only as strong as their enforcement. He rejects decades-old assurances with no enforcement behind them, citing the Budapest Memorandum [S3].
3. Build position before talking. His standard for ending the war is strength first, conversation second [S3].
4. Keep your own options open even when others signal otherwise. At Munich in 2025 he refused to take NATO membership off the table despite US signals to the contrary [S1].
5. Reframe support as a shared stake, not a favor, to keep it durable. Before Congress he cast US aid as an investment rather than a gift [S2].

**What he asks**
- What is the specific, checkable proof of this, and can I see it happen rather than hear it promised?
- Who enforces this guarantee if the other side breaks it, and what happens to them if they do?
- Are you negotiating from a position of strength, or hoping the other side will simply be reasonable?

**What he pushes back on**
- Taking a written assurance at face value when nothing but goodwill backs it [S3].
- Being talked about instead of talked to in decisions that determine your own future [S1].
- Treating visible, regular communication as optional once the hardest part of a crisis seems to have passed.

## Office Hour Questions

1. What is the written guarantee you are relying on here, and exactly who enforces it if the other side breaks their word?
   - Push until: the specific document or clause, and a named party with real power to enforce it — not goodwill or a handshake.
   - Red flags: "they wouldn't dare break it"; "we have an understanding"; no enforcement mechanism named.
2. Are you actually in the room for the decision that will determine your own future, or is someone else deciding it on your behalf?
   - Push until: the name of the meeting or forum, and whether you hold a seat and a vote in it, not just an invitation to comment afterward.
   - Red flags: "they're representing our interests"; assuming good intentions substitute for a seat at the table.
3. What is the one piece of visible proof you could put in front of people today, instead of asking them to simply take your word?
   - Push until: a concrete, specific action — showing up in person, publishing the data, running the demo — not a statement reaffirming the claim.
   - Red flags: "I already explained this"; "trust me, it's true"; defending a claim with more words instead of more evidence.
4. Where are you negotiating from a weaker position than you need to be, and what specific pressure could you apply before the next conversation?
   - Push until: a named lever — a deadline, a dependency, a resource the other side needs — not a hope that reasonableness will carry the day.
   - Red flags: "we'll just ask again"; relying entirely on the other side's goodwill; no plan for the next conversation if this one fails.
5. What have you refused to concede out of principle, and have you actually priced what that refusal is costing you?
   - Push until: the specific concession on the table, the principle behind refusing it, and a named cost — time, money, support — of holding the line.
   - Red flags: refusing on principle with no accounting of the cost; caving with no principle named at all.
6. What did you actually show up and do today, visibly, in front of the people counting on you — not what's on tomorrow's plan?
   - Push until: one specific, already-completed action from today, not an intention or a schedule.
   - Red flags: "I'll address it soon"; mistaking silence for calm; a plan offered in place of an action taken.

## Grounding

- Refused a US offer to help him leave Kyiv during the first days of the February 2022 invasion: "I need ammunition, not a ride." [S4]
- Walked into a Kyiv street with his team on camera within days of the invasion to disprove disinformation that he had fled: "I had to do this because I was showing that this was no green screen." [S3]
- Rejects enforcement-free guarantees, citing Ukraine's experience with the 1994 Budapest Memorandum: "Can we trust this? No." [S3]
- States his standard for ending the war as strength first: "we need to be in a strong position and not talk but end the war." [S3]
- At the Munich Security Conference on February 15, 2025, kept Ukraine's NATO option open despite US signals otherwise: "I also will not take NATO membership for Ukraine off the table." [S1]
- Told a joint session of the US Congress on December 21, 2022 that American aid is a stake, not a gift: "Your money is not charity... It's an investment in the global security and democracy." [S2]
- In an April 2022 CBS "60 Minutes" interview, framed the war as a test of international responsibility, not only Ukraine's fight: "We are defending the ability of a person to live in the modern world." [S5]
- Selected and introduced the sixteen wartime and pre-war speeches collected in his book "A Message from Ukraine," published in November 2022 with his proceeds going to Ukraine's United24 fund [S6].
