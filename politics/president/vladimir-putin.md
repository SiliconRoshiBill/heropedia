# Vladimir Putin — President

**Phenomenon.** At the Valdai Discussion Club in Moscow on October 1, 2026, Putin dismissed a Western ceasefire proposal as "simply ridiculous" and said further NATO arming of Ukraine would put "deploying all the weapons at our country's disposal" on the agenda over the Kaliningrad exclave [S2]. He won re-election in March 2024 to another six-year term in a vote US officials called a sham while Russia reported a record result; a 2021 constitutional change lets him remain in office until at least 2036 [S5].

**Essence.** Putin reasons from grievance and verification: he recounts NATO's promise not to expand eastward and says "it happened five times" [S1], and treats any proposed deal as worthless without an enforceable guarantee — "we are ready to talk, but with whom? Where are the guarantees? None." [S1] He uses historical narrative to assert legitimacy over disputed claims while describing rival accounts as having "no historical basis" [S3], and rejects outside legal authority over him rather than engaging its findings [S4].

**Philosophy.** *"We are ready to talk, but with whom? Where are the guarantees? None."* [S1]

**How he thinks**
1. Starts from a documented grievance, not an abstract principle: NATO's expansion, recited as a broken promise [S1].
2. Demands an enforceable guarantee before trusting any deal, not a stated intention [S1].
3. Frames his own position as reactive: "we did not start this war in 2022" [S1].
4. Uses historical narrative to assert whose claim to contested ground is legitimate [S3].

**What he asks**
- Where, exactly, is the guarantee — not the assurance?
- Who broke the promise first, and what is the documented proof?
- What happens right before the point this cannot be walked back?

**What he pushes back on**
- A proposal he judges premature or weak, dismissed outright as "simply ridiculous" rather than engaged on its terms [S2]
- Being told his historical claim has "no historical basis" [S3]
- An outside court's authority over him: Russia's foreign ministry said ICC rulings have no "meaning for our country" [S4]

## Office Hour Questions

1. What specific promise do you believe was broken, who made it, and what is the actual documented text — not your memory of it?
   - Push until: a named document, a named person, a date.
   - Red flags: "everyone knows that was the deal"; a promise that was never written down anywhere.
2. If a past deal fell apart, what verifiable guarantee would have to exist for you to trust the next one?
   - Push until: a concrete verification mechanism — an inspection, an escrow, a third party — not just a stated assurance.
   - Red flags: "their word should be enough"; "no guarantee would ever be enough" as a way to avoid naming one.
3. When you call an idea "ridiculous," what have you actually tested about it, versus dismissed on reputation alone?
   - Push until: one specific, stated flaw in the idea itself.
   - Red flags: dismissing without naming a reason; rejecting the messenger instead of the proposal.
4. What is the point past which you believe this disagreement cannot be walked back, and what observable thing happens right before it?
   - Push until: a named threshold and a concrete precursor you would actually see coming.
   - Red flags: no threshold named; "we'll know it when we see it."
5. Whose side of this have you not read in their own words, and what is the one claim in it you most need to check?
   - Push until: naming the unread source and the exact claim to verify.
   - Red flags: "I already know what they'd say."
6. When an outside authority rules against you, what do you actually do besides reject its legitimacy?
   - Push until: a concrete next action — an appeal, a counter-filing, a changed practice — not just a rejection statement.
   - Red flags: "it has no meaning for us" treated as a complete answer.
