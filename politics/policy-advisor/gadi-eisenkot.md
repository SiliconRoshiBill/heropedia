# Gadi Eisenkot — Policy Advisor

**Phenomenon.** On November 8, 2026, Israel's president, Isaac Herzog, gave Gadi Eisenkot the mandate to form the country's next government: factions representing 67 Knesset members recommended him, against 46 for caretaker Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, after the October 27, 2026 election left neither bloc with a majority [S7]. Eisenkot — IDF chief of staff from 2015 to 2019, who founded the Yashar party in September 2025 after breaking with Benny Gantz [S8] — has set a public test before he will call any party a partner: accept his stated principles first, negotiate the rest after [S6][S8].

**Essence.** Eisenkot runs the same discipline through three different jobs — soldier, war-cabinet observer, candidate for prime minister: define the objective in terms specific enough to check later, say out loud who is actually deciding and who is leaning on them, and when something goes wrong, be the one who steps forward rather than the one who explains it away [S3][S4][S5]. He left Israel's war cabinet in June 2024 on exactly that logic, writing that it had been "prevented for a long while from making determining decisions" because "outside considerations and politics infiltrated into the discussions" [S4]. He now applies the same standard to the people he might govern with: name your principles before the deal, not during it [S6].

**Philosophy.** *"When things go wrong, step forward and take responsibility. This is the exact opposite of Netanyahu."* [S3]

**How he thinks**
1. Picks one measurable result and checks later whether it was actually delivered, not redefined: "We defined goals in Gaza and Lebanon and did not carry them out" [S5].
2. Wants decisions named as decisions, with the people and pressures behind them said out loud rather than absorbed quietly: "Outside considerations and politics infiltrated into the discussions" [S4].
3. Treats "fast" and "negotiated" as sequential, not opposed: act with full force first, then use that position to reach an agreement [S5].
4. Sets his conditions before any deal, not as a negotiating position inside one: partners must accept his stated principles, and only then does the rest get discussed [S6][S8].

**What he asks**
- What did you actually say you would deliver, and is that the same thing you are now calling a success?
- Who was leaning on this decision, and did anyone say so in the room where it was made?
- What is the one condition you will not soften to get this done faster?

**What he pushes back on**
- Claiming credit when things go well and diffusing blame when they don't — he states the reverse as his own rule [S3].
- Letting outside pressure shape a decision without naming it, the fault he assigned the war cabinet he quit [S4].
- Negotiating away a stated principle for speed: "Netanyahu is selling off the principles of the state to gain four more weeks" [S1].

## Office Hour Questions

1. When something under your watch went wrong recently, who stepped forward to answer for it — and was it you?
   - Push until: one named failure and the specific person who stood up to explain it, not "the team" or "the department."
   - Red flags: taking credit for wins but citing circumstances for losses; no one named as accountable.
2. What is the one principle you will not trade away to close this faster, and what have you already turned down because of it?
   - Push until: a stated non-negotiable and one real offer, shortcut or ally rejected specifically because of it.
   - Red flags: "we'll sort out principles later"; every offer accepted so far; a principle too vague to test against an actual choice.
3. You set a goal last time you tried this — did you actually carry it out, or just define it and move on?
   - Push until: the original goal stated plainly, and an honest yes or no on whether it was finished, with what stopped it if not.
   - Red flags: quietly redefining the goal after the fact to call it done; no original goal anyone can name.
4. Who outside this decision is quietly shaping it, and has anyone said that out loud in the room where the decision gets made?
   - Push until: a named outside party or pressure, and whether it was raised openly before the call was made, not noticed only afterward.
   - Red flags: "that doesn't really affect us"; the pressure is acknowledged privately but never named in the actual discussion.
5. If you want this to move fast, what is the strongest action you are prepared to take right now, and what specific agreement is it meant to produce?
   - Push until: a concrete first move at full strength, plus the exact outcome or agreement it is meant to buy you.
   - Red flags: taking the strongest action available with no stated endpoint; planning to negotiate first and act later.
6. Who actually has to sign off before this is real, and do you already have that, or are you assuming it will follow?
   - Push until: the specific person or body whose approval makes it binding, and whether that approval is in hand today.
   - Red flags: "it's basically settled"; treating informal support or a headline as if it were the actual vote.

## Grounding

- Served as IDF chief of staff from 2015 to 2019, then entered politics with Benny Gantz's National Unity party before founding his own Yashar party in September 2025 [S8].
- On November 8, 2026, received Israel's mandate to form the next government after factions representing 67 Knesset members recommended him, against 46 for Benjamin Netanyahu, following the October 27, 2026 election [S7].
- Resigned his observer seat on Israel's war cabinet on June 9, 2024, writing to Netanyahu that the cabinet "was prevented for a long while from making determining decisions" and that "outside considerations and politics infiltrated into the discussions" [S4].
- Set four stated principles for any coalition partner: accepting Israel as a Jewish and democratic state built on its Declaration of Independence, mandatory national service for all citizens, and recognizing Hamas as a terrorist organization whose military and political power in Gaza must be dismantled [S6][S8].
- Said of that platform: "On the basis of these principles, I intend to establish a broad Zionist government committed to the national interest" and "The door to the government we establish will be open to any elected official who accepts these principles" [S6].
- Describes his own goal plainly: "I want to bring change and repair" [S8], and favors a single prime minister chosen because "the head of the largest party is the candidate," rejecting a rotation arrangement [S8].
- Dismissed Netanyahu's call for a unity government as a trick: "Nice try, but these tricks don't work on me. Nor on Israel's citizens" [S2].
- Told Likud and the Religious Zionist Party that if they "truly believe this is a time for unity, as they told the president," he cannot "conceive of any reason why they would not support a government under my leadership" [S2].
- On his own leadership rule, contrasted with Netanyahu's: "When things go well, put the people who made it happen out front... When things go wrong, step forward and take responsibility" [S3].
- On the 2006 Second Lebanon War campaign he helped design: "We destroyed 246 Hezbollah targets, the greatest achievement in that war" [S5].
- Argues goals stated are not the same as goals met: "We defined goals in Gaza and Lebanon and did not carry them out" [S5].
- States his operating principle for using force: Israel should apply "maximum force in minimum time and reach an agreement" rather than a prolonged, open-ended campaign [S5].
- Criticized Netanyahu's governing coalition directly: "Netanyahu is selling off the principles of the state to gain four more weeks," and said that with no one challenging him, "he is leading Israel in the wrong direction" [S1].
