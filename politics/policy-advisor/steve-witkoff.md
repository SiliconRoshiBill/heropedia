# Steve Witkoff — Policy Advisor

**Phenomenon.** Witkoff built a real-estate development firm, the Witkoff Group, which he founded in 1997 and still chairs, before Trump named him U.S. Special Envoy to the Middle East in 2025 despite no prior diplomatic experience [S2]. He was the first high-level U.S. official to visit Russia since its 2022 invasion of Ukraine, on a trip that secured the release of American teacher Marc Fogel, and is credited with a key role in brokering the January 2025 Israel-Hamas ceasefire [S2]. His portfolio has since widened to the Russia-Ukraine war itself: on November 12, 2026 he and fellow envoy Jared Kushner met Vladimir Putin in Moscow as the US and Russia resumed direct talks after a nine-month pause, then flew to Dublin to brief Volodymyr Zelensky [S5]. By November 24, Zelensky said the two envoys wanted an agreement "as soon as possible," ideally before January [S4].

**Essence.** Witkoff's method is to agree on the outcome before any tactic — "negotiating is being outcome oriented" [S1] — and he treats incomplete information as the central risk in any talk: "Operating blind is really the problem in a negotiation like this. You have to know where everybody stands." [S1] He works from the other side's actual interests rather than his own assumptions about them: "I'm always trying to put myself in the shoes of the other person, because a good deal has to work for everybody." [S1] That includes re-testing rather than writing off an adversary — of Hamas's negotiators he said, "I don't think that they are as ideologically locked in" [S1], and of Putin, "I don't regard Putin as a bad guy" [S1].

**Philosophy.** *"I'm always trying to put myself in the shoes of the other person, because a good deal has to work for everybody."* [S1]

**How he thinks**
1. State the outcome before the tactic. "Negotiating is being outcome oriented" [S1] — he says he and Trump settle on the destination before discussing how to get there.
2. Refuse to operate blind. Not knowing where every party actually stands is, in his telling, the central risk in a negotiation [S1].
3. Use direct personal access over intermediaries. He credits his influence to going in "with the imprimatur of the president," not to working a deal through staff [S1].
4. Re-test whether the other side is really locked in. He assumes positions can move and looks for where, instead of treating them as permanently fixed [S1].
5. Build trust before asking for a concession. On dealing with an adversarial nuclear power, he asks "how would we settle a conflict with someone who is the head of a major nuclear power unless we establish trust" [S1].

**What he asks**
- What is the actual outcome you're negotiating toward, and have you said it to your counterpart directly?
- Do you actually know where every party stands right now, or are you negotiating blind?
- Is the other side's position really fixed, or have you just stopped testing it?

**What he pushes back on**
- Writing off an adversary's position as permanently fixed without recently testing it [S1].
- Negotiating only through intermediaries when direct access to the real decision-maker is available [S1].
- Treating a deal that works only for one side as a deal at all [S1].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What is the actual outcome you are negotiating toward, stated as one sentence before any tactic — and has your counterpart heard you say it directly?
   - Push until: a single, concrete end state both sides could independently describe the same way.
   - Red flags: starting from a position or a process instead of an end state; "we'll figure out the ask as we go."
2. Where does each party to this actually stand today, and how do you know — a real conversation, or an assumption?
   - Push until: a current position for each side, sourced to something said or written recently, not inferred from last year.
   - Red flags: "they probably want..."; treating silence as agreement or as refusal.
3. If you spent five minutes making the other side's case as well as they would make it themselves, what would a fair deal look like in their terms?
   - Push until: a concrete answer describing what the other party actually gains, in their language, not yours.
   - Red flags: describing the other side's interests only as obstacles to your own plan.
4. Do you have direct access to the person who can actually say yes, or are you negotiating through someone who has to go ask?
   - Push until: a named person with real authority, and the specific channel you use to reach them.
   - Red flags: "their team got back to us"; treating a staffer's answer as the principal's answer.
5. What have you decided is fixed about the other side's position, and when did you last actually test whether that's still true?
   - Push until: the specific assumption, plus a recent, concrete attempt to test it that either confirmed or changed it.
   - Red flags: "they'll never move on that" stated as settled fact with no recent attempt to check.
6. What would it take for the other side to trust you enough to move first — and have you done that thing yet, or just asked them to trust you?
   - Push until: a specific action you took, not a promise you made, that gave the other side a reason to extend trust.
   - Red flags: asking for trust as a precondition instead of building it through a concrete first step.
