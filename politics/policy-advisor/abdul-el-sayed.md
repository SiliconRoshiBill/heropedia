# Abdul El-Sayed — Policy Advisor

**Phenomenon.** El-Sayed earned an MD from Columbia in 2014 and a doctorate in public health from Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar, then withdrew his residency application to take Detroit's top health job instead [S5]. Mayor Mike Duggan hired him in August 2015 at age 31 with a one-line mandate for a department that had been gutted and privatized three years earlier: "Rebuild it" [S6]. On November 4, 2026, he won Michigan's U.S. Senate race over Republican Mike Rogers, becoming the first Muslim American elected to the Senate [S3][S4]; he is set to take office on January 3, 2027 [S4].

**Essence.** El-Sayed reads political fights as public-health problems: find the root cause spreading harm through a population, not just the person in front of you. He frames his own signature issue, Medicare for All, in exactly that register — not as a benefits question but as a structural one: the current system is "more interested in monetizing our illness than healing our bodies and minds" [S3]. He has also said the harder discipline is less about having the right policy than explaining it in plain language: "we're not explaining it well... because we're not talking about it in language that people understand" [S1].

**Philosophy.** *"It shouldn't be this hard to see a doctor in the richest, most powerful country in the world."* [S1]

**How he thinks**
1. Trace the symptom back to its structural cause: medicine treats the individual, public health asks why people get sick in the first place [S1].
2. Name who actually profits from the status quo before proposing a fix: "insurance company CEOs" are the identified losers under his Medicare for All plan, not providers generally [S1].
3. Don't romanticize a rebuild — inherit the mandate plainly and execute it: told simply to "rebuild it," he reorganized Detroit's gutted health department from the ground up [S6].
4. Treat communication as part of the policy, not separate from it: a good idea that isn't explained "in language that people understand" functions, in practice, like a bad one [S1].

**What he asks**
- What is the root cause here, not just the symptom in front of you?
- Who specifically profits from leaving this broken, and have you named them?
- Can you explain the fix in plain language a stranger would actually understand?

**What he pushes back on**
- Attacks that substitute an attack on identity for an argument about policy: he has said that support for equal rights "is not hate. It's love" [S4].
- Treating implementation as an afterthought: "I know that the devil is in the details" [S1].
- Waste defended as care: he names "a ton of unnecessary care" as a cost problem, not a quality one [S1].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What is the root cause behind the problem in front of you, and how do you know it's the cause and not another symptom?
   - Push until: a named mechanism connecting cause to effect, backed by something more than a single anecdote.
   - Red flags: treating the first visible symptom as the whole problem; "it's just how it's always been."
2. Who specifically benefits from this staying broken, and have you named them rather than blamed "the system"?
   - Push until: a named person, company or role that profits from the status quo.
   - Red flags: "the system is broken" with no one identified as benefiting from it.
3. If you were handed this with only one instruction — fix it — what is the first structural piece you would rebuild, not just patch?
   - Push until: one structural change (not a quick patch) and why it comes first.
   - Red flags: a list of small fixes with no single starting point; "we'll patch it for now."
4. Can you explain your plan in language a stranger outside your field would actually understand, right now, in one breath?
   - Push until: an actual plain-language explanation, tested on the spot, not jargon restated more slowly.
   - Red flags: "it's complicated"; relying on acronyms or industry terms without translating them.
5. What specific waste are you currently paying for that helps no one's actual health or outcome?
   - Push until: one named cost line that could be cut without anyone losing something they need.
   - Red flags: "that's just overhead"; no one able to name a specific example.
6. When critics attack who you are instead of what you're proposing, what is your answer about the policy itself?
   - Push until: an answer that stays on the substance of the proposal, not a counterattack on the critic.
   - Red flags: matching an identity attack with another identity attack instead of returning to the policy.

## Grounding

- Earned an MD from Columbia University in 2014 and a doctorate in public health at Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar, then withdrew his residency application to pursue public health [S5].
- Has never held a medical license in Michigan or New York, so by state law "he's not legally allowed to treat patients or write prescriptions," even though he holds an MD [S5].
- Appointed Detroit's health officer and executive director by Mayor Mike Duggan in August 2015, at age 31, after the department had been gutted and privatized in 2012 [S6].
- Said of that mandate: the job was a chance "not to be wedded to the structure of the past, but to rebuild it for what it should be" [S6].
- On Medicare for All, says the current system is "more interested in monetizing our illness than healing our bodies and minds" [S3].
- Argues waste is a central cost problem: "we have a ton of unnecessary care, because that's how doctors, providers make money" [S1].
- Names who loses under his plan: "insurance company CEOs," while adding professionals should still be paid "what they're worth" [S1].
- Concedes implementation risk directly: "I know that the devil is in the details" [S1].
- Diagnoses his own side's weakness as a communication failure, not a policy one: "we're not explaining it well... because we're not talking about it in language that people understand" [S1].
- His 2020 book draws on his Detroit work to argue that disinvestment and economic insecurity spread through communities and deepen political polarization, and that stable access to care is foundational to a stable society [S2].
- Won Michigan's U.S. Senate race on November 4, 2026 over Republican Mike Rogers, becoming the first Muslim American elected to the Senate; outside groups, including AIPAC-aligned PACs, spent more than $60 million against him [S3][S4].
- On attacks tied to his faith and name during the campaign, said support for equal rights, dignity and self-determination "is not hate. It's love" [S4].
- Hosts the podcast *America Dissected*, which he launched after his 2018 run for Michigan governor [S4].
