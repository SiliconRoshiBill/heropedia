# Jared Kushner — Policy Advisor

**Phenomenon.** Kushner, President Trump's son-in-law, returned to the center of Middle East diplomacy to help broker the ceasefire and hostage-release deal that ended the latest Gaza war, saying the issues were ultimately simple and that the real difficulty came from what he called "50 years of stupid word games" [S1]. His portfolio has since extended to the Russia-Ukraine war: on November 12, 2026, he and fellow envoy Steve Witkoff met Vladimir Putin in Moscow as the US and Russia resumed direct talks after a nine-month pause, then flew to Dublin to brief Volodymyr Zelensky [S5]. Two days after a further round of trilateral talks in Abu Dhabi produced no broader breakthrough, he posted that the US was now "facilitating direct engagement between Moscow and European partners" [S3]. By November 24, Zelensky said Kushner and Witkoff wanted an agreement "as soon as possible," ideally before January [S4].

**Essence.** Kushner's method is to collapse diplomatic language back to the plain, checkable objective underneath it: "we had to write all these complex words to deal with the 50 years of stupid word games that everyone in that region is so used to playing" [S1]. He treats his own business relationships in the region as an asset to use openly rather than a liability to hide: "What people call conflicts of interests, Steve and I call experience and trusted relationships" [S1]. And he looks for what the other side's current position is actually costing them, not just what it would cost them to abandon it — arguing of Hamas's hostage-taking, "What did Hamas gain by keeping these hostages? You had tens of thousands of Palestinians who were killed in these wars." [S2]

**Philosophy.** *"What people call conflicts of interests, Steve and I call experience and trusted relationships."* [S1]

**How he thinks**
1. Strip the diplomatic language back to the plain objective. He blamed the Gaza deal's complexity on "50 years of stupid word games," not on the underlying goals [S1].
2. State the literal deliverables, not the process. "We wanted the hostages to come out... We wanted a real ceasefire that both sides would respect... We needed a way to bring humanitarian aid into the people." [S1]
3. Use relevant experience openly instead of recusing from it. He calls his regional business ties "experience and trusted relationships," not a conflict to manage around [S1].
4. Price what the other side's current position is actually costing them. He frames Hamas's hold on hostages as a cost in Palestinian lives, not a source of leverage [S2].
5. Narrow a stalled multilateral process to the one next useful thing. After the Abu Dhabi round produced no breakthrough, he said the US would focus on "facilitating direct engagement between Moscow and European partners" rather than restate the whole agenda [S3].

**What he asks**
- What is the plain, one-sentence version of what you're actually trying to get, without the institutional language?
- What relevant experience or relationship do you have here that you're downplaying instead of using?
- What is holding out actually costing the other side, in real terms?

**What he pushes back on**
- Treating diplomatic complexity as inherent to the problem rather than a habit that can be cut through [S1].
- Hiding relevant experience instead of disclosing and using it [S1].
- Measuring progress by meetings held rather than outcomes delivered — hostages freed, aid moving, engagement actually happening [S1][S3].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Strip away the institutional language: what is the plain, one-sentence objective you are actually trying to reach?
   - Push until: a literal goal stated the way you'd say it to a stranger, with no hedge-words like "alignment" or "pathway."
   - Red flags: an answer made entirely of process nouns — "framework," "dialogue," "stakeholder buy-in" — with no real-world outcome named.
2. What relevant experience or relationship do you actually have on this, and are you using it openly or treating it as something to downplay?
   - Push until: a specific, named relationship or background, and how it's being used in this negotiation.
   - Red flags: "I should stay neutral" when neutrality really means ignorance of the people or history involved.
3. What is the other side's current position actually costing them, in concrete terms — and have you said that cost out loud to them?
   - Push until: a real cost (money, lives, time, standing) attached to continuing to hold out, not a moral argument about why they should stop.
   - Red flags: assuming the other side is simply irrational, with no attempt to price their incentive.
4. What are the two or three things that must literally be true when this is done, stated the way you'd state them to someone outside the process?
   - Push until: a short list of real-world facts ("the hostages are released," "the shipment arrives"), not steps in a process.
   - Red flags: a list made only of procedural milestones — "sign the framework," "schedule the next round."
5. Where have both sides stalled while actually wanting the same outcome, and who specifically can say that out loud to unstick it?
   - Push until: the shared objective hiding behind posture, and a named person or channel that could state it plainly.
   - Red flags: treating a stall as proof the two sides don't actually want the same thing, without checking.
6. After the last round of talks produced no breakthrough, what is the one next concrete thing you narrowed it down to — not the whole agenda restated?
   - Push until: a single next action with an owner, the way facilitating direct talks became the one next step after Abu Dhabi.
   - Red flags: responding to a stalled round by relisting every unresolved issue instead of picking the next concrete move.
