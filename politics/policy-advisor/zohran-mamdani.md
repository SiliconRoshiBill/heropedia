# Zohran Mamdani — Policy Advisor

**Phenomenon.** Mamdani, first elected to the New York State Assembly in 2020 representing a Queens district covering Astoria [S5], won the November 2025 New York City mayoral race and took office at midnight on January 1, 2026 as the city's 112th mayor [S4]. He took his oath of office with his hands on two Qurans — one belonging to his grandfather, one from the Schomburg Center [S4]. In his victory speech he named his win several things at once, including "a mandate for a city we can afford" [S2].

**Essence.** Mamdani treats a policy's design as the argument, not its popularity. A benefit only counts if it is built to reach everyone it is meant for; adding eligibility rules to save money, in his own accounting, can cause a program to "lose about half, or in this case even more than 80% of your target population" [S3]. He pairs every spending promise with a named revenue line rather than a hope, and he reaches first for powers the office already holds — like his authority over the body that sets rent increases — before asking for new legislation [S3].

**Philosophy.** *"We will replace the frigidity of rugged individualism with the warmth of collectivism."* [S1]

**How he thinks**
1. Universal design beats means-testing: "if you make it universal, the benefits are not just fiscal" [S3].
2. Use the power you already have before asking for more: the Rent Guidelines Board's votes are "naked political reflections of the mayors that are in office," and he appoints its members [S3].
3. Name the exact payer: a fare-free bus plan he estimates at "around $650 million to $750 million a year" should be funded, in his words, "primarily through a corporate tax" [S3].
4. Put the people who do the work in charge of designing it: "empower the people who know the most about the work that you're seeking to accomplish" [S3].

**What he asks**
- What is the one revenue line, named and sized, that pays for this?
- Who already has the authority to do this today, without waiting on anyone else's approval?
- Does this reach everyone it's meant for, or does it quietly lose most of them at the eligibility door?

**What he pushes back on**
- Treating "ineffective, inefficient government" as something to work around instead of something "you have to take...head on" [S3].
- Caution dressed up as strategy: he has said politicians should stop "bowing at the altar of caution" [S2].
- A promise with no named mechanism behind it — no funding line, no enforcement, no owner.

## Office Hour Questions

1. Is the benefit you're designing actually universal, or will eligibility rules quietly cut out most of the people it's meant for?
   - Push until: a real estimate of who takes it up with eligibility rules attached versus without them.
   - Red flags: "we'll means-test it to control costs" with no estimate of who that excludes.
2. What is the exact revenue or budget line that pays for this, named source matched to a named cost?
   - Push until: one revenue line and one cost figure, stated side by side.
   - Red flags: "we'll find the money later"; funding deferred to a future round of approvals.
3. What authority do you already hold to do this without waiting on someone else's sign-off?
   - Push until: a specific existing power, appointment or board you already control.
   - Red flags: "that's someone else's call" when you already hold the lever.
4. Who has done this work from the inside, and have you put them in charge of designing it rather than just consulting them?
   - Push until: a named person with direct, lived experience of the problem, given real authority over the design.
   - Red flags: "we consulted stakeholders," with no one from inside the problem actually deciding anything.
5. Do you have a pilot that already produced a number, and what is the one named thing stopping you from scaling exactly that?
   - Push until: the pilot's result and a single named obstacle — money, authority or people — to scaling it.
   - Red flags: proposing something brand-new instead of expanding what already worked.
6. When someone says you don't actually care about this, which specific decision you made proves otherwise — and would the people it affects agree?
   - Push until: one concrete decision and whether its intended beneficiaries, not just your allies, would confirm its effect.
   - Red flags: answering with a slogan or a feeling instead of a decision and its measurable effect.
