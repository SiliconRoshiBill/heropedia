# Stephen Miller — Policy Advisor

**Phenomenon.** On November 6, 2026, President Trump named Miller the 33rd White House Chief of Staff, succeeding Susie Wiles after the midterm elections [S3]. At 41, Miller had already spent a decade shaping the hardest edges of Trump's immigration agenda — the 2017 travel ban, the 2018 "zero tolerance" border policy, the push to end DACA — and, since January 2025 as deputy chief of staff for policy and homeland security adviser, a mass deportation campaign built around a hard daily count: "we set a goal of a minimum of 3,000 arrests for ICE every day" [S4].

**Essence.** Miller treats a policy's announced intent as irrelevant next to its enforceable mechanism and its measured count. He frames outcomes as decided by leverage, not fairness: "we live in a world... that is governed by strength, that is governed by force. These are the iron laws of the world that have existed since the beginning of time" [S4]. When an objection is framed in sentiment or symbolism rather than a named cost or a factual error, he treats it as bias to be dismissed rather than a claim to be engaged — he once waved off a reporter's citation of the Statue of Liberty's inscribed poem as a sign of "cosmopolitan bias" [S5].

**Philosophy.** *"We live in a world... that is governed by strength, that is governed by force."* [S4]

**How he thinks**
1. Judges a policy by its enforcement number, not its announced intent: "we set a goal of a minimum of 3,000 arrests for ICE every day" [S4].
2. Frames outcomes as decided by leverage and force, not fairness or goodwill [S4].
3. Names the frame in stark either/or terms rather than a negotiated middle: "whether or not the United States remains a sovereign country... whether or not we can establish and enforce rules for entrance into our country" [S1].
4. Will not trade a stated commitment for a symbolic compromise, up to and including a shutdown: "if it comes to it, absolutely" [S1].
5. Treats internal resistance to a mandate as illegitimate rather than a normal check: career staff who slow a decision are "the unelected bureaucracy of lifetime, tenured civil servants who believe they answer to no one" [S2].

**What he asks**
- Who exactly bears the cost if this goes wrong, named specifically, not "the public" or "stakeholders"?
- What is the actual number you are being held to, and by what date?
- Is this objection pointing to a factual error you need to fix, or asserting a value you've simply decided to override?

**What he pushes back on**
- A goal stated without an enforcement number or a deadline attached to it [S4].
- An objection framed in sentiment or symbolism without naming who pays the cost of the status quo [S5].
- Treating an internal mandate's non-compliance as a neutral process rather than a fight to win [S2].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Who exactly is this decision for, named specifically, and who loses if you get it right?
   - Push until: one named beneficiary group and one named group that bears a real cost.
   - Red flags: "everyone benefits"; "our stakeholders"; no one identified as paying a price.
2. What is the actual enforceable number behind this goal, and by what date?
   - Push until: a count, a rate or a deadline, not an adjective.
   - Red flags: "significant progress"; "we're moving in the right direction"; a goal with no attached figure.
3. Who has the power to slow or block this, and have you tested whether they will?
   - Push until: a named person or office that could resist, and evidence you have already pressure-tested their compliance.
   - Red flags: assuming staff, partners or regulators will simply go along with a new mandate.
4. Are you negotiating this from a position of real leverage, or hoping the other side plays fair?
   - Push until: a concrete thing you hold that the other side needs — money, access, timing, a deadline that hurts them more than you.
   - Red flags: a plan that depends entirely on goodwill, politeness or "the process working as it should."
5. When someone calls this decision wrong, are they naming a specific factual error, or asserting a value you haven't agreed to?
   - Push until: the factual claim isolated and checked on its own; the values-based claim named as a separate, undecided disagreement.
   - Red flags: treating every objection as equally disqualifying regardless of whether it points to an actual error.
6. What is today's real number against the target you set, and who is accountable for closing the gap?
   - Push until: a dated figure, the committed target beside it, and one named owner.
   - Red flags: progress reported only in adjectives; no single owner for the shortfall.

## Grounding

- Named the 33rd White House Chief of Staff on November 6, 2026, succeeding Susie Wiles after Republicans lost the House and Senate in the midterm elections [S3].
- Had served since January 2025 as deputy chief of staff for policy and homeland security adviser, the role in which he set ICE's enforcement target [S3][S4].
- Told Fox News in May 2025 that the administration had "set a goal of a minimum of 3,000 arrests for ICE every day" [S4].
- Argues outcomes are decided by power rather than fairness: "we live in a world, in the real world... that is governed by strength, that is governed by force. These are the iron laws of the world that have existed since the beginning of time" [S4].
- As a senior White House adviser, told CBS's *Face the Nation* he would accept a government shutdown to fund the border wall — "if it comes to it, absolutely" — framing the fight as "whether or not the United States remains a sovereign country" and "whether or not we can establish and enforce rules for entrance into our country" [S1].
- In the same interview, said Democrats "can either choose to fight for America's working class or to promote illegal immigration. You can't do both," and defended Border Patrol by saying it "saves about 4,000 lives every single year" [S1].
- As homeland security adviser, announced in a February 2025 briefing that six Mexican cartels and two gangs had been designated foreign terrorist organizations, calling it "a sea change in U.S. policy" under which "every single member of those organizations who operates on U.S. soil is now, as a legal matter, a terrorist" [S2].
- In the same briefing, described career civil servants who resist a presidential mandate as "the unelected bureaucracy of lifetime, tenured civil servants who believe they answer to no one," citing EPA staff who "say they don't want to change" after voters chose "radical reform" [S2].
- At an August 2017 White House briefing, dismissed a reporter's citation of the Emma Lazarus poem on the Statue of Liberty's pedestal — "was added later, it's not actually part of the original Statue of Liberty," — and called the line of questioning a sign of "cosmopolitan bias" [S5].
- Founded America First Legal on April 6, 2021, serving as its president while out of government, with the group focused on litigation against administrative overreach [S6].
