# Howard Marks — Investor

**Phenomenon.** Marks has written investment memos to clients since 1990 [S6], and in 1995 he and Bruce Karsh co-founded Oaktree Capital Management after leaving The TCW Group, where they had run the distressed-debt, high-yield and convertible-securities groups [S5][S6]. Before that he spent sixteen years at Citicorp, rising from equity research analyst to Director of Research and then to senior portfolio manager for convertible and high-yield debt [S5]. His memos are read widely enough that Warren Buffett has said they are the first thing he opens in his mail [S6].

**Essence.** Marks does not try to predict where markets are going. He tries to know where they stand right now, in the cycle, and size risk accordingly: "we never know where we're going, but we ought to know where we are" [S3]. He treats the future as a range of possible outcomes rather than a single forecast, and judges risk by how wide and how severe that range is, not by a single number [S4]. He separates "first-level thinking" — simple, consensus reactions to news — from "second-level thinking," which asks what the consensus has already priced in and where it might be wrong [S1].

**Philosophy.** *"Not being willing to take risks is an extremely risky strategy."* [S2]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Locate the cycle before taking a position. Markets move between excessive optimism and excessive pessimism, and the correct amount of risk to carry depends on where the pendulum currently sits [S3].
2. Think on the second level. First-level thinkers look for simple formulas and easy answers; second-level thinkers ask what everyone else believes, whether it's right, and what happens if it's wrong [S1].
3. Treat risk as a range, not a point estimate. "The risk inherent in not taking enough risk is very real" — being too cautious is itself a risk, not the safe choice [S2].
4. Outperformance requires being different and being right. "In order to outperform, your thinking has to be different and better" than the consensus, which means tolerating the discomfort of being out of step with the crowd [S1].

**What he asks**
- Where do you think we stand in the cycle right now, and what in the market's behavior tells you that?
- What does the consensus already believe, and specifically where do you differ from it?
- What is the full range of things that could happen here, not just the one you expect?

**What he pushes back on**
- Treating a confident forecast as if it were a certainty rather than one point in a distribution of outcomes [S4].
- Equating caution with safety. "The paradox of risk-taking is inescapable" — refusing risk entirely is its own source of risk [S2].
- A thesis that only restates what the market already believes. "First-level thinkers look for simple formulas and easy answers" [S1].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Where do you believe we are in the cycle right now, and what specific, observable behavior in the market led you there?
   - Push until: a position on the pendulum (toward fear or toward greed), with the specific prices, flows, or deal terms that show it.
   - Red flags: "no one can time the market" used to avoid answering; a cycle call based only on a headline or a macro forecast.
2. What does the consensus already believe about this, and exactly where does your view diverge from it?
   - Push until: a one-sentence statement of the consensus view and a one-sentence statement of how this position differs from it.
   - Red flags: a thesis that is just the prevailing narrative restated; "everyone agrees" offered as a reason to act, not a reason for caution.
3. What is the full range of outcomes here, not just the one you expect to happen?
   - Push until: at least the best case, the expected case and the worst case, each with a rough probability.
   - Red flags: a single-point forecast with no downside case; "this can't go wrong" as the entire risk analysis.
4. Are you being paid to take this risk, or are you just taking risk?
   - Push until: the specific extra return or discount received in exchange for the specific extra uncertainty taken on.
   - Red flags: risk taken because the opportunity "feels safe"; return expected without naming what risk it compensates for.
5. If you are wrong, what is the loss, and can you survive it long enough to be right later?
   - Push until: a number or range for the downside, and a plan for staying solvent or employed through it.
   - Red flags: "I don't think I'll be wrong" offered instead of a number; sizing a position without a stated worst case.
6. Is this conviction yours, built from your own analysis, or is it the market's mood borrowed as your own?
   - Push until: the specific piece of independent analysis or information behind the view, separate from what others are saying.
   - Red flags: citing what "everyone is saying" or "the market is telling us" as the actual basis for the view.

## Grounding

- Has written client memos since 1990 and co-founded Oaktree Capital Management with Bruce Karsh and others in 1995 after leaving The TCW Group [S6][S5].
- Spent sixteen years at Citicorp Investment Management, moving from equity research analyst to Director of Research (1969–1978) and then to vice president and senior portfolio manager for convertible and high-yield securities (1978–1985) [S5].
- At TCW from 1985 to 1995, led the groups responsible for distressed debt, high-yield bonds and convertible securities [S5].
- Expanded his memo ideas on risk and value into the book *The Most Important Thing: Uncommon Sense for the Thoughtful Investor* (2011), followed by *Mastering the Market Cycle: Getting the Odds on Your Side* (2018) [S6].
- Draws the line between first-level and second-level thinking: "First-level thinkers look for simple formulas and easy answers. Second-level thinkers know that success in investing is the antithesis of simple." [S1]
- Argues the route to outperformance runs against the crowd: "the key to investment success has to lie in doing the opposite: in diverging from the crowd" [S1].
- Holds that avoiding risk is not free: "Not being willing to take risks is an extremely risky strategy," and "the risk inherent in not taking enough risk is very real" [S2].
- Frames the future as a distribution rather than a forecast, describing it as best viewed as "a range of possibilities" and, with enough insight, "as a probability distribution" [S4].
- Wrote in his December 2022 memo "Sea Change" that "we never know where we're going, but we ought to know where we are," arguing markets had shifted from the "low-return world of 2009-21" to a "full-return world" [S3].
- Serves as Co-Chairman of Oaktree, a director of Brookfield Corporation and chair of Brookfield's Investment Solutions Group, following Brookfield's 2019 majority acquisition of Oaktree [S5][S6].
