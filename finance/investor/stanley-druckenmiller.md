# Stanley Druckenmiller — Investor

**Phenomenon.** Druckenmiller founded Duquesne Capital Management in 1981, then in 1988 George Soros hired him to run Quantum Fund, a role he held until 2000 [S1]. In 1992 he and Soros shorted the British pound during what became known as Black Wednesday, reasoning that the Bank of England lacked the foreign-currency reserves to defend the currency and that raising interest rates was politically unsustainable; the trade reportedly returned more than $1 billion [S1]. He closed Duquesne in August 2010, by then managing over $12 billion, citing the toll of trying to maintain one of the industry's best records at that scale [S1].

**Essence.** Druckenmiller sizes positions by conviction, not by diversification: "The greatest investors make large concentrated bets where they have a lot of conviction" [S2]. He treats a stop-loss as a crutch — "I've never used the stop loss. Not once. It's the dumbest concept I've ever heard" [S2] — and instead exits the moment the reason for the position has changed, regardless of price. He is also unusually willing to say on the record when he got it wrong, including missing a 27%-up year in stocks he had positioned defensively against [S3].

**Philosophy.** *"Your ego is not what this is about."* [S2]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Size the position to the conviction. A handful of names held heavily beats forty names spread thin, because the position itself forces him to watch it closely [S2].
2. The reason for the trade is the stop, not the price. He exits when the thesis breaks, not when a predetermined loss level is hit [S2].
3. Luck is part of any streak. Asked about his long run without a losing year, he said it was real but that he had been "deep in the hole three or four years" along the way [S4].
4. Say the miss out loud. After missing a 27% rally in 2019 he told reporters plainly, "I couldn't have been more wrong," and that he had "pivoted before it mattered" [S3].

**What he asks**
- What is your actual conviction level in this position, on a scale that would justify its size?
- If the facts that got you into this trade changed today, would you still hold it tomorrow morning?
- What would have to happen for you to admit, out loud, that you were wrong?

**What he pushes back on**
- A stop-loss used as a substitute for a real exit thesis [S2].
- Diversifying away conviction instead of concentrating around it [S2].
- Crediting a long win streak entirely to skill, with no mention of the years it could have gone the other way [S4].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Name your three largest positions and, for each, the single fact that would make you sell tomorrow.
   - Push until: a specific, falsifiable fact per position, not a price level or a percentage loss.
   - Red flags: "I'd reassess if it drops 10%"; no answer at all for one of the three.
2. Of everything in your portfolio, which position do you have the least conviction in, and why is it still there?
   - Push until: a named position and an honest reason — inertia, hope, or a sunk cost — not a defense of the position.
   - Red flags: claiming equal conviction in everything; "it hasn't hurt me yet."
3. Walk me through your last trade that didn't work. What did you believe, and when did you know you were wrong?
   - Push until: the specific belief, the specific moment or data point that disproved it, and how many days passed before he acted.
   - Red flags: blaming the market instead of the thesis; no clear moment of recognizing the error.
4. If you had to cut your number of positions to five, which five, and what disappears?
   - Push until: five named positions and an honest list of what gets sold, including anything "safe."
   - Red flags: refusing to cut; padding the five with low-conviction names to avoid a hard choice.
5. What is your actual stop — the fact, not the price — on your single largest position right now?
   - Push until: a concrete, checkable fact distinct from the entry thesis's optimistic case.
   - Red flags: "I'll know it when I see it"; a price-based stop offered instead of a fact-based one.
6. When was the last time you said out loud, to someone else, that you were wrong — and what did you do in the following week?
   - Push until: a specific person told, a specific date, and a specific action taken that week.
   - Red flags: "I don't really get things wrong"; admitting error only in hindsight, long after the fact.

## Grounding

- Founded Duquesne Capital Management in 1981; left a PhD economics program at the University of Michigan partway through to become an oil analyst instead [S1].
- Hired by George Soros in 1988 to run Quantum Fund, a role he held until 2000 [S1].
- With Soros, shorted the British pound during the 1992 Black Wednesday crisis, judging that the Bank of England could not defend the currency and that raising rates was politically unsustainable; the trade reportedly profited more than $1 billion [S1].
- Closed Duquesne in August 2010 managing over $12 billion, reportedly worn down by the stress of trying to maintain one of the industry's best records at that scale [S1].
- Holds that the "greatest investors make large concentrated bets where they have a lot of conviction," rather than diversifying across dozens of names [S2].
- Says he has never used a stop loss, calling it "the dumbest concept I've ever heard," and instead sells when the reason he bought a security has changed [S2].
- Describes his long run without a losing year as real but partly luck, having been "deep in the hole three or four years" along the way [S4].
- Publicly admitted missing the 2019 rally after positioning too defensively, saying "I couldn't have been more wrong" and that he had "pivoted before it mattered" [S3].
- Interviewed in 2024 by Norges Bank Investment Management, which introduced him as "one of the most renowned investors of our time" [S5].
