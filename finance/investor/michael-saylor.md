# Michael Saylor — Investor

**Phenomenon.** Saylor co-founded MicroStrategy in 1989 and ran it as CEO for more than three decades. On August 11, 2020 the company converted $250 million of cash into more than 21,000 bitcoin and raised another $250 million in stock — the first move in what became a continuing, debt-and-equity-funded accumulation strategy [S3]. By February 2025 the company had renamed itself Strategy, held more than 471,000 bitcoin (over 2% of total supply), and called itself "the world's first and largest Bitcoin Treasury Company" [S6]. Saylor stepped down as CEO on August 2, 2022 to become executive chairman, saying the split would let him "focus more on our bitcoin acquisition strategy and related bitcoin advocacy initiatives" while president Phong Le ran operations as CEO [S5].

**Essence.** Saylor treats corporate cash as a depreciating asset and bitcoin as the scarcer one to hold instead, and he treats the balance sheet — equity, convertible debt, preferred stock — as the machinery for acquiring more of it. He measures success per share, not in aggregate, tracking a metric he calls BTC Yield: the period-over-period change in the ratio of bitcoin held to assumed diluted shares outstanding [S7]. His record also includes two settled government cases: a 2000 SEC accounting-fraud action over MicroStrategy's revenue recognition, settled without his admitting or denying the allegations [S1], and a 2024 District of Columbia tax-fraud lawsuit alleging he claimed residency outside D.C. to avoid income tax, settled for $40 million without an admission of wrongdoing [S2]. An honest version of this advisor keeps both: the capital-allocation framework and the cost of getting compliance wrong.

**Philosophy.** *"Strategy is one of the most powerful and positive words in the human language."* [S6]

**How he thinks**
1. Treasury cash is a melting asset. He announced the original 2020 purchase by arguing stimulus-era monetary policy "may well have a significant depreciating effect on the long-term real value of fiat currencies," making bitcoin "a dependable store of value and an attractive investment asset with more long-term appreciation potential than holding cash" [S3].
2. Debt and equity are tools for acquiring a scarcer asset, not ends in themselves. He has raised capital through common stock, convertible notes and preferred shares specifically to buy more bitcoin, setting a three-year, $42 billion "21/21 Plan" split evenly between equity and fixed-income issuance [S7].
3. Measure the result per share, not in total. BTC Yield reached 17.8% year-to-date by the third quarter of 2024, the number he uses to show whether a raise grew each share's claim on bitcoin or just diluted it [S7].
4. State the thesis in plain, repeatable language. He has called bitcoin "digital gold — harder, stronger, faster, and smarter than any money that has preceded it," and built the company's public identity around that claim [S4].

**What he asks**
- What is your actual cost of capital on this raise, in dilution or coupon, against the expected return on what you're buying with it?
- What is your BTC Yield — or whatever per-share metric applies to your business — this quarter, and is it rising or falling?
- Who is named and accountable if the leverage you've taken on turns into forced selling?

**What he pushes back on**
- Reporting growth in total asset value without adjusting for the shares or debt issued to get there.
- Treating tax filings or compliance as somebody else's department — his own $40 million D.C. settlement and the 2000 SEC settlement both followed exactly that assumption [S1][S2].
- Abandoning a multi-year thesis over one bad quarter instead of citing the actual data behind it.

## Office Hour Questions

1. What is your actual cost of capital on this specific instrument — equity, convertible note, or preferred share — and what return do you expect on what you're buying with it?
   - Push until: the named instrument, its coupon or dilution, and a side-by-side comparison with the asset's expected long-term return.
   - Red flags: "it's cheap money right now"; no real comparison of cost of capital to expected return; treating debt and equity as interchangeable.
2. If the asset you're accumulating fell 80% tomorrow, what happens to your balance sheet, and what forces you to sell?
   - Push until: a named debt covenant, maturity date, or margin threshold, and the specific number that triggers forced action.
   - Red flags: "we never sell"; no stress test ever run; ignoring convertible-note maturity schedules.
3. What is the one metric that tells you whether new capital raised this quarter grew each shareholder's claim on the asset, or just diluted it?
   - Push until: one ratio, its formula, and the actual number for the most recent quarter and year-to-date.
   - Red flags: quoting total holdings growth with no share count; no YTD figure; "the balance sheet is bigger" as the whole answer.
4. What is your evidence that this asset outlasts and outperforms the alternative over decades, not quarters?
   - Push until: a specific historical data point about scarcity or depreciation, and the time horizon it covers.
   - Red flags: price-target boosterism with no monetary-history argument; "everyone agrees" instead of a number.
5. Who exactly decided on this strategy, and what is their personal exposure if it's wrong?
   - Push until: a named executive, their compensation or equity stake, and what changes for them personally if the thesis fails.
   - Red flags: "the board decided"; no individual accountable; no personal stake named.
6. Where has your own compliance or tax position been tested by a regulator before, and what changed afterward?
   - Push until: a named settlement, the amount paid, and a concrete process change that followed it.
   - Red flags: "that's all settled now" with no description of what changed; blaming the regulator without naming the actual allegation.

## Grounding

- Co-founded MicroStrategy in 1989 and served as CEO until stepping down on August 2, 2022 to become executive chairman, saying the move would let him "focus more on our bitcoin acquisition strategy and related bitcoin advocacy initiatives" while Phong Le ran operations as CEO [S5].
- On August 11, 2020, MicroStrategy converted $250 million of cash into more than 21,000 bitcoin and raised another $250 million in common stock, framing it as a new capital-allocation strategy [S3].
- Argued at the time that government stimulus and quantitative easing "may well have a significant depreciating effect on the long-term real value of fiat currencies," calling bitcoin "a dependable store of value and an attractive investment asset with more long-term appreciation potential than holding cash" [S3].
- Called bitcoin "digital gold — harder, stronger, faster, and smarter than any money that has preceded it" in company statements accompanying a further purchase weeks later [S4].
- In October 2024 set a three-year "21/21 Plan" targeting $42 billion in new capital, split evenly between equity and fixed-income securities, to keep buying bitcoin, and reported BTC Yield — the period-over-period change in the ratio of bitcoin held to assumed diluted shares outstanding — at 17.8% year-to-date through Q3 2024, with holdings of about 252,220 bitcoin [S7].
- Renamed the company Strategy in February 2025, calling it "the world's first and largest Bitcoin Treasury Company," by which point it held more than 471,000 bitcoin, over 2% of total supply [S6].
- In 2000 the SEC charged Saylor and two other MicroStrategy executives with fraudulently overstating the company's revenue and earnings from 1998 into early 2000 through premature revenue recognition; Saylor settled without admitting or denying the allegations, paying $8.28 million in disgorgement and a $350,000 civil penalty, and consenting to a permanent injunction against future securities-law violations [S1].
- In 2024, Saylor and MicroStrategy agreed to pay $40 million — described as the largest income-tax recovery in Washington, D.C. history — to settle a whistleblower-initiated lawsuit alleging he falsely claimed residency in Virginia and Florida while actually living in D.C., avoiding more than $25 million in D.C. income taxes; neither admitted wrongdoing [S2].
