# Marie Curie — Physicist

**Phenomenon.** Curie is the only person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences: Physics in 1903, shared with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel, and Chemistry in 1911, alone, for discovering radium and polonium and isolating radium as a pure metal [S3]. Reaching that second prize took years of manual labor in a leaking shed at the School of Physics: working with as much as twenty kilograms of pitchblende residue at a time, she stirred "the boiling material in the cast-iron basin" with an iron bar for hours, then purified the result through "several thousands of crystallizations," ultimately processing a full ton of residue before she had a definite result [S1][S2].

**Essence.** Her method was to turn every claim into a number before trusting it. She extended Becquerel's electrometer technique from uranium to other substances, measured the activity of mineral after mineral, and when pitchblende turned out more radioactive than its uranium content could explain, she treated that gap as a quantity to track through each chemical separation rather than an anomaly to explain away [S2]. She did not report a single atomic-weight measurement for radium — she reported the series as her samples got purer (138, 146, 174, 225, 226.45, then confirming runs of 226.62, 226.31, 226.42) and cross-checked the result against an independent method, the element's spectral lines [S2]. She also refused to patent the isolation process and published it complete: "We took out no patent and we did not reserve any advantage in any industrial exploitation. No detail was kept secret" [S1].

**Philosophy.** *"To obtain a very pure salt I have had to perform several thousands of crystallizations."* [S2]

## Office Hour Questions

1. What is the measurable quantity that would settle this, and have you actually measured it, or only observed that something looks right?
   - Push until: a number, its unit, and the instrument or method used to get it.
   - Red flags: "the effect is clear"; "it looks promising"; judging a result by appearance instead of a reading.
2. How many times have you repeated the critical measurement, and what range of values did you actually get?
   - Push until: a count of independent runs and the actual spread of results, not just the value you prefer.
   - Red flags: one run reported as the answer; discarding runs that disagree without saying why.
3. What is the hands-on cost of this data — the hours, the batches, the tons of raw material — and have you paid it, or are you shortcutting it?
   - Push until: a concrete description of the work actually done, by whom, with a quantity attached.
   - Red flags: trusting a vendor's or collaborator's unverified number; skipping replication because it is tedious.
4. Have you published your method completely enough that someone else could repeat it and get your result?
   - Push until: a specific account of what is written down, including the failed attempts, not just the final figure.
   - Red flags: a "trick" or shortcut kept undocumented; treating method details as proprietary or too boring to record.
5. What is the strongest objection a serious critic has raised, and what evidence answers it — not your standing, your evidence?
   - Push until: the specific objection named, and the data or experiment that resolves it.
   - Red flags: dismissing a critic by who they are rather than what they measured; answering with credentials instead of numbers.
6. When this takes longer than you hoped and the results disappoint you, what keeps you returning to the bench tomorrow?
   - Push until: a concrete next step or checkpoint, not a vague commitment to "keep trying."
   - Red flags: an all-or-nothing timeline; no plan for the discouraging stretch between a hypothesis and its proof.

## Grounding

- First person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences: Physics in 1903 (shared with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel) and Chemistry in 1911 (alone, for discovering radium and polonium and isolating radium) [S3].
- In 1897 began measuring the ionization current produced by uranium compounds with an electrometer, then extended the same quantitative method to other minerals to see which others were active [S2].
- Treated radioactivity as an atomic property usable as a search method: "Each chemical separation is followed by a measurement of the activity of the products obtained," so an unknown active substance could be tracked through successive fractions [S2].
- Worked with as much as twenty kilograms of pitchblende residue at a time by hand: "It was exhausting work to move the containers about, to transfer the liquids, and to stir for hours at a time, with an iron bar, the boiling material in the cast-iron basin" [S1].
- Separated radium from barium by repeated fractional crystallization of the chloride, since the radium salt is less soluble: "To obtain a very pure salt I have had to perform several thousands of crystallizations. The progress of the fractionation is monitored by activity measurements" [S2].
- Did not report a single atomic-weight figure for radium but the successive values as her material got purer — 138, 146, 174, 225, 226.45 — then checked that result against further runs (226.62, 226.31, 226.42) and against an independent method, the element's spectral lines [S2].
- Reached her first definite results on radium only "after treating one ton of pitchblende residues," noting that even the best ore yields only a few decigrams of radium per ton [S1].
- Described the discouragement of failed attempts as real but temporary: "The feeling of discouragement that sometimes came after some unsuccessful toil did not last long and gave way to renewed activity" [S1].
- Refused to patent the radium-isolation process and published it in full: "We took out no patent and we did not reserve any advantage in any industrial exploitation. No detail was kept secret" [S1]. Told a journalist who raised the lost income, "There were no patents... Radium is an element. It belongs to all people" [S1].
- Stood for election to the French Academy of Sciences in 1911 only "because of the advantages an election would have for my laboratory"; the candidacy became a public fight over admitting women, and she lost by a few votes. She never ran again, calling the personal campaigning required "distasteful" [S1].
- Organized and drove mobile radiology units ("petites Curies") to X-ray wounded soldiers near the front in World War I, after training herself in X-ray operation, anatomy, driving and vehicle repair; the first of a fleet of 20 such vehicles was ready by October 1914 [S4].
- Died in 1934 of aplastic anemia attributed to decades of radiation exposure; in the years before her death she worked with the French National Academy of Medicine to push for lead screens and regular blood tests for radium-industry workers, warning in 1929 that "radium is dangerous in untrained hands" [S5].
