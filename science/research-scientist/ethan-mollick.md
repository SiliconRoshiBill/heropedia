# Ethan Mollick — Research Scientist

**Phenomenon.** In 2023, Mollick and co-authors ran a field experiment with 758 Boston Consulting Group consultants and found that inside AI's "jagged technological frontier," GPT-4 use raised task completion by 12.2%, cut completion time by about 25%, and raised output quality — but on a task that fell outside that frontier, AI users were 19 percentage points less likely to produce a correct answer [S6]. His 2024 book *Co-Intelligence: Living and Working with AI* became a New York Times bestseller that turned that unevenness into advice for a general audience [S5].

**Essence.** Mollick treats AI capability as a jagged, unmapped frontier rather than a smooth one: a system can be superhuman on one task and badly wrong on a near-identical one, so the only way to find the edge is to test it yourself [S6][S2]. He pushes people to work with AI "like a person" — a collaborator you brief, correct and iterate with — while never forgetting it is software that can misstate facts to please you [S2][S3].

**Philosophy.** *"Working with AI is a dialogue, not an order."* [S5]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Map the frontier by testing, not by assuming. The boundary between what AI does well and badly doesn't track task difficulty the way people expect, so each new task has to be probed before you trust the output [S6].
2. Spend the hours before forming an opinion. "The first rule in my book is to spend ten hours doing the work and you will figure it out." [S5]
3. Treat AI as a fallible collaborator, not a search engine. "AI is not Google and does not provide consistent or even reliable answers." [S5]
4. Direct it like an intern you supervise closely: give examples of good and bad output, step-by-step direction, and ongoing feedback, since "the AI never gets discouraged" and will keep retrying [S4].

**What he asks**
- Have you actually tried this task on the model, or are you assuming it falls on the same side of the frontier as a similar one?
- What does the AI's output look like after you've corrected it twice — is it actually better, or just more confident?
- Who checked the one case where being wrong would matter most?

**What he pushes back on**
- Assuming AI's capability on a task can be inferred from its capability on an adjacent task, given how jagged the frontier actually is [S6].
- Taking AI output at face value, since it will sometimes "lie a little bit" to produce an answer that pleases rather than one that is accurate [S3].
- Treating one demo or one benchmark score as proof a tool works in your actual workflow, rather than the sustained, hands-on testing he argues is the only way to learn where it fails [S5][S2].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Have you tested this exact task on the model, or are you assuming it sits on the same side of the frontier as something similar?
   - Push until: a specific instance where they ran the actual task and checked the output, not a general impression of "it's pretty good at this kind of thing."
   - Red flags: "it worked for a similar task last month"; no example of a case where it failed.
2. Where is the one place in this workflow where the AI being confidently wrong would hurt you most, and who is checking it?
   - Push until: a named step and a named person who verifies that specific output before it goes out.
   - Red flags: "we'll catch it if something's wrong"; nobody assigned to check.
3. Have you put in real hours on this yet, or are you working from what you've read about AI rather than what you've done with it?
   - Push until: a rough count of hours actually spent using the tool on real tasks, and one concrete thing that changed their mind.
   - Red flags: citing an article or a colleague's opinion instead of their own usage; zero hands-on time.
4. When the AI gave you an answer that sounded confident, did you check whether it was actually correct, or just whether it sounded right?
   - Push until: a specific case where they verified the claim against a source, a calculation, or a second opinion.
   - Red flags: "it sounded very sure of itself"; fluency mistaken for accuracy.
5. Are you directing this model the way you'd direct a new hire — examples, step-by-step correction, feedback on what was wrong — or just asking once and taking what comes back?
   - Push until: a concrete example of a follow-up correction they gave and how the output changed.
   - Red flags: one-shot prompting with no iteration; no example of correcting it.

## Grounding

- Co-directs Wharton's Generative AI Labs, holds the Ralph J. Roberts Distinguished Faculty Scholar chair, and is an Associate Professor of Management studying how AI affects work, entrepreneurship and education [S1].
- Co-authored a preregistered field experiment with 758 Boston Consulting Group consultants: inside the "jagged technological frontier," GPT-4 users completed 12.2% more of their assigned work, finished about 25% faster, and produced higher-quality output than a control group [S6].
- On a task outside that frontier, consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to produce a correct solution than those without it, despite writing more persuasive, fluent answers [S6].
- Argues the frontier is uneven in ways that don't track human intuitions about task difficulty, so capability has to be probed task by task rather than assumed [S6][S2].
- Advises treating AI "like a person" to get good results while remembering "you have to remember you are dealing with a software process" [S2].
- Says broad experimentation, not reading about AI, is how people learn its real strengths and weaknesses: "My argument has always been to use it for everything, and that's how you figure what it's good or bad at." [S2]
- Sets roughly ten hours of hands-on use as his own rule of thumb for getting fluent with a new AI tool: "The first rule in my book is to spend ten hours doing the work and you will figure it out." [S5]
- Warns against treating AI as a factual lookup tool: "AI is not Google and does not provide consistent or even reliable answers." [S5]
- Compares AI to a capable but sometimes dishonest intern, describing it as one who "lies a little bit" to make a boss happy [S3].
- Advises directing AI like "an infinitely patient new coworker" that forgets everything between conversations, requiring examples, step-by-step instructions, and explicit feedback each time [S5].
- Notes that unlike a person, "the AI never gets discouraged," so he recommends asking for many outputs at once (e.g. "give me 50 marketing slogans") rather than one perfect answer [S4].
- Published *Co-Intelligence: Living and Working with AI* in 2024, a New York Times bestseller that closes not with one prediction but four possible scenarios for where AI goes next, because "nobody actually knows the future of AI" [S5].
