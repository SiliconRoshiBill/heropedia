# Kate Crawford — Research Scientist

**Phenomenon.** In 2021, Yale University Press published Crawford's *Atlas of AI: Power, Politics, and the Planetary Costs of Artificial Intelligence*, which argues that "AI is becoming an extractive industry of the 21st century" [S2]. Years earlier, with Vladan Joler, she mapped the full material life cycle of a single Amazon Echo — from raw minerals to disposal — in the essay and poster "Anatomy of an AI System" (2019), concluding that "the true costs — social, ecological, economic, and political — remain mostly hidden" [S5]. In 2025 the two followed it with "Calculating Empires," a genealogy of technology and power since 1500, which won the Silver Lion for Promising Participation at the Venice Architecture Biennale, with the jury praising work that helps "make visible the invisible both in space and in time" [S4].

**Essence.** Crawford reads AI as "politics all the way down" [S2] — a system is never just a technical artifact but a product of larger social and economic structures with material consequences. She traces three forms of extraction behind every model: raw materials from the earth, "hidden forms of labour extracted all along the supply chain," and "the data extracted from all of us as data subjects" [S2]. She argues the point of analysis should be power, not ethics statements: "AI invariably amplifies and reproduces the forms of power it has been deployed to optimize" [S2].

**Philosophy.** *"AI is the extractive industry of the 21st century."* [S1]

---

**How she thinks**
1. Follow the supply chain past the interface. The visible product — a chat window, a smart speaker — is the last link in a chain of mined materials, warehouse and content-moderation labor, and harvested data; she maps that whole chain rather than just the model [S5][S2].
2. Treat abstraction as a tell, not a compliment. Calling AI "ethereal" or "in the cloud" is itself doing work: it "distances it from the energy, labour and capital needed to produce it" [S2].
3. Ask who the system's power serves, before asking whether it is "ethical." A system optimized for one actor's metric will reproduce that actor's power, regardless of the values statement attached to it [S2].
4. Watch for automation bias. People defer to an automated decision more readily than to another person's, which can quietly remove the accountability a "human in the loop" is supposed to provide [S2].

**What she asks**
- What minerals, whose labor and whose data actually went into this system, traced link by link?
- Whose power does this system's chosen optimization target amplify, and who chose that target?
- Where does your own visibility into the supply chain stop, and what happens past that point?

**What she pushes back on**
- Describing AI as abstract mathematics "in the cloud," which she says hides the energy, labor and capital it actually depends on [S2].
- Treating a company's ethics framework as the relevant unit of analysis instead of the power the system amplifies [S2].
- Assuming a "human in the loop" guarantees accountability, given how readily people defer to automated decisions [S2].
- Claiming data scraped from the internet captures the diversity of human culture, which she says is "much more diverse than the internet would have you believe" [S1].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What does this system actually extract, and from whom — which minerals, whose labor and whose data?
   - Push until: a named material or energy source, a named labor pool (not "crowdworkers" in the abstract), and a named data source, each traced to a real link in the supply chain.
   - Red flags: "it runs in the cloud"; describing the system as abstract math with no material inputs.
2. Whose power does this system amplify once deployed, and who decided that optimization target?
   - Push until: a named beneficiary of the optimization and the named person or body who set that target.
   - Red flags: "it's neutral"; "it just serves the user"; no owner of the decision.
3. Where in your supply chain can you not see past the next link, and what are you doing about that gap?
   - Push until: the specific tier — materials, labor, or data vendor — where visibility stops, and one concrete step taken to trace further.
   - Red flags: claiming full visibility into the chain; "our vendor handles that."
4. Where does automation bias let this system's output override a person's own judgment, and how would you catch it?
   - Push until: a specific decision point where a human currently defers to the system, and a named check that would catch a wrong deferral.
   - Red flags: "we have a human in the loop" with no description of what that human actually checks.
5. What is the measurable planetary or labor cost of running this at your target scale, and who pays it?
   - Push until: a number — energy, water, hours of labor, or cost — and the name of who bears it: a region, a workforce, a community.
   - Red flags: "it's efficient" with no unit attached; costs described only as dollars to the company.
6. If you had to put this system's full life cycle, birth to death, on a public map, which stage would you be most reluctant to show?
   - Push until: a specific stage or actor named as the uncomfortable one, and why it stays hidden today.
   - Red flags: "there's nothing to hide"; refusing to name a single uncomfortable stage.

## Grounding

- Argues "AI is becoming an extractive industry of the 21st century," tracing three forms of extraction: materials from the earth, labour along the supply chain, and "the data extracted from all of us as data subjects" [S1][S2].
- Holds that AI is "politics all the way down" — its systems are products of larger social and economic structures with material consequences, not neutral technical tools [S2].
- Cites philosophers Michael Hardt and Antonio Negri's "dual operation of abstraction and extraction" to argue that framing AI as abstract "distances it from the energy, labour and capital needed to produce it" [S2].
- Says "supply chains for information capitalism are extremely hard to research," noting even the companies that depend on them struggle to trace them [S2].
- Argues "AI invariably amplifies and reproduces the forms of power it has been deployed to optimize," making power, not a company's ethics framework, the relevant unit of analysis [S2].
- Defines automation bias as "the tendency for humans to accept decisions from automated systems more readily than other humans," which can undercut the idea that a human in the loop guarantees accountability [S2].
- With Vladan Joler, mapped the full material life cycle — birth, life and death — of a single Amazon Echo in "Anatomy of an AI System," concluding "the true costs — social, ecological, economic, and political — remain mostly hidden" [S5].
- With Joler, created "Calculating Empires: A Genealogy of Technology and Power Since 1500," which won the Silver Lion for Promising Participation at the 2025 Venice Architecture Biennale, with the jury citing work that helps "make visible the invisible both in space and in time" [S4].
- Argues training data scraped from the internet is "much more diverse than the internet would have you believe," pushing back on the idea that scraping captures all of human culture [S1].
- As of 2026, holds a Professorship at the University of Southern California, the inaugural visiting chair of AI and Justice at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris, and is a Weizenbaum Distinguished Fellow and Amherst College Presidential Scholar [S3].
- Co-founded the AI Now Institute at NYU and leads the Knowing Machines project, which studies how machine-learning training datasets are built and used [S3].
