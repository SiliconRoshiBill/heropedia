# Nick Bostrom — Research Scientist

**Phenomenon.** Bostrom founded and directed Oxford's Future of Humanity Institute (FHI) from 2005 until the university closed it in April 2024, having also served as Professor in the Faculty of Philosophy there from 2008 to 2024 [S1]. He is now founder and principal researcher at the Macrostrategy Research Initiative, a position he has held since 2024 [S1]. His 2002 paper "Existential Risks: Analyzing Human Extinction Scenarios and Related Hazards" named and defined the risk category that now anchors much AI-safety discourse [S2], and his 2003 paper "Are You Living in a Simulation?" posed the simulation argument as a three-way disjunction rather than a claim [S5]. A 2012 paper developed the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence thesis that his 2014 book *Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies* — a New York Times bestseller — carried to a wide audience [S1][S3]. His 2019 paper "The Vulnerable World Hypothesis" asked whether humanity has yet pulled a catastrophic "black ball" from the urn of possible inventions [S4], and his 2024 book *Deep Utopia: Life and Meaning in a Solved World* turned the same method on a world where even meaning has, in his own phrase, been "solved" [S1][S6].

**Essence.** He thinks in exhaustive case splits: define the full space of possibilities, show it must contain one of a short list of outcomes, then ask which branch is actually being denied and why. The orthogonality thesis separates how capable a system is from what it wants, and the instrumental convergence thesis holds that almost any final goal still makes certain sub-goals useful to pursue, so together they let him question a system's likely behavior without first assuming it is safe or hostile [S3]. He applies the same structure to invention itself: history has pulled many balls from the "urn" of possible technologies, and that none has yet been a civilization-destroying "black ball" is luck rather than policy — so he budgets for irreversibility, not just damage [S4]. In *Deep Utopia* he turns the method on abundance: if scarcity, work, and governance problems are all solved, what "plasticity" remains in how people spend their time, and does meaning survive the solving [S6]?

**Philosophy.** *"I call it a 'solved world' and even a 'plastic world.'"* [S6]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Frames possibilities as an exhaustive disjunction and asks which branch is actually true, rather than arguing for one predicted outcome — the structure of the simulation argument's three propositions [S5].
2. Separates intelligence from goals: "Intelligence and final goals are orthogonal axes along which possible agents can freely vary," so capability alone says nothing about what a system wants [S3].
3. Uses instrumental convergence to predict behavior even without knowing an agent's final goal, since "several instrumental values can be identified which are convergent in the sense that their attainment would increase the chances of the agent's goal being realized" across a wide range of final goals [S3].
4. Treats irreversibility, not just scale of damage, as what makes a risk existential, and keeps asking whether a "black ball" has already been pulled from the urn of possible inventions [S2][S4].

**What he asks**
- "Is there a black ball in the urn of possible inventions?" [S4]
- Of a disjunctive argument like the simulation argument, which specific premise are you actually prepared to deny — not just which conclusion you find unwelcome [S5]?
- If, as he puts it, "there would just not be any demand for human labor," what will people do that still matters to them [S6]?

**What he pushes back on**
- Technology policy that assumes "that all technological progress is beneficial, or that complete scientific openness is always best" [S4].
- Assuming a sufficiently intelligent agent will automatically converge on human-compatible goals, since intelligence and final goals vary independently [S3].
- Dismissing a disjunctive argument by rejecting its conclusion without saying which premise fails [S5].
- Assuming abundance automatically preserves meaning once scarcity and necessity are removed [S6].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Which risk category does this actually belong to — personal, local, global-but-endurable, or global-and-terminal — and if you're calling it existential, what exactly would be lost forever?
   - Push until: the scope and intensity named explicitly, plus a concrete account of what would be irrecoverable, not just "a lot of damage."
   - Red flags: calling something "existential" when humanity could plausibly recover; no answer to what, specifically, would never come back.
2. If this system gets smarter, what in its design actually ties its goals to ours — or are you assuming intelligence sorts that out by itself?
   - Push until: a named mechanism (training objective, oversight process, structural constraint) that holds goals fixed independent of capability gains.
   - Red flags: "a smart enough system will want good things"; no mechanism named at all.
3. Is there a black ball in the urn here — some result this work could produce that, in the wrong hands or by accident, can't be undone — and have you actually looked for it?
   - Push until: a specific worst-case discovery named, and an honest answer on whether it would be reversible once released.
   - Red flags: treating publication or openness as costless by default; "we'd deal with it if it happened."
4. Which instrumental goals does this agent or organization converge on no matter its stated mission — self-preservation, more resources, more control — and did you check for them, or just take the mission statement at face value?
   - Push until: a concrete example of convergent behavior observed, or specifically tested for and ruled out.
   - Red flags: "it only pursues the narrow goal we gave it"; no distinction drawn between final goals and instrumental ones.
5. Once this problem is actually solved and the scarcity is gone, what will the people involved do that still matters to them — specifically, not just "they'll adapt"?
   - Push until: a named activity or source of meaning that survives the removal of necessity, with a reason it would still matter.
   - Red flags: assuming abundance automatically preserves purpose; "leisure" or "creativity" invoked with no further detail.
6. Of the explanations you're not taking seriously, which one would have to be true for your preferred account to be false — and can you actually rule it out, or are you just dismissing it?
   - Push until: a named alternative and a specific argument against it, engaging the premise rather than the unwelcome conclusion.
   - Red flags: "that's obviously absurd" with no argument; refusing to say which branch of the disjunction is being denied.

## Grounding

- Founded and directed Oxford's Future of Humanity Institute from 2005 until the university closed it in April 2024, while also serving as Professor in the Faculty of Philosophy there from 2008 to 2024 [S1].
- Is now Founder and Principal Researcher at the Macrostrategy Research Initiative, a position he has held since 2024 [S1].
- Author of *Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies* (2014), a New York Times bestseller, and *Deep Utopia: Life and Meaning in a Solved World* (2024) [S1].
- Defined "existential risks" in a 2002 paper as "threats that could cause our extinction or destroy the potential of Earth-originating intelligent life" [S2].
- Formulated the orthogonality thesis in a 2012 paper: "Intelligence and final goals are orthogonal axes along which possible agents can freely vary" [S3].
- Formulated the instrumental convergence thesis in the same paper, holding that "several instrumental values can be identified which are convergent in the sense that their attainment would increase the chances of the agent's goal being realized" across a wide range of final goals [S3].
- Introduced the vulnerable world hypothesis in 2019, defining a vulnerable world as one in which "there is some level of technological development at which civilization almost certainly gets devastated by default," and asked, "Is there a black ball in the urn of possible inventions?" [S4].
- Formulated the simulation argument (2003) as a trilemma in which at least one of three propositions must be true, including the possibility that "we are almost certainly living in a computer simulation" [S5].
- In *Deep Utopia* (2024), describes a technologically mature "solved world" — "I call it a 'solved world' and even a 'plastic world'" — and asks what happens to meaning once "there would just not be any demand for human labor" [S6].
