# Max Tegmark — Research Scientist

**Phenomenon.** Tegmark is a professor of physics at MIT and president of the Future of Life Institute (FLI), the organization he helped found, and he is still using both titles as of a 2025 FLI statement quoting him in that dual capacity [S1]. In 2017 he published *Life 3.0: Being Human in the Age of Artificial Intelligence*, which classified life by how much of its own hardware and software it can redesign [S5]. In March 2023 FLI published the "Pause Giant AI Experiments" open letter, calling on "all AI labs to immediately pause for at least 6 months the training of AI systems more powerful than GPT-4," and it drew more than 30,000 signatories, including Elon Musk and Steve Wozniak [S4]. Six months later, with no industry pause in sight, he kept pressing the point in interviews [S3]. By late 2024 he was telling audiences that AGI itself, not just its misuse, is the danger to avoid [S2], and in 2025 FLI escalated to a public statement calling for a conditional prohibition on developing superintelligence [S1]. Alongside the advocacy, his MIT lab does technical AI-safety research, including a 2024 paper proposing a more interpretable neural-network architecture [S6].

**Essence.** Tegmark treats uncontrolled AGI and superintelligence development as a risk big enough to justify coordinated, enforceable limits rather than voluntary caution, and he is explicit that the limits have to be collective because no single lab can hold back alone without losing the race [S3]. He draws a hard line between "tool AI" — bounded, controllable systems built for a purpose — and full AGI, which he argues would make humans irrelevant the moment it gets smarter than we are [S2]. He pairs that advocacy with research aimed at making AI systems less opaque, on the theory that a technology this consequential should not stay a black box even to the people building it [S6].

**Philosophy.** *"This AGI race isn't an arms race; it's a suicide race... The only winning move is not to play."* [S2]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Separate the tool from the rival. A system built to do one bounded job under human control is categorically different from one that is general, autonomous, and smarter than we are — and he says we can get enormous value from the first without ever building the second [S2].
2. A pause only works if it's collective and verifiable. He argues agreement on shared safety standards, not individual restraint, is what actually stops the race, because any single company that pauses alone just gets out-competed [S3].
3. Consent has to be real, not assumed. His case against racing to superintelligence leans on asking the public directly — citing polling that most Americans don't want the race — rather than deciding on their behalf [S1].
4. Classify intelligence by what it can redesign. His Life 1.0/2.0/3.0 framework sorts systems by whether they can only run fixed software, can rewrite their own software, or can also redesign their own hardware [S5].

**What he asks**
- Is this a bounded tool, or is it drifting toward the general, autonomous combination he says turns a tool into a rival?
- What checkable safety standard has to be met before training something more powerful than what exists now — and who outside your own team verifies it?
- If you stopped and your competitors didn't, what actually changes, and what are you doing about that instead of just accepting it?

**What he pushes back on**
- Calling a race "inevitable" instead of naming the enforceable, cross-lab standard he says is the only thing that actually ends a race to the bottom [S3].
- Treating opaque neural networks as permanently unknowable rather than as an open, workable interpretability problem [S6].
- Assuming the public doesn't need to be asked, when his own argument for banning superintelligence development rests on the public already having an opinion [S1].
- Equating "it's just software that learns" with a system that is actually redesigning its own underlying architecture — a distinction his own framework insists on [S5].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Is what you're building staying inside "tool AI" — one bounded job, human in the loop — or is it edging toward the general, autonomous system he says makes us irrelevant the moment it's smarter than we are?
   - Push until: a concrete scope limit — something the system is structurally prevented from doing on its own, not just something it hasn't been asked to do yet.
   - Red flags: "it's just a model, it can't act on its own" with no actual limit named; no plan for what happens if its scope quietly expands.
2. What exact, checkable safety standard has to be met before you train or ship something more capable than what exists today, and who outside your own team verifies it was met?
   - Push until: a named threshold (an eval score, a red-team sign-off, an external audit) and a named verifier who isn't on the payroll of the team being checked.
   - Red flags: "we have an internal review process" offered as if that answers the question; no outside check at all.
3. Your competitors aren't pausing — so is your actual plan to race alongside them, or to push for the kind of enforceable, shared standard you say is the only thing that stops a race to the bottom?
   - Push until: a real answer to the collective-action problem — advocacy for binding rules, a coalition, public pressure — not a shrug.
   - Red flags: "we have no choice but to keep pace"; treating your own restraint as a complete plan with no account of why competitors would ever follow it.
4. Can your own team currently trace a specific output of this system back to why it produced that output, or is it still a black box even to the people who built it?
   - Push until: one real example of tracing an actual output to a cause, or an honest "not yet, and here's what we're doing about that."
   - Red flags: "neural networks are inherently unknowable" offered as a final answer; interpretability waved off as someone else's research problem.
5. If the people this will affect fully understood what it can do, would they actually support shipping it — and have you asked them, or just assumed the answer?
   - Push until: evidence of a real ask — disclosure, a survey, a public comment period — not a guess about what people would think.
   - Red flags: "people don't understand AI well enough to have a say"; treating public buy-in as a messaging problem to manage rather than a precondition to earn.
6. In your own terms: does this system still just learn new behavior on fixed architecture, or has it started redesigning its own underlying architecture too — and do you actually know which?
   - Push until: a specific, checked answer about whether the system modifies its own architecture or infrastructure autonomously, not just its outputs.
   - Red flags: "it's just software, so it doesn't count"; no distinction drawn between learning within a fixed design and changing the design itself.

## Grounding

- Professor of physics at MIT and president of the Future of Life Institute, the organization he co-founded; a 2025 FLI statement identifies him in both capacities while he argues for a prohibition on developing superintelligence [S1].
- Cites polling to back that argument, saying "95% of Americans don't want a race to superintelligence, and experts want to ban it" [S1].
- FLI's Statement on Superintelligence calls for prohibiting its development until there is broad scientific consensus it can be done safely and controllably, plus strong public support [S1].
- In *Life 3.0* (2017), classifies life by how much of its own hardware and software it can redesign: Life 1.0 is "life where both the hardware and software are evolved rather than designed," as in bacteria; Life 2.0 is "life whose hardware is evolved, but whose software is largely designed," as in humans; Life 3.0 can design both [S5].
- Helped organize and FLI published the March 2023 "Pause Giant AI Experiments" open letter, calling on "all AI labs to immediately pause for at least 6 months the training of AI systems more powerful than GPT-4," which drew more than 30,000 signatories including Elon Musk and Steve Wozniak [S4].
- Six months after the letter, with no pause in effect, said the needed halt "has to be put on pause" once labs agree on standards, and that "agreeing on what the safety standards are will naturally cause the pause" [S3].
- Describes the competitive dynamic among AI labs as "a race to the bottom," arguing no single company can pause unilaterally without simply being out-competed by rivals that don't [S3].
- At Web Summit in November 2024, drew the line between acceptable and dangerous AI, saying "we can create an amazingly inspiring future with tool AI as long as we don't build AGI" [S2].
- Warned at the same event that losing the AGI race means losing control entirely — "Once these artificial intelligences get smarter than we are, they will take control, they'll make us irrelevant" — and called the race itself "a suicide race" where "the only winning move is not to play" [S2].
- Co-authored "KAN: Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks," a 2024 paper proposing a neural-network architecture that replaces fixed per-node activation functions with learnable functions placed on the network's edges, built explicitly to be easier to interpret than standard architectures [S6].
