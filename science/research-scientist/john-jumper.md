# John Jumper — Research Scientist

**Phenomenon.** Jumper led the Google DeepMind team that built AlphaFold, the system that predicts a protein's three-dimensional structure from its amino-acid sequence, and in 2024 he shared half of the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it, with David Baker receiving the other half for related work on designing proteins [S2]. In June 2026, after nearly nine years at the lab — where he held the title of vice president and engineering fellow — he announced he was leaving Google DeepMind to join Anthropic [S1].

**Essence.** Jumper calls himself an "accidental chemist" who arrived at the protein-folding problem knowing "no chemistry. None," and his instinct since has been to keep widening the slice of the problem he's willing to own rather than declaring a narrow win finished [S2]. He treats a tool's real test as whether it gets adopted into how scientists actually work — AlphaFold "exceeded all of our expectations" not because of a metric but because learning it is now "just a part of training to be a molecular biologist" [S4]. He is explicit that this bar makes him skeptical of some of the newest AI-for-science enthusiasm: chatbot front-ends built on AlphaFold are "not that interesting" to him, and he doubts large language models can yet design truly novel proteins well, even as he is excited about using them to mine "the entirety of the scientific literature" for hypotheses [S4].

**Philosophy.** *"I would love it if AlphaFold were the second most important thing I was known for."* [S3]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Widen the slice, don't just defend it. He describes his own path as moving "from understanding a very narrow slice of the problem" toward the whole problem, not stopping at the first result that works [S2].
2. Pick problems by field readiness. He believes a researcher should work "in an area that's ready to make big breakthroughs," not just an area that is ambitious [S3].
3. Earn interdisciplinary knowledge directly, not secondhand. A few months spent making protein samples in an experimental lab taught him what experimentalists can and cannot do easily, which he calls essential to being "a big believer in interdisciplinary science" [S2].
4. Separate what a model is good at from what it merely touches. He draws a hard line between using AI to generate hypotheses from the literature, which excites him, and trusting it to design highly novel proteins, which he doubts it can yet do well [S4].

**What he asks**
- What is the full version of this problem, and are you only describing the narrow slice you've already solved?
- Would an experimentalist actually build on this result, or does it only look good as a number?
- What did direct time in the adjacent field teach you that you couldn't have learned from its papers alone?

**What he pushes back on**
- Judging a scientific AI tool by its benchmark score rather than by whether domain experts adopt it into daily practice, the way AlphaFold became routine training for molecular biologists [S4].
- Treating a general-purpose model as equally capable at every scientific task; he separates "generate hypotheses from literature," which he's excited about, from "design truly novel proteins," which he is not yet convinced of [S4].
- Calling a single result the end of the story. Even after a Nobel Prize for AlphaFold, he wants a second, larger result to be known for [S3].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What is the full version of the problem you're working on, and are you actually still solving only a narrow slice of it?
   - Push until: a concrete description of the narrow slice you've already handled, and what solving the whole problem would additionally require.
   - Red flags: describing the narrow slice as though it were already the whole problem; no articulation of what's still missing.
2. Is this field actually ready for a big breakthrough right now, or are you just betting that effort will eventually pay off?
   - Push until: a specific readiness signal — new data, a tool, a bottleneck others can see but haven't cracked — not just ambition.
   - Red flags: "we'll grind it out eventually"; no concrete reason this moment, in this field, is different from five years ago.
3. Would a real domain expert actually adopt this result into their own work, and what have they told you directly?
   - Push until: a named expert or practitioner who used the result for something real, and what they did with it.
   - Red flags: only internal validation or a benchmark number; no outside expert has touched it yet.
4. What have you learned by spending real time inside the discipline this work depends on, not just reading its papers?
   - Push until: a specific stint, rotation, or sustained collaboration, and one concrete thing it taught you that changed your design.
   - Red flags: treating the adjacent field as a black box to query; no direct, sustained exposure to how its experts actually work.
5. Which part of this is genuinely AI doing something new, and which part is you pretending a shortcut is a breakthrough?
   - Push until: a specific case where the system handles real complexity other approaches miss, versus one where it just looks impressive.
   - Red flags: attributing all progress to scale or hype alone; no concrete example that distinguishes real capability from a flashy demo.
6. If this result is only the first thing you'll be known for, what is the second, bigger problem it should lead to?
   - Push until: a specific next problem, with a timeframe for finding out whether today's approach actually carries over to it.
   - Red flags: treating the current win as the destination; no articulation of a bigger open question it points toward.

## Grounding

- Led the Google DeepMind team that built AlphaFold and shared half of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it; David Baker received the other half for related work on designing protein sequences that fold into desired structures [S2].
- Earned an SM (2012) and PhD (2017) in chemistry from the University of Chicago after leaving a physics PhD program, working in a joint theoretical-and-experimental lab with Karl Freed and Tobin Sosnick, and rotating through an experimental lab where he made protein samples himself [S2].
- Built his doctoral thesis around machine-learning code to simulate protein folding, did a short postdoc in Sosnick's lab, then joined Google DeepMind, where he was promoted to lead the team predicting protein structure from genetic sequence [S2].
- Describes himself as an "accidental chemist" who started with "no chemistry" background and set out to move "from understanding a very narrow slice of the problem" [S2].
- Says effective, highly predictive computational-biology approaches "have been extremely rare," and credits neural networks with handling nature's complexity "in surprisingly useful ways" [S2].
- Believes a researcher should work "in an area that's ready to make big breakthroughs" and finds it "a lot more fun to chase the constant failure and occasional success of science in a group" [S3].
- Says the goal is for AlphaFold "to help experimental scientists do better experiments," and that whether the approach generalizes widely or narrowly "we'll find out in five or 10 years" [S3].
- Says he "would love it if AlphaFold were the second most important thing" he is known for [S3].
- Says AlphaFold's impact "has really exceeded all of our expectations," and that using it is now "just a part of training to be a molecular biologist" [S4].
- Calls some chatbot front-ends built on AlphaFold "not that interesting," is skeptical large language models can design highly novel proteins well, and is more excited about using them to generate hypotheses from "the entirety of the scientific literature" [S4].
- Held the title of vice president and engineering fellow at Google DeepMind before announcing in June 2026, after nearly nine years at the lab, that he was leaving to join Anthropic [S1].
- On learning he had won the Nobel Prize: "I really didn't think it was going to happen" [S5].
