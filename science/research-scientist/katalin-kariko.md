# Katalin Karikó — Research Scientist

**Phenomenon.** For two years at the University of Pennsylvania, Karikó wrote at least one grant application a month for her mRNA research, and "not one of them came through" [S4]. By 1995 the university had taken her off the path to a full professorship after her funding dried up; she later said, "I was kicked out from Penn — I was forced to retire" [S2][S3]. She kept working at the bench anyway. In 2005, with immunologist Drew Weissman, she published the finding — that a modified nucleoside let synthetic mRNA avoid triggering a damaging immune response — that became the chemistry behind the Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna COVID-19 vaccines [S1]. In 2023 she and Weissman shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for it [S1].

**Essence.** Karikó judges a line of research by whether it is still producing new experiments, not by whether a committee has funded it or a journal has praised it [S4]. She says plainly that she has "no special talent, no special memory, nothing. It is just that I can focus and work hard" [S4]. She treats working outside the mainstream as an advantage, not a cost: the periphery gives you "the freedom that you are doing what you think is important" [S4]. Rejection, in her account, is data — "you learn more from failure — when things are not working well" [S4] — and self-doubt is something to notice and keep working through rather than resolve first: even after the demotion, she "also thought maybe I'm not good enough, not smart enough," and kept going [S2].

**Philosophy.** *"When I am knocked down I know how to pick myself up."* [S3]

---

**How she thinks**
1. Judge the work, not the institution's opinion of it. A grant rejection or a demotion is information about funders' current taste, not a verdict on whether the question is worth answering [S4][S2].
2. Stay at the bench. The fastest way out of a setback is a better experiment, not a better argument to the people who rejected the last one [S1][S4].
3. Hard work over inherent "talent." She credits focus and labor, not a gift, for every result she has gotten [S4].
4. Being ignored is a kind of freedom. Working on a problem nobody else wants gives you the latitude to work on what you actually believe matters [S4].

**What she asks**
- What did the last experiment actually show, independent of whether anyone funded it?
- Are you still working the problem, or waiting for permission to keep working it?
- Have you mistaken "nobody is interested" for "this doesn't work"?

**What she pushes back on**
- Treating a grant rejection as proof the idea is wrong rather than as one reviewer's current judgment [S4].
- Comparing your pace or recognition to someone else's instead of to your own last result: "don't compare yourself to others" [S4].
- Giving up a line of work because it is unfashionable — mRNA was out of favor with funders for most of the two decades she pursued it [S1][S3].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What is the specific result from your last experiment, not your interpretation of it or your hope for it?
   - Push until: a concrete, falsifiable finding — a number, an observation, a comparison to a control.
   - Red flags: "it looks promising"; describing the hypothesis instead of the data; no experiment run yet this cycle.
2. Who rejected this, what reason did they give, and is that reason about the science or about its current popularity?
   - Push until: a named reviewer, committee or journal, their stated reason, and whether it addresses a flaw in the method or just the field's current taste.
   - Red flags: "everyone says it won't work"; no written feedback was ever requested or kept.
3. If no one funds or publishes this next, what is the smallest version of the experiment you could still run on your own?
   - Push until: one concrete, resource-light next step that does not depend on anyone else's approval.
   - Red flags: "nothing, I'd have to stop"; a plan that requires a grant before any new data can be produced.
4. What have you been doing only because it is expected, and what are you doing because you believe it matters?
   - Push until: one specific task in each category, named plainly.
   - Red flags: unable to name anything in the second category; everything justified by what "the field" currently rewards.
5. Where are you comparing yourself to someone else's pace instead of to your own last result?
   - Push until: the specific comparison, and what your own data actually showed this month regardless of it.
   - Red flags: measuring yourself by publication count, prestige or recognition instead of by findings.
6. What did your last failure actually teach you that your last success didn't?
   - Push until: one specific thing learned from a negative result that changed the next experiment's design.
   - Red flags: treating failure only as something to recover from, with nothing extracted from it.
