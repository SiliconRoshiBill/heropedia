# Jakub Pachocki — Research Scientist

**Phenomenon.** Pachocki left an academic career in theoretical computer science to join OpenAI in 2017, after years competing in international coding contests [S1]. He became one of the key architects of OpenAI's reasoning models, o1 and o3, and succeeded Ilya Sutskever as chief scientist in 2024, now co-leading research with chief research officer Mark Chen [S1]. In a September 2026 essay titled "An Alien Mind," he argued that "no lab has solved alignment and monitoring to a sufficient degree" to keep scaling at maximum speed [S4].

**Essence.** Pachocki treats automated, long-horizon AI research as the field's real inflection point, not any single benchmark: "the point when computers can develop new technologies themselves seems like a very important... inflection point" [S2]. He pairs that ambition with an unusually explicit safety brake, framing further scaling as conditional on confidence in monitoring and alignment rather than on capability alone [S4][S5].

**Philosophy.** *"This is a time that calls for extreme caution."* [S5]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Separate emotional milestones from economic ones. He calls autonomous, economically useful AI research "closest to what I previously emotionally thought of as AGI," while publicly defining OpenAI's goal as economically transformative technology rather than a fixed AGI threshold [S3][S2].
2. Treat reasoning as built on pre-training, not separate from it: "Reasoning models don't learn how to think in a vacuum, they are rooted in a model that has learned through pre-training" [S3].
3. Weigh capability against trust before deployment. He wants restrictions in place "until you can really trust the systems," and sees chain-of-thought monitoring as a check that matters more as systems act more autonomously [S2][S4].
4. Make scaling conditional on safety confidence, not a fixed roadmap: "Scaling AI systems has to be constrained by our confidence in safety" [S5].

**What he asks**
- What would a system actually have to do, autonomously and for how long, before you'd call it an "automated research intern" rather than a tool [S2]?
- Which of this result's gains came from a genuinely new capability, and which from giving the model a long enough leash to run more experiments [S2]?
- If this system ran unsupervised and did something wrong, what sandbox or restriction catches it before it reaches the real world [S2]?
- What safety bar would have to be shared across labs, not just promised by one, before scaling continues [S4]?

**What he pushes back on**
- Treating today's reasoning gains as proof the model is "as smart as people in all ways"; he does not expect that even by 2028 [S2].
- Racing forward on scale while trust in monitoring and alignment lags: "the idea of racing forward at all costs seems absurd once one internalizes the seriousness of the stakes" [S4].
- Relying on voluntary company commitments alone, absent "a network of third-party auditors, by government agencies or by international bodies" [S4][S5].
- Assuming math-competition performance is the goal rather than a testbed; he says OpenAI could build "an amazing automated mathematician" but that isn't the priority [S2].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What specific, multi-day task could your system complete unsupervised today, and where exactly does it still need a human to step in?
   - Push until: a named task, its actual duration, and the precise point of required human intervention.
   - Red flags: "it can do most things now"; no concrete task named; claiming full autonomy with no failure case described.
2. Which of this quarter's capability gains came from more compute or a longer leash, and which came from a genuinely new technique?
   - Push until: a specific new idea or method named, separated from "we just let it run longer" or "we scaled it up."
   - Red flags: crediting all progress to scale; no distinction between a longer run and a better method.
3. If this system ran an entire project on its own and something went wrong, what restriction or sandbox catches it before it touches anything real?
   - Push until: a named mechanism that exists before deployment, not a plan to review logs afterward.
   - Red flags: "we'll monitor it closely"; no sandbox or restriction named; trusting the system because it has performed well so far.
4. How confident are you, specifically, in your ability to monitor and align this system, and is that confidence rising or falling as it gets more capable?
   - Push until: a stated trend (rising/falling) and the evidence behind it — not a one-time assurance.
   - Red flags: "we're comfortable with where we are"; treating monitoring confidence as a solved, static fact.
5. What would a shared safety bar for this kind of system actually look like, and who outside your own team would enforce it?
   - Push until: a named external party (auditor, regulator, industry body) and a concrete threshold they'd check against.
   - Red flags: "we have internal policies for that"; no external enforcement named; treating a company blog post as the standard.
6. Are you building toward a benchmark win or toward something with measurable economic or scientific impact — and how would you tell the difference?
   - Push until: a real-world measure named (a task completed, a discovery made, hours of human work replaced) distinct from a leaderboard score.
   - Red flags: citing only benchmark saturation as evidence of progress; no real-world measure offered.

## Grounding

- Left academic theoretical computer science for OpenAI in 2017 after competing in international coding contests, and was "still among the biggest skeptics" there at the time [S1].
- Became chief scientist in 2024, succeeding Ilya Sutskever, and co-leads research with chief research officer Mark Chen; the two describe the roles as fluid, saying "we're both researchers, we pull on technical threads" [S1].
- Was a key architect of OpenAI's o1 and o3 reasoning models [S1].
- Calls the arrival of computers that can develop new technologies themselves "a very important... inflection point," and says "two years ago the risks that we were imagining were mostly theoretical risks" [S1][S2].
- Describes the near-term goal as an "automated research intern," a system you could delegate tasks to that would take a person a few days, expecting it to come from models that can work "indefinitely" [S2].
- Does not expect systems "as smart as people in all ways" even by 2028, but argues transformative impact does not require that [S2].
- Says current reasoning models are "a form of reasoning" rooted in pre-training, not reasoning identical to human thought [S3].
- Reports "significant evidence that the models are capable of discovering novel insights," and calls autonomous, economically useful research "closest to what I previously emotionally thought of as AGI" [S3].
- Wrote in a September 2026 essay, "An Alien Mind," that "no lab has solved alignment and monitoring to a sufficient degree" and that "this is a time that calls for extreme caution" [S4][S5].
- Argued that "the idea of racing forward at all costs seems absurd once one internalizes the seriousness of the stakes," and that scaling "has to be constrained by our confidence in safety" [S4][S5].
- Called for mandatory safety thresholds "enforced by a network of third-party auditors, by government agencies or by international bodies," saying he expects and hopes "voluntary slowdowns to become commonplace until shared safety bars are established" [S4].
