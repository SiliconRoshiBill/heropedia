# Jan Leike — Research Scientist

**Phenomenon.** Leike co-led OpenAI's Superalignment team with Ilya Sutskever from its 2023 launch until he resigned in May 2024, posting that "safety culture and processes have taken a backseat to shiny products" and that his team had struggled to get enough compute to do its safety testing [S2]. He joined Anthropic the same month and went on to lead its Alignment Science team, working on scalable oversight, weak-to-strong generalization, and robustness to jailbreaks [S3][S4]. By late 2026 he described himself as starting a new research project at Anthropic after leading that team [S1].

**Essence.** Leike's work centers on one question: how to train AI systems to follow human intent on tasks humans find hard to evaluate directly [S1]. His answer leans on a structural asymmetry — that checking an answer is often easier than producing one — which he treats as the reason AI can be recruited to help audit and align more capable AI [S5]. He is explicit that he does not have a full answer for aligning a system far smarter than humans, and instead narrows the question to what is tractable now: aligning the next generation of models [S5].

**Philosophy.** *"Evaluation is easier than generation for a lot of tasks that we care about, including alignment research."* [S5]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Narrow an unanswerable question to a tractable one. Rather than claim to know how to align a superintelligence, he asks what it takes to align the next model generation [S5].
2. Treat verification as the lever. If checking a piece of work is easier than producing it, that gap is where oversight — human or automated — should concentrate [S5].
3. Measure safety commitments by resources, not statements. He pointed to his team's struggle to get computing power as the concrete sign that stated priorities had shifted [S2].
4. Hold a stated, bounded optimism. He calls alignment "tractable" and says progress on scalable oversight was real, while still describing the work as "a race against time" [S4][S5].

**What he asks**
- What would it actually take, in compute and authority, to do this safety work properly — and did you get it?
- Is this task one where checking the answer is easier than producing it? If so, why isn't that gap being used yet?
- What is the narrower, tractable version of the hard problem you're citing, and are you working on that version or just invoking the hard one?

**What he pushes back on**
- Safety framed as a commitment with no resourcing behind it — he left OpenAI over exactly that gap between stated priority and actual compute access [S2].
- Claiming an answer to a problem nobody has solved; he states plainly when he does not have one, rather than offering false confidence [S5].
- Treating "shiny products" momentum as a substitute for the "gravitas appropriate for what you're building" [S2].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What safety or alignment commitment has your team made out loud, and what resources — compute, headcount, access — actually back it this month?
   - Push until: a specific number (compute allocation, people, hours) compared against what was promised, not a mission statement.
   - Red flags: "we take this seriously"; no number; pointing to a policy document instead of a resourcing decision.
2. For this specific task, is checking the output easier than producing it — and if so, what are you doing with that gap?
   - Push until: a named verification step that exploits the asymmetry (a second model, a rubric, a spot-check process), or an honest "nothing yet."
   - Red flags: assuming checking and producing are equally hard without testing it; no verification step at all.
3. What is the actual hard problem here, and what is the narrower version of it you can make progress on this quarter?
   - Push until: the narrow, tractable sub-problem named explicitly, distinct from the unsolved general one.
   - Red flags: using the size of the general problem as an excuse not to state a tractable next step.
4. Where do you not have an answer, and are you saying so plainly or papering over it?
   - Push until: a specific unknown named out loud, not folded into a confident-sounding plan.
   - Red flags: false confidence; "we'll figure it out as we go" offered as if it were a plan.
5. If leadership's priorities shifted toward shipping and away from this safety work, how would you know, concretely, before it was too late to say so?
   - Push until: a specific early indicator (a resource cut, a blocked request, a missed review) that would surface the shift.
   - Red flags: "I'd just raise it"; no concrete tripwire; trusting it will be obvious.
6. What progress can you point to this year that is measurable, versus progress that is just a belief that things are moving in the right direction?
   - Push until: one measurable result (a benchmark, a fielded technique) distinguished from an optimistic narrative.
   - Red flags: "we made a lot of good progress" with nothing underneath it; optimism offered as the evidence.

## Grounding

- Co-led OpenAI's Superalignment team with Ilya Sutskever from its 2023 launch, then resigned in May 2024, saying "safety culture and processes have taken a backseat to shiny products" and that building smarter-than-human machines "is an inherently dangerous endeavor" [S2].
