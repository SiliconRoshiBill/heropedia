Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said this week that US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner want a Russia-Ukraine peace agreement reached ["as soon as possible,"](https://www.yahoo.com/news/politics/articles/zelenskyy-trumps-envoys-pushing-peace-115100018.html) ideally before January, even as he insisted, "We very much want peace too, but not at Ukraine's expense alone." The push follows a November 12 trip in which Witkoff and Kushner [met Vladimir Putin in Moscow](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/12/us-envoys-arrive-in-moscow-for-russia-ukraine-talks-with-putin) as the US and Russia resumed direct talks after a nine-month pause, then flew to Dublin to brief Zelensky. Heropedia's new [Steve Witkoff persona](/politics/policy-advisor/steve-witkoff) is built around the habit behind that trip and the ones before it in Gaza: settle on the outcome first, refuse to negotiate without knowing exactly where everyone stands, and keep re-testing whether the other side's position is really as fixed as it looks.

This is an AI persona based on Witkoff's own interviews and public statements, with every fact and quote tied to a source on the hero page. It is not Steve Witkoff, and neither he nor the US government has any affiliation with or gives any endorsement to Heropedia. The persona has no view on the terms of any peace deal, who should concede what, or the politics of the war — it works from his documented negotiating habits, not from a position on the conflict.

## Why his method is worth borrowing

Before he had a diplomatic title, Witkoff spent decades closing real-estate deals, and he says the same discipline carries over: "negotiating is being outcome oriented," meaning he and the president settle on where they want to land before arguing about how to get there. He treats incomplete information as the single biggest risk in any talk — "operating blind is really the problem in a negotiation like this. You have to know where everybody stands" — which is why he has sat across from Vladimir Putin and Hamas's negotiators directly rather than relying on cables from staff. That direct contact changes what he's willing to assume. Of Hamas he has said, "I don't think that they are as ideologically locked in," and of Putin, "I don't regard Putin as a bad guy" — not endorsements, but a refusal to treat either side's position as permanently fixed before testing it. The persona's questions are built from that same habit: name the outcome, check the facts on the ground yourself, and keep probing whether "they'll never move on that" is still true.

## The six questions it will ask

Bring one real negotiation — a vendor contract, a dispute with a partner, a stalled ask from your own boss — because the persona pushes until your answer names a real person, a real channel, or a real test, not a general sense of where things stand.

1. **What is the actual outcome you are negotiating toward, stated as one sentence before any tactic — and has your counterpart heard you say it directly?** It wants a concrete end state both sides could describe the same way, not a position or a process.
2. **Where does each party to this actually stand today, and how do you know — a real conversation, or an assumption?** A current position sourced to something said recently, the same standard behind his insistence on knowing "where everybody stands" before acting.
3. **If you spent five minutes making the other side's case as well as they would make it, what would a fair deal look like in their terms?** What they actually gain, in their language — not your framing of their objection.
4. **Do you have direct access to the person who can actually say yes, or are you negotiating through someone who has to go ask?** A named person with real authority, closer to sitting across from Putin himself than waiting on a staffer's reply.
5. **What have you decided is fixed about the other side's position, and when did you last actually test whether that's still true?** The specific assumption, and a recent attempt to check it — the same test behind "I don't think that they are as ideologically locked in."
6. **What would it take for the other side to trust you enough to move first — and have you done that thing yet, or just asked them to?** A concrete action, not a request for good faith.

## What it will push back on

The persona rejects writing off an adversary's position as permanently fixed without recently testing it, and it pushes back on negotiating only through intermediaries when direct access to the real decision-maker exists. It also rejects calling something a deal when it only works for one side — his own standard is that "a good deal has to work for everybody." It has no opinion on the terms under discussion in any specific negotiation, including the one he's currently running; it works from his documented habit of starting from the outcome and testing assumptions, not from a position on the war.

## How to get a useful session

- **Bring one real negotiation, not a general frustration.** "I'm stuck on this clause with this specific counterpart" works far better than "negotiations never go my way."
- **Know who actually holds the authority to say yes** before question four, so the session can test your access instead of your hope.
- **Decide what you've been assuming is fixed** before question five, so the session can actually test it rather than confirm it.

## Try Jared Kushner on a different kind of discipline

A different diagnostic habit on the very same negotiation catches different blind spots. Heropedia also adds [Jared Kushner](/politics/policy-advisor/jared-kushner) today — Witkoff's fellow envoy in both the Gaza and Ukraine talks, whose own rule is to strip diplomatic language down to the plain objective underneath it and price exactly what holding out is costing the other side. Where Witkoff asks whether you actually know where everyone stands, Kushner asks what the plain, one-sentence version of your goal is once you cut the institutional language away.
