Most product reviews are polite. Someone walks through a deck, the room nods, and the thing
ships mostly unchanged. An office hour with Heropedia's [Steve Jobs persona](/product/product-critic/steve-jobs)
is built to break that pattern. It does not grade on a curve, and it does not soften anything.

This is an AI persona based on Jobs's public interviews, talks and the biography written about
him, with every fact and quote tied to a source on the hero page. It is not Steve Jobs, and he
has no affiliation with Heropedia and does not endorse it.

## Why this persona is worth your Tuesday afternoon

The persona's standard is binary: a thing is either insanely great or it is not worth shipping.
"Good" does not count. Before anything else, it pushes on three questions: why does this product
need to exist, who exactly is holding it, and what did you say no to in order to make room for it.

Those three questions come straight from how Jobs ran Apple. He told WWDC developers in 1997:
"Focus is about saying, No." He framed that discipline as subtraction, not restraint: "I'm
actually as proud of the things we haven't done as the things I have done. Innovation is saying
no to 1,000 things." The persona asks you to show the same proof of subtraction before it will
take your product seriously.

## The six questions it will ask

Bring a real product and have rough answers ready, because it pushes until the answer is
specific.

1. **Why does this need to exist, and can you say why in one sentence?** A feature list does not
   count. Neither does the pitch "it's like X but for Y".
2. **Who, specifically, is holding this, and what are they doing the moment before and after?**
   Not a persona slide. One named kind of user in one concrete situation.
3. **What did you cut to make room for the thing this does well?** Three specific features or
   requests you said no to, and who asked for each.
4. **Walk me through the first five seconds after it opens — what do they see, frame by frame?**
   A flow diagram does not answer this. Neither does "they'll figure it out."
5. **What does it do before anyone touches anything, and why that default and not another?**
   The persona treats "it's configurable" as a dodge. It wants the exact default and the
   reasoning that chose it.
6. **Are you proud enough of this to use it yourself today, or are you still making excuses for
   it?** It wants a direct yes with a reason, or the specific part you're embarrassed by and the
   plan to fix it before shipping.

These follow the standard documented on the hero page: defaults are the product, because
"Settings are a confession that you couldn't decide." The test for craft is whether you'd use the
thing yourself, not whether it tested well in a focus group.

## What it will reject outright

The persona is written to dismiss five specific excuses, because they are the ones that let
mediocre products ship. "Our users asked for it" does not work: "A lot of times, people don't
know what they want until you show it to them." "That's how the competitors do it" gets the
reply that you should stop trying to be a competitor. "It tested well in focus groups" fares no
better — he told BusinessWeek in 1998, "It's really hard to design products by focus groups."
"We don't have time to make it that polished" and "It's just the MVP" both get pushed back on
too: delay the launch rather than ship something you aren't proud of.

## How to get a useful session

- **Show the thing, not the deck.** The persona's own standard is to review the actual product,
  not the roadmap. If you don't have something to walk through yet, come back when you do.
- **Answer in one sentence, not a list.** When it asks why the product exists, a feature list is
  a sign the product itself is confused, not just the pitch.
- **Name the cuts.** If you can't name three real features or requests you said no to, the
  persona's position is that you haven't made the product yet — only assembled one.
- **Walk through the first five seconds literally.** Frame by frame, not a diagram. This is where
  most teams discover they've never actually looked at their own opening screen.
- **Write down the one thing to be proud of by Friday.** The session is built to end there: either
  a direct yes, or the specific part you're embarrassed by and a plan to fix it.
