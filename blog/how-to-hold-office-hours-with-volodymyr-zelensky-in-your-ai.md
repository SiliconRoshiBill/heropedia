In his nightly video address on November 18, 2026, Volodymyr Zelensky said Ukraine's steps to strengthen its own defenses send Russia a clear message that the war must end, and that Europe should take part in any future peace talks because [issues affecting Ukraine have to be resolved with Ukraine in the room](https://kyivindependent.com/russia-will-achieve-none-of-its-objectives-zelensky-details-upcoming-allies-meeting/). Days earlier, he had [warned that a European security framework floated by US negotiators could end up benefiting Russia](https://www.adn.com/nation-world/2026/11/14/zelenskyy-says-a-new-european-security-framework-floated-by-us-could-benefit-putin/) rather than Ukraine, rejecting any changes to Ukraine's borders or neutral status before a ceasefire is even reached. Heropedia's new [Volodymyr Zelensky persona](/politics/president/volodymyr-zelensky) is built around the habit behind both statements: insisting that nothing about his country's future gets decided without proof, enforcement and a seat at the table.

This is an AI persona based on Zelensky's own speeches, interviews and writing, with every fact and quote tied to a source on the hero page. It is not Volodymyr Zelensky, and neither he nor the Ukrainian government has any affiliation with or gives any endorsement to Heropedia. The persona has no view on the war's politics, the terms under discussion, or who is right in the negotiations — it works from his documented habit of demanding proof and leverage, not from a position on the conflict itself.

## Why his method is worth borrowing

Within days of Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022, when the United States offered to help him leave Kyiv, Zelensky refused and later recalled his own answer plainly: "I need ammunition, not a ride." When disinformation claimed he had fled the capital, he didn't issue a statement denying it — he walked into a Kyiv street with his aides on camera, saying afterward, "I had to do this because I was showing that this was no green screen." He treats diplomacy the same way he treats a rumor: as a claim to be checked, not a promise to be trusted. Asked about the enforcement-free security assurances Ukraine received in the 1994 Budapest Memorandum, his answer was blunt: "Can we trust this? No." His own standard for ending the war follows from that same instinct: "we need to be in a strong position and not talk but end the war." The persona's questions are built from that habit — proof over promise, position before conversation.

## The six questions it will ask

Bring one real situation — an agreement you're relying on, a decision being made without you in the room, a claim you keep restating instead of demonstrating — because the persona pushes until your answer names a specific document, a specific lever, or a specific action, not a general sense of good faith.

1. **What is the written guarantee you are relying on here, and exactly who enforces it if the other side breaks their word?** It wants the clause and a named enforcer with real power, not a handshake — the same standard behind his skepticism of the Budapest Memorandum.
2. **Are you actually in the room for the decision that will determine your own future, or is someone else deciding it on your behalf?** A named meeting or forum, and whether you hold a seat and a vote in it, modeled on his insistence that Ukraine's future be settled with Ukraine present.
3. **What is the one piece of visible proof you could put in front of people today, instead of asking them to simply take your word?** Closer to walking into a Kyiv street on camera than issuing a statement.
4. **Where are you negotiating from a weaker position than you need to be, and what specific pressure could you apply before the next conversation?** A named lever, not a hope that the other side will be reasonable — the same standard behind "we need to be in a strong position and not talk but end the war."
5. **What have you refused to concede out of principle, and have you actually priced what that refusal is costing you?** The specific concession on the table, and a named cost of holding the line.
6. **What did you actually show up and do today, visibly, in front of the people counting on you — not what's on tomorrow's plan?** Modeled on his habit of addressing the public directly and often, rather than going quiet once a crisis feels less acute.

## What it will push back on

The persona rejects taking a written assurance at face value when nothing but goodwill backs it — his own standard is to name the enforcer, not just the clause. It pushes back on being talked about instead of talked to in decisions that determine your own future, and on treating regular, visible communication as optional once the hardest part of a situation seems to have passed. It has no opinion on the terms of any peace plan, who should concede what territory, or Ukraine's politics — it works from his documented habit of demanding proof and leverage, not from a position on the war.

## How to get a useful session

- **Bring one real guarantee or dependency**, not a general worry about being treated unfairly. "I'm relying on this verbal commitment from X" works far better than "I'm not sure they'll follow through."
- **Know what leverage you actually have** before question four, so the session produces a specific move instead of a wish.
- **Decide what you're not willing to concede before question five**, so the session can price the cost honestly instead of avoiding the question.
