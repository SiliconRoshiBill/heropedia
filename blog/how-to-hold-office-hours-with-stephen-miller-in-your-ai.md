On November 6, 2026, President Trump [named Stephen Miller the 33rd White House Chief of Staff](https://www.whitehouse.gov/releases/2026/11/president-trump-names-stephen-miller-as-33rd-white-house-chief-of-staff/), succeeding Susie Wiles two days after Republicans [lost control of both chambers of Congress](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/6/who-is-stephen-miller-trumps-new-white-house-chief-of-staff) in the midterms. At 41, Miller had already spent a decade as the architect of the administration's hardest-edged immigration policy — the 2017 travel ban, the 2018 family-separation "zero tolerance" border policy, and since January 2025 a mass deportation campaign built around a hard daily count. Whatever you think of the politics, the way he argues for it is a distinct, repeatable method, and that method is what Heropedia's [Stephen Miller persona](/politics/policy-advisor/stephen-miller) runs as an office hour.

This is an AI persona built from Miller's own recorded words — a CBS *Face the Nation* interview, a White House press briefing transcript, and a televised exchange over the Statue of Liberty's inscribed poem — with every fact and quote tied to a source on the hero page. It is not Stephen Miller, and neither he nor the White House has any affiliation with or gives any endorsement to Heropedia.

## The test behind the rhetoric

Strip away the specific policy fights, and Miller's method is consistent across a decade of them: a goal without an enforcement number and a deadline is not a goal, and an outcome is decided by leverage, not by sentiment. Announcing his deportation targets to Fox News in 2025, he put it in exactly those terms: the administration had "set a goal of a minimum of 3,000 arrests for ICE every day." Pressed on immigration politics more broadly, he has framed the whole fight as one governed by force rather than fairness — "we live in a world, in the real world... that is governed by strength, that is governed by force. These are the iron laws of the world that have existed since the beginning of time."

That same instinct shows up when he is challenged rather than when he is announcing something. In a 2017 White House briefing, a reporter cited the poem on the Statue of Liberty's pedestal to argue for a more generous immigration bill. Miller didn't engage the poem's content — he noted it "was added later, it's not actually part of the original Statue of Liberty," and dismissed the whole line of questioning as a sign of "cosmopolitan bias." The persona generalizes that same move into a sharp, useful diagnostic question that has nothing to do with immigration: when someone calls your decision wrong, are they pointing at an actual factual error, or asserting a value you never agreed to adopt?

## The six questions it will ask

Bring one real decision — a negotiation, a plan with a soft deadline, a project where you suspect you're relying on someone else's goodwill — because the persona is built to convert vague intentions into numbers and named parties.

1. **Who exactly is this decision for, named specifically, and who loses if you get it right?** One named beneficiary, one named group that pays a real cost — not "everyone" or "our stakeholders."
2. **What is the actual enforceable number behind this goal, and by what date?** A count, a rate or a deadline. "Significant progress" doesn't pass.
3. **Who has the power to slow or block this, and have you tested whether they actually will?** A named blocker, with evidence you've pressure-tested their resistance rather than assumed cooperation.
4. **Are you negotiating this from real leverage, or hoping the other side plays fair?** One concrete thing you hold that the other party needs.
5. **When someone calls this decision wrong, are they naming a factual error, or asserting a value you haven't agreed to?** The factual claim gets isolated and checked; the values disagreement gets named as a separate, open question.
6. **What is today's real number against the target you set, and who is accountable for the gap?** A dated figure next to the committed target, with one named owner.

These follow the method documented on the hero page: measure by enforcement, not announcement; assume leverage decides outcomes; and separate a factual objection from a values-based one instead of treating every pushback as equally disqualifying.

## What it will push back on

The persona won't accept a goal stated only as a direction of travel. If you describe progress as "significant" or "promising" without a number and a date attached, expect it to ask for both before it moves on. It treats that gap — between what sounds like momentum and what is actually measured — as the first thing worth fixing in almost any plan.

It is also specifically built to separate an objection grounded in fact from one grounded purely in sentiment, because that is the distinction Miller has repeatedly drawn under pressure. The persona isn't making a judgment about which kind of objection should win; it is forcing you to say, out loud, which kind you're actually facing before you decide how to answer it.

## How to get a useful session

- **Bring one real decision**, not a general complaint about a slow organization. A specific negotiation, deadline or stalled plan gives the persona something concrete to push against.
- **Have a number ready, or admit you don't.** Question two is where most sessions stall the first time, because most plans don't actually have one yet.
- **Name your leverage before the session, not during it.** If you can't name what you hold that the other side needs, that's the real finding.
- **Decide in advance whether you're facing a fact or a value.** Question five only works if you're honest about which one you're actually arguing.
