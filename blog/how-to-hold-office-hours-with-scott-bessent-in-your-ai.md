Nvidia spent eleven days without a permanent chief executive, and the fight over how it should fill the job pulled in a cabinet secretary and some of Silicon Valley's largest investors. On November 12, 2026, [Jensen Huang](/founder/ceo-advisor/jensen-huang) [resigned](https://sfstandard.com/2026/11/12/nvidia-ceo-jensen-huang-resigns/) as Nvidia's president, CEO and board member amid a board investigation into his personal conduct, with CFO [Colette Kress](/founder/ceo-advisor/colette-kress) stepping in as interim CEO. Three days later, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent went on Fox News and [called for "a dramatic shake-up"](https://www.foxbusiness.com/business-leaders/bessent-calls-nvidia-leadership-shake-up-amid-china-chip-scandal-urges-outside-ceo), telling the board it needed to hire from outside the company and giving it ten days to answer whether it had ignored warning signs that chips were reaching China through shell companies. Then, on November 23, Nvidia's board reversed course entirely: it [reinstated Huang as president and CEO effective immediately](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/nvidia-board-reinstates-jensen-huang-as-president-ceo-and-board-director), with board chair Harvey C. Jones saying the board has "every confidence in Jensen."

Bessent didn't get the outside hire he asked for. But the way he argued for one — isolate the checkable fact, attach a date to the demand, separate the personal scandal from the compliance question underneath it — is a distinct, reusable method, and that method is what Heropedia's [Scott Bessent persona](/politics/policy-advisor/scott-bessent) runs as an office hour.

This is an AI persona built from Bessent's own recorded words — his Fox Business remarks on Nvidia, a South China Morning Post interview on chip sales through Singapore, and an All-In Podcast interview on tariffs and the Fed — with every fact and quote tied to a source on the hero page. It is not Scott Bessent, and neither he nor the Treasury Department has any affiliation with or gives any endorsement to Heropedia.

## The test behind the demand

Bessent's background is four decades as a global macro investor, rising at Soros Fund Management under Stanley Druckenmiller before founding his own hedge fund, Key Square Capital Management, in 2015. That training shows up in how he handled Nvidia's crisis: instead of starting from the personal scandal dominating headlines, he asked a narrower question he could actually check. "I want to keep coming back to what was the culture at Nvidia, and were they pressing these seemingly illicit sales to goose their revenues and their earnings?" he said, adding that he'd seen reporting that "only 1% of the chips sold in Singapore remained in Singapore."

He also refused to accept an open-ended review. "I think we need answers in the next 10 days from the board and from the outside counsel," he said — a demand for a number and a date, not a general sense of urgency. And asked whether Nvidia's situation called for new export-control legislation, he said no: "We have all the rules we need; we just need them enforced." The persona generalizes that same instinct into a question that has nothing to do with chips or China: before you accept a conclusion, can you name the one fact behind it that you could actually go check?

## The six questions it will ask

Bring a real decision — a deal you're evaluating, a claim a colleague made that you haven't verified, a deadline nobody has actually written down — because the persona is built to separate what you know from what you're assuming.

1. **What is the one provable fact here, separate from the story everyone is telling about it?** A specific, checkable fact, not a conclusion borrowed from someone's reputation.
2. **What is the actual deadline on this decision, and who is accountable for missing it?** A date and a named party, not "soon."
3. **Do you need a new rule here, or does an existing one just need to be enforced?** Name the rule you already have before asking for a new one.
4. **What leverage do you actually hold in this negotiation, and have you used it yet?** A concrete asset or dependency, not an appeal to fairness.
5. **If you stated this as a probability instead of a certainty, what would the number be, and why?** An actual range, with the evidence behind it.
6. **Where are you confident about the direction but not the timing, and how does your plan account for that gap?** Separate what you're sure of from what you're guessing at.

These follow the method documented on the hero page: isolate the checkable fact, demand a date, and treat confidence about where something is headed as different from confidence about when it arrives.

## What it will push back on

The persona won't let a scandal substitute for an answer to the compliance question sitting underneath it. If you describe a situation only in terms of who looks bad, expect it to ask what the actual, checkable fact is — a filing, a transaction, a number — separate from anyone's reputation. It treats that gap, between the story and the record, as the first thing worth resolving in almost any dispute.

It's also built to resist closed-minded forecasting. Bessent has argued that most forecasting failures come from people who "didn't have an open mind," not from bad information. The persona will ask you to state a real probability rather than a certainty, and to say what evidence that number rests on — not who you'd rather be right.

## How to get a useful session

- **Bring one real claim or decision**, not a general sense that something is off. A specific deal, deadline or disputed story gives the persona something concrete to check.
- **Have your "provable fact" ready, or admit you don't have one yet.** Question one is where most sessions stall the first time.
- **Separate your deadline from your sense of urgency.** If the date is really just a feeling, question two will catch it.
- **Decide in advance whether you're arguing direction or timing.** Question six only works if you're honest about which one you're actually unsure of.
