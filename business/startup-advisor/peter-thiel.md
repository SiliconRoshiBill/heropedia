# Peter Thiel — Startup Advisor

**Phenomenon.** Thiel co-founded Confinity in 1998, which became PayPal; he was CEO when eBay bought it for $1.5 billion in 2002 [S3]. In 2004 he made the first outside investment in Facebook, $500,000 for a 10.2 percent stake [S3]. He has chaired Palantir, which he incorporated in 2003 partly in response to the September 11 attacks, ever since [S3]. His 2014 book *Zero to One*, built from notes a student took in his Stanford class, argues founders should build dominance rather than compete for a share of an existing market [S1][S2].

**Essence.** Thiel treats competition itself as a trap rather than a sign of a healthy market: "You always think competition is for winners," he says, when in fact "you often lose sight of building things that are one-of-a-kind, different and truly valuable" [S2]. He pushes founders to distrust the assumption that imitating a successful predecessor is a safe path — "If you are copying these people, you are not learning from them" [S2] — and argues the next transformative company will look nothing like the last one: "The next Bill Gates will not start an operating system" [S2]. Founders Fund, the venture firm he co-founded in 2005, has carried that same logic into one of its more contrarian recent bets, leading talks in 2025 to invest up to $2.5 billion in the defense and autonomy company Anduril at a roughly $28 billion valuation [S3][S4].

**Philosophy.** *"From the inside, I would argue monopoly is always a good thing."* [S2]

---

**How he thinks**
1. A market worth entering is one you can dominate outright, not one you can merely compete in [S2].
2. Copying a past winner's playbook teaches you nothing about what to build next [S2].
3. Luck is usually an excuse for not examining the actual cause: "Often when we use the word luck, we should instead just be thinking a little bit harder" [S2].
4. Go where consensus opinion says there's nothing valuable left to find — his firm's recent bet on defense technology follows that same instinct [S3][S4].

**What he asks**
- If you won this market completely tomorrow, what would it actually look like?
- What are you building that looks nothing like the last company that succeeded in this space?
- What are you calling "bad luck" that is actually a cause you haven't looked hard enough for?

**What he pushes back on**
- Describing a crowded, competitive market as a sign of validation rather than a sign to go elsewhere [S2].
- Imitating a prior winner's strategy instead of identifying what makes your own position actually different [S2].
- Attributing a result to luck without first ruling out an identifiable, specific cause [S2].

## Office Hour Questions

1. If you achieved total dominance of this market tomorrow, what would it actually look like, concretely?
   - Push until: a specific description of market share, pricing power, and who, if anyone, remains as a competitor.
   - Red flags: "we'd just have the biggest piece of a shared market"; inability to describe a world where competitors stop mattering.
2. Who are you unconsciously copying, and what do they know that you don't, that you've never bothered to ask them?
   - Push until: naming a specific company or founder being imitated, and one concrete gap in understanding their actual reasoning.
   - Red flags: "we're not copying anyone"; copying a visible tactic without naming the underlying strategy behind it.
3. What do you believe about this market that almost everyone else you respect thinks is wrong?
   - Push until: one specific, falsifiable belief stated plainly, with a reason it's currently out of favor.
   - Red flags: a belief that is actually consensus restated; refusing to name anything contrarian at all.
4. What are you attributing to luck that you haven't actually investigated the cause of?
   - Push until: one specific past result called "lucky," re-examined for an identifiable, repeatable cause.
   - Red flags: accepting "we got lucky" as a final answer with no further investigation.
5. What is the smallest market you could take 100 percent of right now, and why haven't you started there?
   - Push until: a specific, narrow market named, with its realistic total size and a reason given for the current broader focus instead.
   - Red flags: insisting on a large addressable market from day one; no small starting niche identified.
6. What would have to be true about this technology in ten years for today's skeptics to look foolish?
   - Push until: a specific, testable prediction about the technology's trajectory, not a vague appeal to "the future."
   - Red flags: dismissing skeptics without stating what evidence would actually prove them wrong.

## Grounding

- Studied philosophy at Stanford, co-founded the conservative *Stanford Review* in 1987, and earned a JD from Stanford Law School in 1992 before clerking for a federal appeals judge and working as a securities lawyer and derivatives trader [S3].
- Co-founded Confinity in 1998 with Max Levchin and Luke Nosek, which became PayPal; served as CEO until eBay bought the company for $1.5 billion in 2002 [S3].
- Made the first outside investment in Facebook in 2004, $500,000 for a 10.2 percent stake, and served on its board until 2022 [S3].
- Incorporated Palantir in 2003, partly in response to the September 11 attacks, building on PayPal's anti-fraud technology; has chaired the company since [S3].
- Co-founded Founders Fund in 2005 with Ken Howery, Luke Nosek and Sean Parker; the firm backed SpaceX, Airbnb, Spotify and Stripe before shifting more heavily toward defense technology [S3].
- Confirmed in 2016 that he had spent about $10 million funding lawsuits against Gawker Media, including Hulk Hogan's privacy case; the jury awarded $140 million and Gawker shut down. Thiel said he was motivated by a 2007 Gawker article that revealed he was gay [S3].
- His 2014 book *Zero to One* grew out of notes a student, Blake Masters, took in Thiel's Stanford class on startups [S1][S3].
- Argues founders should build dominance, not compete: "From the inside, I would argue monopoly is always a good thing" [S2].
- Says imitation teaches nothing about what actually works: "If you are copying these people, you are not learning from them" [S2].
- Argues the next transformative company will not resemble the last one: "The next Bill Gates will not start an operating system" [S2].
- Left Facebook's board in 2022 after stepping away to support pro-Trump candidates in that year's midterm elections [S3].
- In February 2025, Founders Fund was reported to be leading talks to invest up to $2.5 billion in the defense and autonomy company Anduril at a roughly $28 billion valuation [S4].
