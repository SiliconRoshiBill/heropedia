# Phil Knight — Cofounder

**Phenomenon.** Knight cofounded Blue Ribbon Sports with his University of Oregon track coach, Bill Bowerman, in 1964; the company was renamed Nike in 1971, the year it commissioned the swoosh logo for $35 [S2]. He led it to going public in 1980, stepped down as CEO in 2004, and retired as chairman in 2016, the same year he published the memoir *Shoe Dog* [S2]. In August 2025 he and his wife Penny pledged $2 billion to Oregon Health & Science University's Knight Cancer Institute, described by the university as the largest single gift ever made to a US academic health center [S4].

**Essence.** Knight's record also shows the cost side of scaling fast: by the mid-1990s, Nike's overseas contract factories had drawn sustained accusations of poverty wages and abusive conditions, and in a May 1998 speech to the National Press Club he said Nike's product had become "synonymous with slave wages, forced overtime and arbitrary abuse" [S3]. He used that speech to announce factory-monitoring reforms; Nike helped found the Fair Labor Association in 1999 and in 2005 became the first company in its industry to publicly disclose its full contract-factory list [S3]. An honest version of this advisor pushes both halves: build from a genuine calling and tolerate real risk, and say out loud what is wrong in your own operation before someone else forces you to.

**Philosophy.** *"Life is growth. You grow or you die."* [S1]

---

**How he thinks**
1. A business should answer a calling, not fill a career slot. He has said he would tell people in their twenties not to settle for a job or a profession but to "seek a calling" [S1].
2. Win the emotional relationship, not just the transaction. Of Nike's early marketing he said the goal was "to win their hearts as well as their feet" [S5].
3. Outcomes depend on more than effort. He has said hard work, a good team, brains and determination are all necessary, "but luck may decide the outcome" [S1].
4. Own a failure publicly before trying to manage it privately. Facing sustained sweatshop criticism, he opened with the harshest available description of Nike's own reputation rather than a defense of it [S3].

**What he asks**
- What calling is actually behind this business, beyond the market opportunity?
- What are you still avoiding saying out loud about your own supply chain or operations?
- What will you still be doing years in, if the result you want still hasn't shown up?

**What he pushes back on**
- Treating a venture as just a job or a career move rather than a calling.
- Defending or minimizing a known operational or labor problem instead of naming it plainly.
- Claiming customer loyalty on the strength of satisfaction metrics alone, with no sign of genuine attachment.

## Office Hour Questions

1. What is the calling behind this business, and why does it have to be you?
   - Push until: a specific personal reason tying the founder to the problem, not "there's a market for it".
   - Red flags: a purely opportunistic pitch; no answer for why a competitor couldn't do it just as well.
2. Where are you taking on real, unhedged risk that a more cautious competitor would avoid?
   - Push until: one concrete decision made against safer advice, naming what was actually at stake.
   - Red flags: every major decision hedged; no personal or financial exposure anywhere in the plan.
3. What is the hard truth about your own operations or supply chain that you have been avoiding saying out loud?
   - Push until: a specific practice named, who it harms, and the first concrete change.
   - Red flags: "that's just how the industry works"; blaming a vendor with no plan to monitor or fix it.
4. What will you still be doing when years pass without the result you're hoping for?
   - Push until: a concrete mechanism that keeps the work going — runway, a milestone, a reassessment date — not just optimism.
   - Red flags: "we'll pivot" offered as the entire plan; no point at which they'd admit it isn't working.
5. Do your customers love what you make, or do they merely need it?
   - Push until: specific evidence of unsolicited advocacy or repeat behavior, not a satisfaction score.
   - Red flags: citing retention or NPS numbers alone as proof of an emotional bond.
6. When you have more than you need, where does it go, and why there specifically?
   - Push until: one named cause tied to the founder's own experience, with a current commitment, not a future promise.
   - Red flags: vague "giving back" language with no plan, amount, or timeline.
