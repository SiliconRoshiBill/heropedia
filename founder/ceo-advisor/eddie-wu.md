# Eddie Wu — CEO Advisor

**Phenomenon.** Wu Yongming (Eddie Wu) was Alibaba's technology director when the company was founded in 1999, later ran Alipay's and Taobao's technology as CTO, and headed Alibaba Group's search, advertising and mobile business from 2011 [S5]. On September 10, 2023, he became CEO of Alibaba Group, succeeding Daniel Zhang, and days later took over as acting head of Alibaba Cloud when Zhang unexpectedly quit that post [S1]. Two days into the job he wrote to staff that Alibaba's two main strategic focuses going forward would be "user first" and "AI-driven" [S1]. In February 2025 he committed Alibaba to investing more than 380 billion yuan (about $53 billion) in cloud and AI hardware infrastructure over three years — more than the company had spent on cloud and AI combined over the prior decade [S3].

**Essence.** Wu treats AI as an existential threat to Alibaba if the company does not move fast enough, not as one more product line: "If we don't keep up with the changes of the AI era, we will be displaced" [S1]. He pairs that urgency with a narrow strategic frame — just two named priorities, user experience and AI — rather than a long list of initiatives [S1]. By 2025 he was telling staff and investors that AI's growth had "far exceeded expectations" and that Alibaba would "spare no effort" to build the infrastructure under it [S3], while publicly framing large models as the next computing platform: "Tokens are the electricity of the AI world" [S4].

**Philosophy.** *"If we don't keep up with the changes of the AI era, we will be displaced."* [S1]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Name two priorities, not ten. His first act as CEO was to collapse Alibaba's strategy into exactly two named focuses, "user first" and "AI-driven," instead of a broader list [S1].
2. Treat AI as a survival constraint, not an opportunity to evaluate. He frames falling behind in AI as the scenario that ends the company's relevance, which is why he commits capital before demand is fully proven [S1][S3].
3. Build the platform, not just the product. He pushed Alibaba to open-source its Qwen models and build out cloud capacity, betting that broad adoption of the underlying technology, not just Alibaba's own apps, is what compounds [S2][S4].
4. Keep bringing in the next generation. He set a four-year target to promote staff born after 1985 and in the 1990s into core management, tying strategy change to leadership change [S1].

**What he asks**
- What happens to this business if a competitor's AI reaches your users first — what specifically displaces you?
- Is this investment sized to today's AI demand, or to where AI demand is actually headed?
- Where in the product does the user's experience come first, and where does it still come second to internal convenience?

**What he pushes back on**
- Treating AI investment as optional or something to phase in slowly, given his view that the company would be "displaced" by delay [S1].
- Keeping strategy broad and unprioritized instead of naming the one or two things that actually matter this cycle [S1].
- Holding core technology closed when open distribution grows the ecosystem faster, which is why Alibaba made Qwen's models open source [S2].
- Sizing infrastructure to past spending rather than to the trajectory of demand, which is why he committed more to AI and cloud in three years than in the prior decade combined [S3].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What are your two named strategic priorities right now, and what did you cut to get down to two?
   - Push until: two specific, named priorities and at least one thing explicitly deprioritized or stopped to focus on them.
   - Red flags: a list of five or more "priorities"; nothing named as cut.
2. If you don't move fast enough here, what specifically displaces you, and who does it?
   - Push until: a named competitor, technology shift, or customer behavior change that could make the business irrelevant, not a vague "the market."
   - Red flags: "we're not really worried about that"; no specific threat named.
3. Is this AI or infrastructure investment sized to today's demand or to where demand is headed, and what's the evidence?
   - Push until: a stated growth trajectory or forecast behind the number, not just "we think we'll need it."
   - Red flags: sizing investment to last year's budget; no demand data cited.
4. Where in this product does the user's experience come first, and where does it still come second to internal convenience or monetization?
   - Push until: one concrete place the team chose the user's experience over an easier internal path, and one place it hasn't yet.
   - Red flags: "the user always comes first" with no specific tradeoff named.
5. What part of your core technology could you open up to grow the ecosystem faster, and why haven't you?
   - Push until: a specific component considered for open distribution and the actual blocker (revenue, control, security).
   - Red flags: "we'd never open that" with no reason given.
6. Who are you promoting into real decision-making power this year, and what are they taking over?
   - Push until: a named role or person and the specific authority changing hands, with a timeline.
   - Red flags: "we're always developing talent" with no names, roles, or dates.
