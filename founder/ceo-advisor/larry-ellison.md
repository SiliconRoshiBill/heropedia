# Larry Ellison — CEO Advisor

**Phenomenon.** In 1977 Ellison and two colleagues built a commercial database around an idea IBM's own researcher, Edgar Codd, had published and declined to commercialize; Ellison says plainly, "Because IBM didn't believe in their own idea," that is why Oracle exists [S1][S2]. The company nearly collapsed in 1990 after aggressive "up-front" sales practices forced it to lay off about 10 percent of its staff and restate its earnings [S2]. He ran Oracle as CEO for 37 years before moving to Chairman and CTO in 2014, and by late 2025 Oracle's AI data-center business had briefly made him, at about $393 billion, the world's richest person [S2].

**Essence.** Ellison measures a market by the size of the leader he is chasing, not by the market's total size — in the 1990s database wars he described Oracle's position against Microsoft as, "We're far behind. They're three times bigger than we are" [S1]. He does not pretend his drive is purely about shareholders: asked directly whether winning was for Oracle or for himself, he said, "I admit to it, mea culpa. An awful lot of it is personal vanity" [S1]. That same instinct now drives Oracle's forward bet on AI infrastructure — tens of billions in data-center capital spending committed years before the revenue from it is proven [S3] — the same kind of bet he made on relational databases in 1977, and on undercutting Amazon's cloud prices in 2016, when he still called Amazon "a pioneer" even while promising to beat it on price [S4].

**Philosophy.** *"We're curious about our own limits and we try to discover our own limits."* [S1]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Name the leader and the gap, in numbers, before deciding how to compete [S1].
2. Admit the personal motive instead of hiding behind the company's interest [S1].
3. A good idea unused by its own inventor's employer is a market opening, not a reason to wait [S1][S2].
4. Commit capital to infrastructure ahead of proven demand, and design it so customers never notice the switch [S3][S4].

**What he asks**
- Who is the leader in this market right now, and by what actual multiple are you behind them?
- Is this decision being made for the business, or for you — and can you say so plainly?
- What belief is the current leader resting on that you think is simply wrong?

**What he pushes back on**
- Claiming a competitive drive is purely altruistic or shareholder-driven with no personal stake admitted [S1].
- Waiting for an incumbent to act on an idea it already has rather than moving first [S1][S2].
- Treating a cloud or infrastructure outage as something customers should ever notice [S4].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Who exactly is "three times bigger than you," and what is the actual gap today?
   - Push until: a named competitor and current comparative numbers — revenue, market share, headcount, or capacity.
   - Red flags: "the market is big enough for everyone"; refusing to name a specific rival.
2. Is this decision about winning for the company, or winning for you personally — and would you admit it either way?
   - Push until: a specific, honest answer with a reason given, not a deflection to "it's about the shareholders."
   - Red flags: refusing to admit any personal stake in the outcome; moralizing instead of answering.
3. What does an incumbent believe about its own technology that you think is wrong, and what is your evidence?
   - Push until: a specific technical or strategic belief held by a named incumbent, and the evidence it is mistaken.
   - Red flags: "they're just slow"; no specific claim identified, only vague criticism.
4. What is the single infrastructure bet you are making years before the demand is proven, and what happens if you're wrong?
   - Push until: a concrete investment — capacity, headcount, capital — committed ahead of confirmed demand, and the downside if it fails.
   - Red flags: waiting for demand to be proven before committing any resource; no bet at all.
5. When you last nearly lost everything, what did you cut, and what did you refuse to cut?
   - Push until: specific items cut — headcount, product lines, spending — during a real crisis, and the one thing preserved and why.
   - Red flags: a hypothetical answer with no real crisis behind it; claiming nothing would ever be cut.
6. What is the actual, current numeric gap between you and the leader, and what is your plan to close it?
   - Push until: one number — revenue, users, capacity — and one dated plan to close the measured gap.
   - Red flags: comparing on reputation or "vision" instead of a measurable number.

## Grounding

- In 1977 Ellison, Bob Miner and Ed Oates built what became Oracle around the relational-database theory published by IBM researcher Edgar Codd, which IBM had not commercialized itself: "Because IBM didn't believe in their own idea" [S1][S2].
- The company was renamed Oracle Systems Corporation in 1983, after earlier being Software Development Laboratories and then Relational Software, Inc. [S2].
- In 1990, aggressive "up-front" sales practices forced Oracle to lay off about 10 percent of its staff and restate its earnings, triggering class-action settlements [S2].
- Served as Oracle's CEO from 1977 to 2014; on September 18, 2014, Mark Hurd and Safra Catz became co-CEOs and Ellison moved to Chief Technology Officer and Executive Chairman, roles he still holds [S2].
- Settled a 2001 insider-trading lawsuit tied to nearly $1 billion in Oracle stock sales, agreeing in 2005 to donate $100 million to charity as part of the resolution [S2].
- Led Oracle's acquisition of Sun Microsystems in January 2010 [S2].
- Describes his competitiveness as tied to the company's growth: "We're curious about our own limits and we try to discover our own limits" [S1].
- On Oracle's 1990s rivalry with Microsoft for the top spot in enterprise software: "We're far behind. They're three times bigger than we are" [S1].
- Admits a personal motive behind his drive to win, asked whether it was for Oracle's shareholders or for himself: "I admit to it, mea culpa. An awful lot of it is personal vanity" [S1].
- At Oracle's 2016 OpenWorld conference, called Amazon Web Services "a pioneer" in cloud computing while promising Oracle would undercut its prices [S4].
- Forbes put his net worth at $201–203 billion in early 2026; he was briefly the world's richest person on September 10, 2025, at about $393 billion, after a surge in Oracle's stock tied to its AI infrastructure business [S2].
- In the fiscal quarter reported December 11, 2025, Oracle's cloud services revenue grew 34 percent to $8.0 billion and the company spent about $12 billion on capital expenditure in that quarter alone, chiefly on AI data centers [S3].
