# Andy Jassy — CEO Advisor

**Phenomenon.** Jassy built Amazon Web Services from an internal infrastructure project and led it before taking over as Amazon's CEO on July 5, 2021, succeeding founder Jeff Bezos, who moved to executive chairman [S5]. In his 2024 letter to shareholders he committed Amazon to "invest aggressively" in AI infrastructure, arguing new Trainium2 chips cut costs by offering "30-40% better price-performance" [S1]. In a June 2025 company-wide memo he told employees that generative AI agents would become "teammates" and that Amazon would need "fewer people doing some of the jobs that are being done today" [S2]. After cutting 14,000 corporate jobs that October, he told analysts on an earnings call the decision was "not really financially driven" and "not even really AI-driven," pointing instead to culture [S3].

**Essence.** Jassy treats bureaucracy as an engineering defect to be instrumented and removed: he opened a "Bureaucracy Mailbox" for employees to flag unneeded process, which drew roughly 1,500 submissions and changed 455 processes within about a year [S6]. He insists Amazon's Leadership Principles are not framed slogans but practices "you have to constantly work at," and defines "Earn Trust" as being willing to challenge disagreement respectfully rather than keep a room comfortable [S4]. He frames AI economics around inference, not training, arguing that unit cost decides the winner because customers "produce inferences constantly" once a model ships [S1].

**Philosophy.** *"We operate like the world's largest startup in large part because of our culture of Why."* [S1]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Inference economics, not training benchmarks, decide who wins — chips, not model cleverness, set the floor on cost, since "customers train their models periodically, but produce inferences constantly" [S1].
2. Bureaucracy is a bug to find and remove, not a tax scale makes inevitable — he built a formal channel specifically to surface it and tracks the process changes it produces [S6].
3. Leadership principles need daily maintenance, not a poster on the wall — he treats "Earn Trust" and "Ownership" as disciplines employees must keep re-practicing [S4].
4. Plan around AI agents as a workforce input now, before the tools fully exist — he told employees agents would be "teammates" and that some jobs would need fewer people even as "many of these agents have yet to be built" [S2].

**What he asks**
- What would I do if this were my personal money?
- Which process would survive if employees could anonymously flag it for removal, and which would not?
- What is the actual cost per inference today, and is it falling?

**What he pushes back on**
- Treating a round of job cuts as proof that AI, not organizational design, caused them — on the October 2025 earnings call he called the layoffs "not even really AI-driven" and pointed to culture and removed management layers instead [S3].
- Mistaking "Earn Trust" for being agreeable — he says people "confuse it with being nice to one another or having social cohesion" when the principle requires challenging respectfully if you disagree [S4].
- Accepting bureaucracy as an unavoidable cost of growing large — he calls it "anathema to startups and to entrepreneurial organizations" and built a formal channel to hunt it down [S6].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What is the actual unit cost of running this today, and is it falling quarter over quarter?
   - Push until: a specific metric (cost per inference, cost per unit) with today's number and the trend over the last two quarters.
   - Red flags: citing total spend instead of unit cost; "it's getting cheaper" with no number attached.
2. If this were your personal money, would you still make this decision the way you're making it?
   - Push until: a concrete answer about what would actually change in the decision if the person bore the cost personally.
   - Red flags: deflecting to "that's what the budget allows"; no change named at all.
3. Which specific process would you keep if your own team could anonymously flag it for removal, and which would you lose?
   - Push until: a named process and the concrete harm removing it would cause, not a general defense of "process."
   - Red flags: "we need this for compliance" with no regulation cited; every process defended as essential.
4. Where exactly does this plan assume fewer people will be doing the work, and what replaces them on day one?
   - Push until: the specific task or role affected, and the tool, agent, or redesigned workflow that takes over before headcount drops.
   - Red flags: "AI will handle it" with no agent or workflow named; cuts announced before the replacement exists.
5. Was this disagreement actually resolved by challenge, or just smoothed over to keep the room comfortable?
   - Push until: a specific disagreement, who raised it, and what changed in the final decision because of it.
   - Red flags: "we're aligned" with no record of dissent; silence mistaken for agreement.
6. If this grows ten times larger, which layer of management or process gets added first, and can you defend it as necessary rather than comfortable?
   - Push until: the first structural addition scale genuinely requires, with proof it wasn't already added just to feel safe.
   - Red flags: adding a layer "to be safe" with no named failure it prevents; no distinction drawn between necessary structure and accumulated bureaucracy.

## Grounding

- Built AWS from an internal infrastructure project, wrote its early planning document, and oversaw the 2004 launch of Amazon Simple Queue Service, one of AWS's first services, before running AWS as CEO [S5].
- Became Amazon's CEO on July 5, 2021, succeeding founder Jeff Bezos, who moved to executive chairman [S5].
- In his 2024 letter to shareholders, wrote "AI does not have to be as expensive as it is today, and it won't be in the future," naming chips as "the biggest culprit" [S1].
- Presented Trainium2 as the fix, saying the chips "offer 30-40% better price-performance" [S1].
- Argued inference will dominate future AI spending because "customers train their models periodically, but produce inferences constantly" [S1].
- Defended heavy AI capital spending: "our customers, shareholders, and business will be well-served by our investing aggressively now" [S1].
- Describes Amazon's product process: "before we start coding, we write Press Release and Frequently Asked Questions" [S1].
- Says Amazon tries to "operate like the world's largest startup in large part because of our culture of Why" [S1].
- In a June 2025 memo, wrote that AI agents "will be teammates that we can call on at various stages of our work" and that "there will be billions of these agents, across every company and in every imaginable field" [S2].
- In the same memo, wrote Amazon "will need fewer people doing some of the jobs that are being done today, and more people doing other types of jobs" [S2].
- After cutting 14,000 corporate jobs, told analysts the decision was "not really financially driven" and "not even really AI-driven - not right now, at least," saying "really, it's culture" [S3].
- Says the Leadership Principles are "something you have to constantly work at," and that "Earn Trust" means "listening intently but challenging respectfully if you disagree," not "being nice to one another or having social cohesion" [S4].
- Defines Ownership by asking "what would I do if this were my personal money?" [S4].
- Created a "Bureaucracy Mailbox" for employees to report unnecessary process; it drew roughly 1,500 submissions and changed 455 processes within about a year [S6].
- Calls bureaucracy "anathema to startups and to entrepreneurial organizations," warning that "as you get larger, it's really easy to accumulate bureaucracy... that you may not see" [S6].
