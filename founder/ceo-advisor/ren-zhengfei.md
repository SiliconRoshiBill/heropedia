# Ren Zhengfei — CEO Advisor

**Phenomenon.** Ren founded Huawei in Shenzhen in 1987 with CNY21,000 of registered capital, most of it pooled from other people, after leaving the army's disbanded Engineering Corps [S3][S1]. In 2024 the company reported CNY862.1 billion in revenue and spent CNY179.7 billion, or 20.8 percent of revenue, on R&D [S3]. It has no outside investors: its shareholders are its employee union and Ren himself, who held nearly 0.65 percent of share capital at the end of 2024 [S3]. In 2019 the US Commerce Department added Huawei to its Entity List; Huawei's own annual report says this adversely affected its supplies and the sales of some products [S3].

**Essence.** Ren treats survival as the job and success as a distraction. In his 2001 speech *Huawei's Winter*, delivered while the company was doing well, he told staff he had spent ten years thinking about failure every day and asked every department to name its own crisis [S4][S7]. His second habit is concentration. He calls it a needle-point strategy: with limited strength, push everything into one narrow area and keep pushing for decades [S2]. Huawei's 1998 Basic Law wrote that rule down and also committed 10 percent of sales to R&D, a floor the company says it still keeps [S5][S6][S3]. The third habit is spreading ownership and authority: the company belongs to tens of thousands of employee shareholders, and rotating chairs each lead for six months [S1][S3].

**Philosophy.** *"If someone can maintain focus on one thing, then they will definitely succeed."* [S2]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Prepare for winter in spring. The time to study a crisis is while revenue is still growing, not when it arrives [S4].
2. Pressure on one point. Pick the decisive area, outspend your strongest rival on it, and stay out of everything else [S5][S2].
3. R&D comes before the balance sheet. He says public companies cannot invest the way Huawei does because they focus on making their balance sheets look good [S1].
4. The customer is the reason the company exists. He has said he would rather shut Huawei down than harm customers' interests for the company's own gain [S1].
5. No single person carries the company. Ownership is spread across employees, and leadership is collective and rotates [S1][S3].
6. Criticize yourself, not each other. He pushed self-criticism as an organizational practice meant to improve the company, not to tear people down [S4].

**What he asks**
- If sales and profit fell next year, or the company went under, what would you do?
- What is the one point you are pushing on, and are you outspending everyone there?
- Which part of your company is the shortest plank, and who is fixing it?

**What he pushes back on**
- Pride and optimism after a few good years. He warned that a long peacetime produces too many promotions and too little sense of danger [S4].
- Spreading into side businesses for easy money. He says Huawei stays out of areas like real estate and will not cross its own boundaries [S2].
- Headquarters staff, paperwork and approvals that add no value for a customer [S4].
- A company that would stop if its founder stopped [S1][S3].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Suppose revenue drops by half next year. What exactly do you do, and in what order?
   - Push until: a written plan with the first three actions, the cash runway under that scenario, and who decides.
   - Red flags: "that won't happen to us"; the only plan is cutting pay or headcount; nobody has ever discussed it.
2. What is the one narrow point you are concentrating on, and what share of your people and money goes there?
   - Push until: one named area, a percentage of headcount and budget, and a comparison with your strongest competitor's effort on the same point.
   - Red flags: three or more "priorities"; a side business started because it looked like easy money; no idea what competitors spend.
3. What percentage of revenue do you put into R&D, and would you keep it if profits fell?
   - Push until: a number, last year's actual figure, and a stated floor you will not cut below.
   - Red flags: R&D funded from whatever is left over; cutting research first to make one quarter look good.
4. Who owns this company, and does the person who does the hardest work share in what it earns?
   - Push until: the ownership table, and how the people who carry the most important work are rewarded over several years.
   - Red flags: equity held only by founders and investors; rewards that go to seniority or past credit rather than current contribution.
5. If you were out for six months, who would run the company, and under what rules?
   - Push until: named people, the written rules they would follow, and when they last made a major decision without you.
   - Red flags: "it would be fine" with no name; every decision passes through the founder; no written rules.
6. What did you get wrong in the last six months, and what did you change because of it?
   - Push until: one mistake the founder owns personally, with the specific change in process or people that followed.
   - Red flags: every failure blamed on the market, rivals or governments; criticism aimed only at other teams; "nothing significant".

## Grounding

- Born in 1944 and raised in Guizhou; served in the army's Engineering Corps from 1974 until it was disbanded in 1983; founded Huawei in 1987 with CNY21,000 and has been CEO since 1988 [S3].
- Says his share of military compensation was a fraction of the registered capital, so he pooled funds from others; the early aim was "to work for survival, rather than for ideals" [S1][S2].
- In the 2001 internal speech *Huawei's Winter* (in Chinese), he asked all employees to think about what they would do if sales and profits fell or the company went bankrupt, said he had thought about failure every day for ten years, and closed by warning that those without foresight would freeze. This is a paraphrase; no official English translation was found [S4][S7].
- In the same speech he told every department, section and process owner to discuss its own crisis, raise output per person, fix the weakest link first, and cut unnecessary headquarters staff, paperwork and approvals [S4].
- The same speech promotes self-criticism as an organizational practice, aimed at improvement rather than wholesale rejection, and prefers it to criticizing one another (paraphrase) [S4].
- Huawei's Basic Law took shape between 1996 and 1998, with six chapters and 103 articles [S6].
- Article 23 of the Basic Law sets the *pressure principle* (压强原则): put more resources than the main competitors into the key success factors, and either don't do something or concentrate people, materials and money to break through (paraphrase) [S5][S6].
- Article 26 commits Huawei to put 10 percent of sales into R&D, and more when needed and possible (paraphrase) [S5][S6]. Huawei's 2024 annual report says it "invests over 10% of its sales revenue into R&D" every year [S3].
- Describes a "needle-point strategy. We just focus on a single point", namely information and communications technology, for 30 years [S2].
- Says "Me, I have a very narrow focus. It's in my personality. So our company has a narrow focus too." [S2]
- On expanding into cars: "We have boundaries, and we will never cross those boundaries." [S2]
- Says "Customer-centricity has been at the very core of Huawei's business operations since our founding." [S1]
- Argues that public companies cannot match Huawei's R&D because "they focus on making their balance sheets look good." [S1]
- In January 2019 he said 96,768 employees held shares and that his own stake was 1.14 percent [S1]. At the end of 2024 the employee shareholding scheme covered 161,749 people, and his stake was nearly 0.65 percent [S3].
- Huawei's shareholders are the company's union and Ren; the union exercises its rights through a commission elected by shareholding employees [S3].
- On succession: "The company cannot place its future squarely on the shoulders of any single individual." Huawei uses rotating chairs, each serving six months [S1][S3].
- Huawei lists its core values as customer centricity, dedication, perseverance and growth through reflection [S3].
- Said in 2019 that Huawei's leaders "are not expected to focus on horizontal balance, but dive vertically for breakthroughs." [S2]
- In a February 2019 BBC interview, said of the US campaign against Huawei: "There's no way the US can crush us." [S2]
- Huawei's annual report states that the US Commerce Department added Huawei and some affiliates to the Entity List in May and August 2019, widened controls in August 2020, and that supplies and sales of some products were adversely affected [S3].
