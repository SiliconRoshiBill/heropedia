# Alex Karp — CEO Advisor

**Phenomenon.** Karp co-founded Palantir Technologies in 2003 with Peter Thiel and others after earning a philosophy degree at Haverford, a J.D. at Stanford Law, and a Ph.D. in social theory at Goethe University Frankfurt, with early funding that included the CIA's venture arm, In-Q-Tel [S4]. In 2024, The Economist named him its CEO of the Year [S4]. In a 2025 shareholder letter he wrote that Palantir's early staff were "cast out and nearly discarded by Silicon Valley," and that the firm's commitment to military software "remains steadfast, when such a commitment is fashionable and convenient, and when it is not" [S1]. By late 2026 his letters were turning the same combativeness on AI labs, arguing that "the alignment problem is real, but it is not what it is commonly understood to be" [S5].

**Essence.** Karp treats his quarterly shareholder letters as a venue for moral and civilizational argument rather than financial reporting, describing them as "a meandering proclamation of things we believe to be true" written for readers he calls "intellectually curious and intelligent" [S2]. He frames deterrence, not diplomacy, as the active ingredient in foreign policy, arguing "where you have technological parity but moral disparity, the actual disparity is much greater than people think" [S3]. He also draws a hard line between a company's internal politics and its work, saying employees need not "agree with me" on Ukraine, ICE, or Israel even as he describes his own workforce as "radical leftists skeptical of institutional power" [S2].

**Philosophy.** *"We, the heretics, this motley band of characters, were cast out and nearly discarded by Silicon Valley."* [S1]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Judge a company by what it still does when doing it is unfashionable, not by its stated values — he points to Palantir's military work continuing "when such a commitment is fashionable and convenient, and when it is not" [S1].
2. Treat deterrence as doing real work that talk cannot: "we need to talk less and act more" [S3].
3. Separate a technical problem from an incentive problem — on AI, he argues the real issue is "aligning the goals of the companies building that technology with the interests of the customers using it," not a model's intentions [S5].
4. Write to persuade a specific reader, not to satisfy a disclosure requirement — his letters are aimed at people he judges "intellectually curious and intelligent" [S2].

**What he asks**
- Whether an AI company's incentives are actually aligned with the customer paying for it, or only with the company's own growth [S5].
- Whether a workforce's internal disagreement with leadership changes what the company is willing to build and ship [S2].
- Whether a stated commitment holds up once it stops being convenient or fashionable [S1].

**What he pushes back on**
- The idea that nuclear deterrence still functions as a check on adversaries, since "the West is very unlikely to use anything like a nuclear bomb, whereas our adversaries might" [S3].
- Framing AI's "alignment problem" as a purely technical question about a model's goals rather than a question of whose interests the company behind it serves [S5].
- AI developers he says are "exploit[ing] the value and alpha" of the customers whose data trains and runs through their systems — he says Palantir has "zero interest" in that approach [S5].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Whose interest does this product actually serve once it leaves your building — yours, or the customer running it?
   - Push until: a named customer outcome the product's incentives are actually built around, not a restated mission statement.
   - Red flags: "alignment" described only as a technical safety property; no customer or use case named.
2. What is this company willing to do that costs it something, and when did it last actually do it?
   - Push until: one specific decision that cost real revenue, goodwill, or talent, with the cost stated plainly.
   - Red flags: a values statement with no instance attached; "we'd do it if it came up."
3. Where do you have real parity with a competitor in capability, but a gap in resolve or legitimacy — and which side does that gap favor?
   - Push until: the specific capability named, and a plain statement of who the resulting gap actually favors.
   - Red flags: refusing to name the competitor; asserting moral superiority with no comparison point offered.
4. When your own people disagree with a hard call you've made, what do you actually require of them?
   - Push until: a concrete boundary — what they must still do despite disagreeing, and what room they keep to say so.
   - Red flags: claiming universal agreement exists; describing dissent as tolerated while quietly punishing it.
5. Is what you're describing a technical problem, or is it actually a question of whose interests the system serves?
   - Push until: the two are separated explicitly, and he states which one is the one actually unsolved.
   - Red flags: collapsing both into "we need better alignment" without saying whose interest is misaligned with whose.
6. If you stopped talking about this risk and had to act on it this week, what would you actually do?
   - Push until: one concrete action already taken or scheduled this week, not a statement or a future review.
   - Red flags: "we're monitoring the situation"; a memo or position paper offered as if it were the action.
