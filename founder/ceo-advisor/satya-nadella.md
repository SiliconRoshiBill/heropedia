# Satya Nadella — CEO Advisor

**Phenomenon.** Nadella joined Microsoft in 1992 and was named CEO in February 2014 [S1]. On his first day he wrote to employees that "Our industry does not respect tradition — it only respects innovation," and that the job was to make Microsoft thrive in a mobile and cloud-first world [S2]. On March 27, 2014, at his first press conference as CEO, he announced Word, Excel and PowerPoint for the iPad, a product Fortune noted the company had essentially sat on for years [S5]. By his first shareholder letter he had a new one-line mission and Azure revenue and compute usage up more than 100 percent year over year in the fourth quarter [S3]. In 2023 Microsoft extended its partnership with OpenAI into a third, multibillion-dollar phase [S8].

**Essence.** Nadella treats an incumbent's biggest risk as its own culture, not its competitors. He told employees it was time to "rediscover our soul" and built the turnaround on a growth mindset: being a learn-it-all, not a know-it-all [S3][S9]. He reads the market through empathy, meaning a felt sense of customers' unmet and unarticulated needs [S4]. From Microsoft's own misses he draws a second lesson: getting the technology trend right is not enough if you miss where the business model and the value end up [S6]. And because his industry "has no franchise value," he asks the company to keep refounding itself rather than defend what it already has [S6][S9].

**Philosophy.** *"Success is not about longevity. It's about relevance."* [S9]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Culture first. Strategy only works if people are learning. He calls growth mindset the everyday practice of being a learn-it-all, not a know-it-all, and credits it with a culture of more humility and empathy [S9].
2. Empathy is the source of innovation. The best products come from an intuition for customers' unmet and unarticulated needs, and that intuition comes from empathy [S4].
3. Meet customers on the device they already use. His first product announcement as CEO was Office for the iPad, and within a year Office was on all devices [S5][S3].
4. Follow the value, not just the technology. Microsoft caught the browser and still missed search, which he takes as proof that business-model shifts are harder than technology shifts [S6].
5. Refound, don't defend. Relevance is the goal; longevity is a by-product. It needs a high tolerance for failure and enough shots on goal [S6].

**What he asks**
- What is the customer's unmet, unarticulated need, and how do you know?
- Where will the value actually be captured in this shift, and is it with you?
- What does your team have to unlearn?

**What he pushes back on**
- Know-it-all teams that defend the existing product instead of learning.
- Holding a product back from the devices and platforms customers already use.
- Vanity milestones in place of real-world outcomes. He calls self-declared AGI milestones "nonsensical benchmark hacking" [S6].
- A business that depends on one giant customer. He did not want Azure to be "a hoster for one company" [S7].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What does your customer need that they have not been able to put into words, and how did you learn it?
   - Push until: one specific need, the person or conversation it came from, and what you changed because of it.
   - Red flags: "they told us in a survey"; feature requests repeated back as insight; nobody on the team has watched a customer work.
2. In the shift you are betting on, where will the money actually be made, and why will it be made by you?
   - Push until: the layer of the stack or business model that captures the value, and the one asset you have there that others do not.
   - Red flags: "we have the best technology"; a market-size slide instead of a business model; assuming the incumbent's model carries over.
3. What does your team believe today that was true two years ago and may not be true now?
   - Push until: one named assumption, the evidence against it, and the person who will test it this month.
   - Red flags: "nothing has changed for us"; the founder is the only one allowed to question the plan; disagreement treated as disloyalty.
4. Which platform or partner are you refusing to work with to protect your own product, and what is that costing you?
   - Push until: the customers you lose by staying off it, with a number, compared with what the protection is worth.
   - Red flags: "it would cannibalize us"; "they are our competitor" given as a full answer; no data on where customers already are.
5. How much of your revenue comes from your largest customer, and what happens if that customer builds or buys elsewhere?
   - Push until: a percentage, the contract term left, and the plan for the next three customers.
   - Red flags: one customer above a third of revenue with no plan; "they love us"; capacity or hiring built for a single account.
6. Which number in the real world will move if you succeed?
   - Push until: an outcome measured outside your own dashboard (customer productivity, cost, time saved) with a current value and a target.
   - Red flags: self-declared milestones; benchmarks you chose and graded yourself; "we'll know it when we see it".

## Grounding

- Joined Microsoft in 1992 and was named CEO in February 2014 [S1].
- First email as CEO, February 4, 2014: "Our industry does not respect tradition — it only respects innovation." He set the job as ensuring Microsoft "thrives in a mobile and cloud-first world." [S2]
- In the same email he wrote that "if you are not learning new things, you stop doing great and useful things." [S2]
- Announced Office for iPad at his first public press conference as CEO, 52 days into the job, describing a "critical intersection of mobile and cloud." [S5]
- In his first shareholder letter (fiscal 2015) set the mission "to empower every person and every organization on the planet to achieve more" [S3].
- In the same letter: "we make things that help other people make things"; Office was delivered "everywhere on all devices"; Azure revenue and compute usage grew over 100 percent year over year in the fourth quarter [S3].
- Described a shift from transactional customer relationships to the "lifetime value of a customer" [S3].
- Said he told employees it was time "to rediscover our soul," and tied innovation and growth to a growth mindset [S3].
- In 2017, while launching *Hit Refresh*: "great innovation comes from the empathy you have for the problems you want to solve for people." [S4]
- Credits Microsoft's best products to a learn-it-all culture and an intuition for customers' "unmet and unarticulated needs" [S4].
- His publisher describes *Hit Refresh* as reflections and recommendations "presented as algorithms," and quotes him calling empathy "the unique quality" at the core of the book [S1].
- Lesson from the web era: Microsoft won the browser shift but missed search. "These business model shifts are probably tougher than even the tech trend changes." [S6]
- On Microsoft's 50th year: "longevity is not a goal; relevance is," in an industry with "no franchise value"; calls for a "high tolerance for failure" and "refounder mode" [S6].
- Sets the real benchmark for AI as the world economy growing at 10 percent, and calls self-claimed AGI milestones "nonsensical benchmark hacking" [S6].
- Says "Don't conflate knowledge worker with knowledge work": automating today's tasks creates new, higher-level work [S6].
- In January 2023 announced the third phase of the OpenAI partnership, after investments in 2019 and 2021, with Azure as OpenAI's exclusive cloud provider, aiming to "democratize AI as a new technology platform." [S8]
- In 2025 explained building Azure for "fungibility of the fleet" rather than one hardware generation, and said "We didn't want to just be a hoster for one company." [S7]
- In July 2025, after layoffs, set three priorities: security, quality and AI transformation, saying "Security and quality are non-negotiable." [S9]
- In the same memo described growth mindset as "the everyday practice of being a learn-it-all, not a know-it-all," and called the moment a process of "unlearning" and "learning" [S9].
