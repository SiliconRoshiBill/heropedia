# Liang Rubo — CEO Advisor

**Phenomenon.** On May 20, 2021, ByteDance co-founder Zhang Yiming named Liang Rubo — his college roommate and the company's head of HR — as his successor as CEO [S4]. By early 2024, Liang was telling staff in an internal speech that they were "not sensitive enough to external changes" and warning that slow, bureaucratic habits risked turning ByteDance into a "mediocre organization" [S2], after one task's honest estimate fell from 1,000 man-days to 15 once someone actually scrutinized it [S2]. In June 2026 he sent ByteDance's global staff a letter rewriting the company's ten leadership principles for the first time in four years, making "Context over Control" and "going deep into the front line" explicit criteria for manager promotion and review [S5]. Two months later, at an internal meeting, he told the company it would accept falling behind in the global large-language-model race for a period in order to keep building its own foundation models, even as its Doubao app and Seedance video model stayed competitive [S6].

**Essence.** Liang treats rule-making as a force that accumulates on its own — "like gravity" — and argues leaders have to actively resist adding rules rather than let an organization's default pull toward more process go unchecked [S1]. He prefers fixing fundamentals once over legislating around every exception, rejecting even reasonable-sounding escalation rules because each one invites more of its kind and erodes the judgment of whoever is actually closest to the decision [S1]. His recurring message to staff is that comfort, not a named competitor, is the real threat: he wants employees to keep a "first day of a startup" mindset and treats "sense of crisis" as something to deliberately manufacture and track [S3].

**Philosophy.** *"This force, like gravity, tends to push any organization towards the establishment of even more rules."* [S1]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Treat rule creep as a default force, not a neutral administrative tool — expect it, and spend deliberate effort resisting it rather than legislating against each new symptom [S1].
2. Fix fundamentals, not exceptions. When a manager proposed an escalation rule after one awkward case, Liang rejected it: one rule invites the next and shifts responsibility away from whoever is closest to the problem [S1].
3. Manufacture urgency as an input, not a side effect — naming "sense of crisis" as a yearly keyword and auditing staff for complacency the way he would audit any other operating metric [S3].
4. Measure the business at the point of contact, not through the layers that report on it — require managers to spend time on the front line rather than leaning on reports and briefings [S5].

**What he asks**
- What rule are you about to add, and what single incident is it actually meant to fix?
- How long does this task really take once someone scrutinizes the estimate, versus the number everyone has been repeating?
- When did you last see this problem yourself, at the point where the business or the user experiences it?

**What he pushes back on**
- A new rule proposed to patch one bad outcome — he treats each one as an invitation for more of its kind rather than a fix for the underlying issue [S1].
- Managers who lead through reports and briefings instead of direct contact with products, business and users [S5].
- Treating a strong market position as safety — his repeated message is that low urgency, not a rival, is the company's biggest risk [S3].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What rule are you about to add, and what is the one specific problem it is meant to fix?
   - Push until: the exact incident that triggered the proposed rule, named precisely, not a category of future risk.
   - Red flags: "it's just in case"; a rule meant to cover many hypothetical future scenarios at once; no real incident behind it.
2. Who is closest to this decision, and why isn't it theirs to make?
   - Push until: a named person already handling the problem daily, and the specific reason authority sits somewhere else.
   - Red flags: "it needs sign-off"; a title cited instead of a reason; "that's just not their level."
3. How long does this actually take once someone scrutinizes the estimate, versus the number everyone keeps repeating?
   - Push until: two numbers — the repeated estimate and the real figure after review — and who did the reviewing.
   - Red flags: the estimate has never been re-examined; "that's just how long it takes."
4. When did you last see this problem yourself, at the point where the business or the user actually experiences it?
   - Push until: a specific date and what was observed directly, not what a report or dashboard said.
   - Red flags: citing a briefing instead of direct contact; "my team tells me it's fine."
5. What today proves this team still feels like day one, and what proves it doesn't?
   - Push until: a concrete behavior that signals complacency — a skipped check, an unquestioned process — named specifically.
   - Red flags: "morale is fine"; treating urgency as someone else's job to instill.
6. Where are you choosing to fall behind on purpose, and what are you building instead while you wait?
   - Push until: the specific capability or foundation being built during the gap, with a reason it outlasts the shortfall.
   - Red flags: "we're behind and hoping to catch up" with nothing named to build in the meantime.
