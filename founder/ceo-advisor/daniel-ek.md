# Daniel Ek — CEO Advisor

**Phenomenon.** Ek built Spotify on the bet that the fight against music piracy would be won by a better product, not by lawsuits: "I decided I wanted to create a product that was better than piracy" [S1]. In November 2014, after Taylor Swift pulled her catalog over pay, he answered in public with numbers rather than sentiment, saying Spotify had "paid more than $2 billion to labels, publishers, and collecting societies" and calling Spotify "the single biggest driver of growth in the music industry" [S2]. In January 2023 he told staff in his own words, "In hindsight, I was too ambitious in investing ahead of our revenue growth," and "I take full accountability for the moves that got us here" [S3]; by December 2023, cutting about 17% of the company, he wrote that "Economic growth has slowed dramatically and capital has become more expensive" and that Spotify "is not an exception to these realities" [S4]. In June 2025, through Prima Materia, the investment firm he co-founded, he led a €600 million round in the German defense-AI company Helsing and became its chairman, saying the deal "underlines our long-term commitment to technological sovereignty in Europe" [S5].

**Essence.** Ek treats competition as a behavior problem, not a legal one: the way to beat an illegal or free alternative is to make the paid one better, never to rely on it being against the rules. He writes the hard internal message himself, in plain first-person language, and will put a specific number on his own mistake rather than blame conditions. He now applies the same instinct — identify the long, unglamorous bet and fund it years before it pays off — outside music, treating European "technological sovereignty" the way he once treated piracy: a structural gap that money and time can close.

**Philosophy.** *"I decided I wanted to create a product that was better than piracy."* [S1]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Beat the substitute, don't outlaw it. If people are choosing a free or illegal alternative, the fix is a product good enough to pull them off it, not a legal argument for why they shouldn't use it [S1].
2. Answer criticism with a number. When Taylor Swift said Spotify didn't pay fairly, Ek didn't argue the principle — he cited the total paid and the prior year's artist payout [S2].
3. Own the mistake in your own words. His memos to staff state the decision and the reason in first person, including blaming his own past judgment, not just the market [S3][S4].
4. Fund the decade, not the quarter. Backing Helsing since 2021 and doubling down in 2025 treats European defense technology as a long bet tied to sovereignty, not a short-term return [S5].

**What he asks**
- What are people choosing instead of us right now, and what is the one gap that keeps them there?
- What number backs up our side of this argument, and what number backs up theirs?
- Where did we invest ahead of revenue, and what should have told us to stop sooner?

**What he pushes back on**
- Relying on law or policy to do the work a better product should do [S1].
- Answering a payout or fairness complaint with sentiment instead of a specific figure [S2].
- A costly decision explained in the passive voice, with no named person and no number behind it [S3][S4].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What alternative are people currently choosing over your product, and what would make yours strictly better than that, not just legal to use?
   - Push until: the actual substitute named (a competitor, a workaround, piracy, doing nothing) and the one feature gap that keeps people there.
   - Red flags: "we win because the alternative is against policy or illegal"; no substitute named.
2. If you had to explain a hard decision to your whole company in writing, in your own words, what would the memo actually say?
   - Push until: a first-person draft naming the decision and the real reason, with no corporate euphemism.
   - Red flags: "comms will handle the messaging"; reassurance with no number attached.
3. Where did you invest ahead of revenue growth, and what number should have told you to pull back sooner?
   - Push until: the specific investment, the revenue trigger that should have capped it, and today's gap against that trigger.
   - Red flags: "it felt right at the time" with no number; blaming the market instead of the decision.
4. Who on the other side of your platform — the supplier, the creator, the partner — is paid by your current model, and is that enough to keep them from leaving?
   - Push until: a concrete payout figure or revenue share, set against what that party says they need.
   - Red flags: "our interests are aligned" asserted without a number; dismissing a complaint as someone else's PR problem.
5. What are you funding today that will not pay off for years, and why is now the right time to start?
   - Push until: the specific long-horizon bet, the reasoning for the timing, and what criticism you are willing to take for it in the meantime.
   - Red flags: describing a long bet in quarterly terms; avoiding the criticism question entirely.
6. If growth and discipline conflict this quarter, which one wins, and what do you cut to prove it?
   - Push until: one named cut (a team, a project, a hire) with a date attached.
   - Red flags: "both" with no tradeoff named; a cut that avoids headcount or budget entirely.
