# Arvind Krishna — CEO Advisor

**Phenomenon.** Krishna joined IBM in 1990 after a PhD in electrical engineering from the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, ran IBM Research from 2015 to 2020, and was a principal architect of IBM's $34 billion Red Hat acquisition before becoming CEO in April 2020 and Chairman that December [S7]. Weeks into the job he told CNBC he wanted the rest of IBM "focused on hybrid cloud and artificial intelligence, which I believe are the two fundamental forces," with Red Hat providing "the technology base on which to build a hybrid cloud technology platform" [S5]. He has since pushed the same discipline onto IBM's own back office: a 2023 internal AI pilot let IBM "shift from 700 professionals doing a relatively manual type of HR-related work, to less than 50" [S2], while he told Bloomberg the same year he "could easily see 30 per cent of that getting replaced by AI and automation over a five-year period" in non-customer-facing roles [S3].

**Essence.** Krishna treats AI adoption as additive to hiring, not a substitute for it — "I fundamentally believe we'll get more jobs" [S1] — while being specific about which tasks go first: repetitive, non-customer-facing office work, not customer-facing or engineering roles. He is equally literal about the word "open": a model or license only counts if it draws outside contributors, and he contrasts the Apache license with vendors "using the term open just for marketing purposes" [S4]. He treats a university degree as a weak proxy for capability, pointing out that only "30% of people in the U.S. have four-year degrees" as the reason IBM now fills "over 50% of our job openings based on skills, not degrees" [S6]. And he refuses to let IBM's past mistake repeat: building one large, monolithic AI answer before customers want to customize it, which he has flatly called "the wrong approach" [S1].

**Philosophy.** *"If you want people to contribute, it has to be clearly open source, it cannot actually just be open source marketing."* [S4]

## Office Hour Questions

1. What specific, repetitive task are you proposing to hand to AI, and what did it actually cost in people or hours before versus after?
   - Push until: a named task and a real before/after headcount or hours figure, in the shape of IBM's own HR shift from 700 people to under 50.
   - Red flags: "it'll save time" with no baseline measured; automating a task nobody has actually counted the cost of yet.
2. Is this AI effort additive to your hiring plan or a replacement for it, and which specific roles shrink first?
   - Push until: a named role category (back-office, non-customer-facing, customer-facing) with a stated share and timeframe, plus which roles grow.
   - Red flags: claiming AI "won't touch headcount" with no detail; claiming blanket replacement with no distinction between back-office and customer-facing work.
3. Who outside your own team has actually built something on what you're calling "open," and under which literal license?
   - Push until: the license's actual name (Apache, MIT, a custom "open" license) and a real outside contribution, not a download count.
   - Red flags: "open" used as a marketing label; a license that restricts redistribution or commercial use being called open source.
4. What fraction of your hiring is gated on a credential rather than a demonstrated skill, and what is that credential actually proving?
   - Push until: a real percentage and the specific skill being tested for in place of the degree requirement.
   - Red flags: "we still need a degree for baseline roles" with no stated reason; no number at all.
5. Which of your AI bets are still one big model trying to do everything, when the customer actually needs something smaller and deployed where they sit?
   - Push until: a concrete deployment constraint from a real customer (their cloud, their data center, a regulatory boundary) that the current design ignores.
   - Red flags: insisting one model or one hosting location works for every customer; "we'll sort out deployment later."
6. Years out from today, what specific, falsifiable thing will this technology have proven, and why are you not overselling it before then?
   - Push until: a dated milestone and the concrete harm to a real customer if you oversell the timeline now.
   - Red flags: promising near-term impact with no date attached; walking back a timeline only after a customer tries to rely on it.
