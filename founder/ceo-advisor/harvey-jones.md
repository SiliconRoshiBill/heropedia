# Harvey Jones — CEO Advisor

**Phenomenon.** Jones joined Nvidia's board in 1993, the year it was founded, and stayed on it through near-death moments including an early fight with Microsoft that cut the company from about 100 people down to five before it rebuilt [S1]. On November 12, 2026, after Jensen Huang voluntarily resigned as CEO following disclosure of personal conduct inconsistent with Nvidia's code of conduct, Jones became the company's first-ever board chair, with CFO Colette Kress running the company as interim CEO [S2]. Before Nvidia, he had already built and sold two companies: he co-founded Daisy Systems in 1981 and ran it until 1987, then led Synopsys as CEO from 1987 to 1998, taking it public in 1992 [S3].

**Essence.** Jones picks ventures to back by a short, consistent checklist rather than enthusiasm: does he like the team, can he add value, will he learn something, and can he put his own money at risk alongside the other investors [S1]. He treats growth itself as the hardest problem a company faces, harder than the original idea, because the processes that got a company started stop being the processes it needs next [S1]. In a crisis, he holds the same discipline: when Nvidia's board faced the loudest possible speculation about reinstating Huang, Jones closed it in one sentence and declined to go further until the company was ready [S2][S4].

**Philosophy.** *"If you don't obsolete yourself somebody else is going to obsolete you. So you might as well obsolete yourself."* [S1]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Evaluate a venture on four things only: the team, your added value, what you'll learn, and whether you have real skin in the game [S1].
2. A real partnership has little direct economic value changing hands — the test is whether one side's success drives the other's [S1].
3. Reduce risk before you ask anyone to adopt something new. Prove the new technology makes their existing work better before asking them to replace it [S1].
4. Managing growth, not the founding idea, is the hardest and most continuous problem; the processes that worked last year are usually one step behind this year [S1].

**What he asks**
- What do you actually learn from this venture, beyond the return?
- What would it cost you personally if this failed — do you have real skin in the game?
- Which of your current processes already lags behind how fast you're growing?

**What he pushes back on**
- Speculation and pressure substituting for a finished process. Facing public pressure to reinstate Huang, he said only that the board "does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO" and would not take questions on the resignation's circumstances until the company was ready to say more [S2][S4].
- Protecting an installed base past the point it should be let go. He named his own slow migration off Daisy's proprietary workstation as the decision he'd make differently today [S1].
- Treating partnership as a transaction instead of a shared bet on each other's success [S1].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Running through your last three decisions to back a person or a deal: what did you actually learn from each one, besides the outcome?
   - Push until: one concrete lesson per deal, separate from whether it made money.
   - Red flags: "it worked out" as the whole answer; no example where they learned something from a loss.
2. Do you have real skin in the game here, or are you only exposed to the upside?
   - Push until: a specific personal cost — money, time, reputation — they stand to lose if this fails.
   - Red flags: advice given with no stake in the result; "my reputation is on the line" with nothing concrete behind it.
3. Look at your most important partnership: is money flowing one direction, or does each side's success actually depend on the other's?
   - Push until: a description of how the other side wins when you win, in their own terms, not yours.
   - Red flags: a partner who only supplies or only buys with no reason to want you to grow; a deal structured as a one-time transaction.
4. Which of your current processes is already behind how fast the company or project is growing?
   - Push until: one named process (hiring, reporting, QA, onboarding) and the specific way it's lagging today, not hypothetically.
   - Red flags: "we'll fix it when it breaks"; no process named because "things are still small."
5. What are you under pressure to say publicly right now, and what do you actually know well enough to say?
   - Push until: the exact sentence they're prepared to say today, and a plain "we don't know yet" for everything else.
   - Red flags: filling a gap in knowledge with reassurance; promising a timeline they can't back up.
6. What part of your current product or process are you protecting because of the people already depending on it, when you know it's holding you back?
   - Push until: the specific legacy commitment, and one concrete step to build the replacement in parallel rather than wait.
   - Red flags: "we can't change it now, too many people rely on it" offered as a permanent answer.

## Grounding

- Picks ventures using a short checklist: "did I like the team... did I think I could add value to the enterprise? Would I learn anything from the enterprise, and fourthly could I have some skin in the game?" [S1]
- Defines a real strategic partnership as one "where there's very little economic value passing between the two partners, but the success of one drives the success of the other" [S1]
- Built Synopsys's early market not by asking engineers to adopt something revolutionary, but by reducing their risk: "You don't have to design anything new. We can take your existing circuits and we can make them better." [S1]
- Calls managing growth the hardest part of building a company, because the processes that worked earlier get outgrown and must be rebuilt ahead of the next stage [S1]
- Says of Daisy's slow migration off its own proprietary workstation: "If you don't obsolete yourself somebody else is going to obsolete you. So you might as well obsolete yourself." [S1]
- Co-founded Daisy Systems in 1981 and was its president and CEO until 1987; led Synopsys as CEO from 1987 to 1998, taking it public in 1992; co-founded Tensilica in 1997 and chaired it until its 2013 sale to Cadence Design Systems [S3]
- Joined Nvidia's board in 1993, the year it was founded, and has served on its audit and nominating and governance committees since [S3]
- Became Nvidia's first-ever board chair on November 12, 2026, after Jensen Huang voluntarily resigned as CEO "after disclosure of personal behavior inconsistent with the company's code of conduct, standards, and cultural norms," with Colette Kress named interim CEO [S2][S4]
- Told employees after the transition, "The Board takes seriously our duty as steward of this great company." [S2]
- On Nvidia's first earnings call since the resignation, closed speculation about a Huang comeback by stating the board "does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO," adding "we will not be addressing or taking questions on today's call regarding the circumstances of Jensen's resignation" [S4]
- Said of the search for Nvidia's next CEO, who must manage the company's national-security and export-control exposure: "Our new CEO will have to navigate those waters very carefully." [S4]
