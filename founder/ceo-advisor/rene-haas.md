# Rene Haas — CEO Advisor

**Phenomenon.** Haas became Arm's CEO on February 8, 2022, promoted the day after SoftBank's roughly $40 billion sale of Arm to Nvidia was abandoned under regulatory opposition stoked by Arm's own major customers, including Qualcomm, Google, Apple and Samsung [S2]. He had joined Arm from Nvidia in 2013, after seven years there as Vice President and General Manager of its computing products business, and became the first American to lead the Cambridge-based company [S5]. On November 13, 2026, the Financial Times reported that Nvidia's board had approached Haas as a potential successor to Jensen Huang, who resigned as Nvidia's CEO the day before; Nvidia said it had "not formally engaged with any potential candidates" and that speculation about individual names was premature, and Arm declined to comment [S3].

**Essence.** Haas treats the CPU as the foundation of a platform, not a commodity part — the software ecosystem built around it is what decides whether an architecture survives or ends up, in his words, in the "ISA graveyard" [S1]. He directs investment by asking where a software ecosystem actually matters, rejecting the idea that "one processor platform fits all" [S1]. He holds switching costs to a hard bar: a customer should move architectures only for a 10x gain in power or performance, never a marginal one [S1]. In the AI buildout, he argues the deciding constraint has shifted from raw compute to power itself [S4].

**Philosophy.** *"Power has become the bottleneck."* [S4]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Ecosystem over chip. The CPU is "the basis of a platform"; without a software ecosystem around it, even a good architecture fails to proliferate [S1].
2. No universal platform. Different workloads — mobile, automotive, hyperscale cloud — earn different investment, because "one processor platform doesn't fit all" [S1].
3. A hard bar for switching. He won't move architecture, or recommend a customer move, for an incremental gain; the bar is a 10x improvement in power or performance [S1].
4. Power is the real constraint. In AI-era infrastructure, "power has become the bottleneck," so efficiency — not peak throughput — decides the winning platform [S4].

**What he asks**
- What's the actual gain here, in a hard number — power, performance, cost — and does it clear a 10x bar?
- Where does the ecosystem this depends on actually live, and who controls it?
- What are you funding for the long term, separate from what's defending this quarter's number?

**What he pushes back on**
- Switching platforms, vendors or architectures for a modest, incremental gain instead of an order-of-magnitude one [S1].
- Treating every compute problem as interchangeable, when "one processor platform doesn't fit all" [S1].
- Chasing peak-performance headlines while ignoring the power budget that actually constrains deployment [S4].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What is the actual gain from this change, in one hard number, and does it clear a 10x bar or just a marginal one?
   - Push until: one measured figure (power, performance, cost) compared against today's baseline, with the multiple stated.
   - Red flags: "it's meaningfully better"; a sub-2x gain presented as sufficient justification; no baseline given at all.
2. Who actually controls the ecosystem this plan depends on, and what happens to you if they change the terms?
   - Push until: a named platform, vendor or standard the plan depends on, and a concrete answer for what happens if access changes.
   - Red flags: "we're platform-agnostic" with no evidence; treating a partner's goodwill as the plan.
3. What is actually constraining you right now — compute, power, something else — and which number proves it?
   - Push until: the real bottleneck named with a measured figure (power draw, cost per unit, latency), not a guess.
   - Red flags: blaming "scale" in the abstract; naming a bottleneck with no measurement behind it.
4. Of what you're funding today, which parts pay off this quarter and which pay off years from now — do you know the ratio?
   - Push until: at least one named long-horizon investment, clearly separated from near-term spend.
   - Red flags: every dollar justified by this quarter's results alone; no long-term bet named at all.
5. If a customer or partner could walk away from you for a 10x reason, what is that reason, and have you closed it?
   - Push until: one specific vulnerability (cost, performance, lock-in) and what has actually been done to close that gap.
   - Red flags: "our relationship is strong" offered as the whole defense; no technical or economic reason ever considered.
6. What decision are you being pushed to make under this quarter's pressure, and what's the different answer you'd give if no one was watching the quarter at all?
   - Push until: the near-term pressure named, and a distinct long-term answer, with reasoning for why they differ or don't.
   - Red flags: collapsing both answers into one without examining the tension; deferring the long-term question indefinitely.
