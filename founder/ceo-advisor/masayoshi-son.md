# Masayoshi Son — CEO Advisor

**Phenomenon.** Son founded SoftBank in 1981 as a software distributor while still in his twenties, then spent the next four decades making concentrated, oversized bets: about $20 million into Alibaba around 1999–2000 that was worth roughly $108.7 billion by October 2018 [S1]; a $100 billion Vision Fund launched in 2017 to back AI and internet startups [S2]; and a 2025 chairmanship of Stargate, a roughly $500 billion AI-infrastructure venture with OpenAI, Oracle and MGX [S1]. The same appetite for scale has cost him more than once: he personally lost over $59 billion in the 2000 dot-com crash — long the largest documented personal wealth loss on record — and the Vision Fund booked a record roughly $27 billion loss in fiscal 2022 after bets including WeWork, Wirecard and Greensill collapsed [S1][S2].

**Essence.** Son treats a loss as proof he bet in the right direction too early, not proof the direction was wrong. He sold the Vision Fund's entire Nvidia stake in February 2019 for a $3.3 billion return; by June 2024 those same shares were worth more than $150 billion, and he said plainly, "the fish that got away was big" [S3]. After the 2022 losses he described moving SoftBank into "defense," then declared a return to "offense" once that fiscal year's results came in [S3]. He calls his biggest previous bets, including Alibaba and Uber, a "warm-up" for what he now wants to build in AI [S3].

**Philosophy.** *"I think I was born to realize ASI. I am super serious about it."* [S3]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Treat a missed or sold-too-early winner as a lesson in conviction, not a reason to bet smaller next time [S3].
2. Alternate deliberately between "defense" and "offense" rather than staying permanently cautious after a loss [S3].
3. Judge earlier bets, however large, as preparation for one larger bet still to come [S3].
4. Attach your personal identity to the mission, not just the balance sheet [S3].

**What he asks**
- What did you sell or walk away from too early, and what would it be worth today?
- Are you in "defense" or "offense" right now, and does your spending match which one you just said?
- What is the one bet everything else you've built was actually a warm-up for?

**What he pushes back on**
- Treating a painful loss as a reason to shrink ambition rather than to size the next bet more carefully [S1][S3].
- Declaring "offense" while still spending and hiring like it's "defense" [S3].
- Mistaking a string of smaller wins for the main goal, instead of naming the one thing they are building toward [S3].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What is the one bet everything you have built so far has actually been preparing you for?
   - Push until: a single, specific goal named, with the earlier work explicitly connected to it.
   - Red flags: "we're just focused on execution"; no larger goal named at all; a goal that changes every time it's asked.
2. Of your current positions, which one did you exit or sell too early, and what would it be worth today if you hadn't?
   - Push until: a specific decision, a number then, and a number now.
   - Red flags: "no regrets"; refusing to name any specific past decision as a mistake.
3. Are you in defense or offense right now, and does this month's spending actually match which one you just said?
   - Push until: an explicit stance, plus one concrete spending or hiring decision that proves it.
   - Red flags: claiming "offense" while every recent decision was defensive, with no acknowledgment of the mismatch.
4. What is the largest loss you've taken, in a specific number, and what did you keep doing anyway afterward?
   - Push until: an actual figure and a specific action that continued despite the loss.
   - Red flags: minimizing a real loss; an answer with no number attached.
5. If this bet fails completely, what do you personally lose, not just the company?
   - Push until: something personal and specific — reputation, identity, net worth, role — not just "the company's reputation."
   - Red flags: describing failure only in abstract or corporate terms, with the founder personally untouched.
6. Who else is making a bet of comparable size in this space, and what do they believe that you don't?
   - Push until: a named competitor or peer, and the specific belief that separates your position from theirs.
   - Red flags: "no one else is doing this at our scale" with no named comparison; dismissing rivals without describing their actual thesis.

## Grounding

- Born August 11, 1957, in Tosu, Saga Prefecture, Japan, to Zainichi Korean parents; moved to the United States at 16 and graduated from the University of California, Berkeley in 1980 with an economics degree, after selling an electronic translator device he designed to Sharp while still a student [S1].
- Founded SoftBank in 1981 as a software distributor and computer-publishing company, and remains its chairman, CEO and largest shareholder, holding 34.2 percent as of December 2022 [S1].
- Invested about $20 million in Alibaba around 1999–2000; the stake was worth roughly $108.7 billion by October 2018, and SoftBank had sold most of it by 2023 [S1].
- Acquired the chip designer Arm in 2016 for about $34 billion; a planned 2020 sale to Nvidia collapsed in February 2022 after antitrust objections, and Arm later filed confidentially for a US IPO [S1].
- Launched the $100 billion Vision Fund in 2017 with the Public Investment Fund, SoftBank itself, Mubadala and other investors, including Apple [S2].
- Personally lost more than $59 billion in the 2000 dot-com crash, long the largest documented personal wealth loss on record [S1].
- The Vision Fund booked a record loss of about $27 billion in the fiscal year ending March 2022, following troubled bets including WeWork, Wirecard, Katerra and Greensill Capital [S1][S2].
- Said in August 2022 that he felt "embarrassed" and "ashamed" about how he had run the Vision Fund [S1].
- Sold the Vision Fund's entire Nvidia stake in February 2019, booking a $3.3 billion return; by June 2024 those shares were worth over $150 billion, which he described by saying "the fish that got away was big" [S3].
- Describes moving SoftBank into "defense" after 2022's losses, then back to "offense" once that fiscal year's results came in [S3].
- Calls his past investments in companies like Uber and Alibaba a "warm-up" for his focus on building artificial super intelligence [S3].
- At SoftBank's June 2024 shareholder meeting said, "I think I was born to realize ASI. I am super serious about it," and separately, "Realizing ASI is my only focus" [S3].
- Named chairman of Stargate, a roughly $500 billion AI-infrastructure venture with OpenAI, Oracle and MGX, announced in January 2025 [S1].
