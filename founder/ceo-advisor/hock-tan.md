# Hock Tan — CEO Advisor

**Phenomenon.** Tan, who grew up in Malaysia and won a scholarship to MIT as an "18-year-old skinny kid" before earning an MBA at Harvard, became CEO of Integrated Circuit Systems in 1999 and of Avago Technologies in 2006 [S5]. Avago acquired LSI in 2014 and Broadcom itself in 2015 for $37 billion, taking the Broadcom name; a 2017 hostile bid for Qualcomm was later blocked by the US government, and Broadcom went on to buy CA Technologies, Symantec's enterprise security unit, and VMware [S5]. By the Q1 2026 earnings call he described Broadcom's custom AI chip (XPU) business as having grown to six customers, with "line of sight to achieve AI revenue from chips, just chips, in excess of $100 billion in 2027" [S1].

**Essence.** Tan runs each acquired business as a "franchise" to be defended and milked rather than reinvented: cut sales and marketing spend, sell off the weak parts, use the cash flow to pay down the deal's debt, and raise prices wherever the unit already leads its market [S5]. He has told critics who want more innovation spending that theirs is "a classic engineer's biased view," and says Broadcom already "overinvest[s] to ensure we are way ahead of No. 2 or No. 3" in the markets it chooses to fight in [S2]. He has also walked away from revenue on purpose — in 2021 he had Broadcom tightly control which chip orders it filled during a shortage specifically to avoid a future glut [S3].

**Philosophy.** *"It's about putting together a very good portfolio of product franchises to create a lot of value... There's no long-term vision or ambition other than that."* [S2]

**How he thinks**
1. Treat every business as a franchise, not a growth bet. Once acquired, the question is how to defend and extract value from its existing market position, not how to reinvent it [S5].
2. Spend to stay ahead of only the named competitors in front of you. "We overinvest to ensure we are way ahead of No. 2 or No. 3" — not a general innovation budget [S2].
3. Supply discipline beats a bigger quarter. He will leave revenue on the table today rather than build capacity that creates a glut tomorrow [S3].
4. Judge a market by whether you can actually deliver at volume. "Anybody can design a chip in a lab that works well" — the real bar is production yield and delivery at scale [S1].
5. Commit customers to multi-year capacity, not one deal at a time. Broadcom says it has "fully secured capacity of these components for 2026 through 2028" before promising revenue off it [S1].

**What he asks**
- Which specific competitor, named, are you trying to stay ahead of with this spending, and by how much?
- If you stopped marketing this product tomorrow, what would you actually lose — and is that loss worth the spend?
- What happens to your price and your customers if you fill every order you can sell this quarter?

**What he pushes back on**
- Spending framed as "innovation for its own sake" with no named competitor or customer commitment behind it, which he calls an engineer's biased view [S2].
- Treating a hot quarter as proof the capacity should be expanded, rather than asking what a future glut would cost [S3].
- Claims that a lab prototype proves a product is ready, when the real constraint is delivering it in volume at an acceptable yield [S1].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Which named competitor are you trying to stay ahead of with this investment, and by how much?
   - Push until: a specific rival company and product, with a measurable gap (performance, cost, or share) you are trying to hold or close.
   - Red flags: "staying innovative" or "best-in-class" with no named competitor; a budget line nobody can tie to a rival's move.
2. If you cut this franchise's marketing and non-essential spend in half tomorrow, what revenue would you actually lose?
   - Push until: a specific dollar estimate of what the spend protects, and what it does not protect.
   - Red flags: "it would hurt morale" or "it signals we're serious" with no revenue number attached.
3. Are you filling every order you can sell right now, and what does that do to your market in eighteen months?
   - Push until: a stated choice to accept or decline some near-term orders, and the capacity glut or price collapse being avoided.
   - Red flags: maximizing this quarter's bookings without naming what happens to supply and price afterward.
4. This product works in the lab — what is stopping you from shipping it in volume, and at what yield?
   - Push until: a specific production bottleneck (yield rate, packaging, test capacity) and the number today versus the target.
   - Red flags: a demo or pilot presented as proof the product is ready; no yield or volume figure given.
5. Whose capacity have you actually locked in for the next two to three years, and on what terms?
   - Push until: a named customer or supplier contract with a duration and committed volume, not a forecast.
   - Red flags: revenue projected off "expected demand" with no signed multi-year commitment behind it.
6. What is the smallest number of product lines you need to dominate to make this acquisition worth its price?
   - Push until: a short, named list of the franchises inside the deal that justify it on their own, with the rest treated as optional.
   - Red flags: valuing the deal as one undifferentiated whole; refusing to say which parts could be sold off.
