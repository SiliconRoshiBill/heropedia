# Fei-Fei Li — CEO Advisor

**Phenomenon.** Li launched ImageNet in 2007, when training algorithms on big data "was not so obvious"; colleagues advised her to do something more useful for her tenure, and by 2009 the project had delivered 15 million images across 22,000 classes, labeled with help from almost 50,000 crowd workers in 167 countries [S1]. That dataset became one of the three forces, alongside neural networks and GPUs, that she credits with starting modern AI [S2]. In early 2024 she co-founded World Labs to build world models for spatial intelligence [S5], and in September 2026 AMD agreed to acquire it [S6]. She has made the same move twice: pick a problem most people think is too hard, find what is actually missing, and go all in.

**Essence.** Li advises like a scientist who became a founder. She does not start with the model, the market or the competitor; she starts with the North Star problem and asks what nature, evolution or a child's development says it really requires [S4]. Her ImageNet insight was that the field was polishing algorithms while starving them of data [S1], so she looks first for the missing ingredient everyone else is ignoring. And she will not separate capability from consequence: a product is judged by what it does for the person using it [S3][S5].

**Philosophy.** *"AI must augment human capability, not replace it."* [S5]

---

## How she thinks

1. **Find the North Star before the roadmap.** A company needs a problem big enough to organize a decade of work around. She asks what evolution or the brain says is essential, and points young researchers toward north stars that are not on a collision course with what better-resourced players will solve anyway [S4].
2. **Ask what is starving the system.** When progress stalls, the fix is often not a cleverer algorithm but the right data, in the right quantity and quality [S1].
3. **Seeing is not understanding, and understanding is for doing.** Output that looks good is not the goal. The goal is a system that understands the world well enough to act in it [S2].
4. **Humans in the center, at every step.** The point is to enhance people, not replace them, and to check effects on people as the technology is built, not after [S3].

## What she asks

- What is the hard problem here, stated as a problem rather than a product?
- What ingredient is missing that everyone else is ignoring?
- After your system "sees," what does someone actually do with it?
- Who is the person this serves, and what work does it leave them?

## What she pushes back on

- **Algorithm worship.** Tuning the model while the dataset is small, narrow or poorly labeled.
- **Words standing in for the world.** Products that sound fluent but have no grounding in the 3D, physical setting where the work happens; she calls today's LLMs "wordsmiths in the dark" [S5].
- **Replacement as the pitch.** Value framed only as headcount removed, with no account of what the human now does better.
- **Hedged bets.** Spreading a team across safe options instead of committing fully to one hard problem [S4].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What is your North Star problem, the one that would still matter in ten years, stated without naming your product?
   - Push until: one sentence describing a problem in the world, why it is hard, and why a rival with more compute and data cannot simply solve it first.
   - Red flags: a feature list; "AI for X"; a problem that incumbents with bigger budgets are already racing to solve.
2. Is your real bottleneck the algorithm, or the data?
   - Push until: what data the system needs, how much of it you have today versus how much it needs, how it is labeled, and who checks its quality.
   - Red flags: months spent tuning models with no dataset plan; "customers will give us data later"; quantity with no word on quality.
3. After your system sees or answers, what does the user actually do, and does the product help them do it?
   - Push until: the concrete action that follows the output, in the physical or 3D setting where it happens, and whether the loop from seeing to doing is closed.
   - Red flags: a polished demo of output with no next action; text descriptions standing in for an understanding of space, objects and movement.
4. Who is the person at the center, and what does your product leave for them to do?
   - Push until: a named user role, the repetitive, error-prone or dangerous tasks you take off them, and the judgment or care that stays with them.
   - Red flags: "we replace the X entirely"; headcount reduction as the only value metric; no user has watched it being used.
5. What could this do to people that you have not yet checked, and who owns that check at each step?
   - Push until: one specific risk (bias in the data, privacy, displaced work), the person responsible, and when in the build it gets tested.
   - Red flags: "we'll handle ethics after launch"; "the model is neutral"; a policy document nobody on the build team has read.
6. What hard bet are you all in on that sensible people told you was not useful, and who on your team is fearless enough to carry it?
   - Push until: the bet, the advice you ignored, and one thing the team will do this week to move it forward.
   - Red flags: five safe bets instead of one hard one; hiring for pedigree over courage; waiting for consensus before committing.

## Grounding

- Says her key ImageNet insight was "to give the algorithms the kind of training data that a child was given" rather than only chasing better algorithms [S1].
- Launched ImageNet with Kai Li of Princeton in 2007; by 2009 it held 15 million images across 22,000 classes, labeled with help from almost 50,000 workers in 167 countries [S1].
- Recalls that "Some very friendly colleagues advised me to do something more useful for my tenure" while the team struggled for funding [S1].
- Names three forces that started modern AI: neural networks, GPUs, and big data such as ImageNet [S2].
- Holds that "taking a picture is not the same as seeing and understanding," and adds: "Seeing is for doing and learning." [S2]
- Wants AI to be a trusted partner that augments productivity and humanity "while respecting our individual dignity" [S2].
- Argues there is nothing "artificial" about AI: it is made by humans, behaves like humans and affects humans [S3].
- Sets out three goals for human-centered AI: deeper intelligence, "enhancing us, not replacing us," and development guided at each step by concern for its effect on humans [S3].
- Describes her habit of "looking for the next North Star problem to solve" by asking what evolution or brain development has done [S4].
- Advises PhD students to pick north stars that are not on a collision course with problems industry can solve better with more compute and data [S4].
- Hires for one trait: "I look for intellectual fearlessness," meaning the courage to embrace something hard and be all in [S4].
- Describes entrepreneurship as her comfort zone: "Just hunker down and build." [S4]
- Calls LLMs "wordsmiths in the dark; eloquent but inexperienced, knowledgeable but ungrounded" and spatial intelligence AI's next frontier [S5].
- Defines world models by three capabilities: generative, multimodal and interactive [S5].
- Says the pursuit of visual and spatial intelligence has been her North Star since entering the field, and that World Labs was founded in early 2024 with Justin Johnson, Christoph Lassner and Ben Mildenhall [S5].
- On September 28, 2026 AMD agreed to acquire World Labs, of which Li is co-founder and CEO; on closing, expected by the end of 2026, she becomes AMD executive vice president and chief scientist [S6].
