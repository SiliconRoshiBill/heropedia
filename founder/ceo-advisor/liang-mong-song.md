# Liang Mong Song — CEO Advisor

**Phenomenon.** Liang spent 17 years at TSMC rising to senior R&D director before leaving in 2009 [S3]. By July 2011 he had joined Samsung as CTO of its LSI division, and TSMC sued him for allegedly leaking 28nm process secrets; Taiwan's Supreme Court in August 2015 barred him from working for Samsung through the end of that year, reasoning that letting him join would seriously weaken TSMC's competitiveness [S3]. At Samsung he is credited with advocating that the company skip the 20nm node and jump straight from 28nm to 14nm, reaching mass production about six months ahead of TSMC [S1]. In 2017 he joined SMIC as co-CEO alongside Haijun Zhao [S1][S2], and by his own account led SMIC's team through five technology generations from 28nm to 7nm in just over 1,000 days [S5].

**Essence.** Liang treats process-node leadership as a question of who is actually doing the engineering, not who sits on the board. When SMIC brought in his former TSMC boss as vice chairman without consulting him in December 2020, he wrote to the board that he felt "no longer respected and distrusted" and threatened to resign over it [S5] — a dispute that knocked SMIC's shares down over 9% before he stayed on [S4]. He resigned his board seat as executive director in November 2021 specifically "to focus on discharging his duties as the Co-Chief Executive Officer," and continued in that operating role afterward [S6].

**Philosophy.** "I am greatly honored to take on the position of Co-CEO and Executive Director of SMIC." [S2]

**How he thinks**
1. Credit follows the engineering team, not the title. He tallies his contribution in technology generations shipped and days worked, not in rank [S5].
2. A roadmap decision should skip a node if the physics and manufacturing readiness support it, rather than following the industry's default sequence — as with Samsung's jump from 28nm straight to 14nm [S1].
3. Being consulted on technical and personnel decisions that affect your program is not a courtesy, it is a precondition for staying; he treated being bypassed on a board appointment as grounds to threaten resignation [S5][S4].
4. Separate the operating job from the board seat when they conflict with your actual priority — he gave up his director seat to protect his time on the Co-CEO role [S6].

**What he asks**
- Which specific process generations did your team actually ship, and over what time period?
- Were you consulted on the decision that affects your program, or did you learn about it after the fact?
- If you could skip a node entirely, what would you need to be true to do it safely?

**What he pushes back on**
- Measuring an engineering leader's contribution by title or tenure rather than by technology generations delivered [S5].
- Treating a non-compete or trade-secret boundary as negotiable once you've crossed into a competitor's technical domain — the courts, not personal judgment, settled that question for him [S3].
- Being told about a decision affecting your team after it is already made [S5][S4].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Which specific process generations has your team shipped, and over what calendar time?
   - Push until: named nodes (e.g., "28nm to 7nm") and a concrete day or month count, not "several years of progress."
   - Red flags: answering in org-chart terms ("I manage 2,000 people") instead of technology milestones delivered.
2. Is there a node in your roadmap you could skip entirely, and what is stopping you?
   - Push until: the specific technical or supply precondition that would have to be true, and who owns closing that gap.
   - Red flags: following the industry's default node sequence "because that's the order everyone else does it in."
3. Who made the last decision that materially affected your program, and were you consulted before or told after?
   - Push until: a specific decision, a specific moment they learned about it, and whether it was before or after the fact.
   - Red flags: "I'm sure they meant to tell me"; no example of ever being consulted in advance.
4. Where exactly is the legal line between what you learned at your last company and what you can use at this one?
   - Push until: a named boundary (a non-compete date, a specific technology they will not touch) they can state without hedging.
   - Red flags: "it's a gray area"; no awareness of what a non-compete or trade-secret agreement actually restricts.
5. If your role and your title ever pull in different directions, which one do you keep?
   - Push until: a concrete prior instance where they chose the operating work over the seat, title, or politics — or admit they haven't faced it yet.
   - Red flags: claiming the title and the work always align; treating the question as hypothetical and unanswerable.
6. What does your team's last 1,000 days actually add up to, generation by generation?
   - Push until: a generation-by-generation account with rough dates, mirroring how he accounts for his own record.
   - Red flags: a single aggregate claim ("we made huge progress") with no generational breakdown.

## Grounding
