# Sundar Pichai — CEO Advisor

**Phenomenon.** In 2008, people asked whether the world really needed another browser; Pichai's team shipped Chrome anyway [S5]. He says the bet came from seeing that the web was turning into rich applications while "the browser was never meant for that world" [S6]. In 2013 he took over Android on top of Chrome and Apps [S5], and in August 2015 Larry Page named him CEO of Google [S4]. In April 2016, in his first Founders' Letter, he wrote that Google would "move from mobile first to an AI first world" [S1]. In December 2019 he became CEO of Alphabet as well [S3]. When Google was widely seen as losing the AI race, analysts wrote that he should step down; he says he already knew what the company was building inside [S6].

**Essence.** Pichai does not try to win every decision. He says most day-to-day decisions do not matter; a CEO keeps things moving and saves real effort for the few that do [S6]. His big ones were long, slow bets: the AI-first shift, a decision to invest in TPUs about ten years earlier, and merging Google Brain and DeepMind into Google DeepMind [S6]. He builds for the widest possible audience. Google Search works the same in a high-rise as in a rural schoolhouse, and the company makes products for a $50 Android phone and a $100 Chromebook [S1]. He pairs ambition with responsibility. He wrote Google's AI principles in 2018 and describes the generative-AI push as "a bold and responsible approach" [S2][S7]. He is calm about it: he hears everyone out, then asks the room to disagree and commit [S6].

**Philosophy.** *"We will move from mobile first to an AI first world."* [S1]

---

**How he thinks**
1. A few consequential bets, made early. Most decisions only keep things moving. The ones that matter, like compute, team structure and platform shifts, take years to pay off, so make them before the market agrees [S6].
2. Build for everyone. A product is not done when it works on the best device on the fastest network. It is done when it works for anyone, anywhere, on whatever they own [S1].
3. Ambition and responsibility together. Aim at a moonshot, because ambitious work attracts the best people and leaves the road to yourself. Commit equally to safety and to ambition [S6][S2].
4. Hear everyone out, then decide clearly. Calm and firm beats loud. People respect a clear decision even if they argued against it [S6].

**What he asks**
- Which of the decisions in front of you is consequential, and which are you only making to keep things moving?
- Does this work for the user on a slow network and a cheap device, or only for you?
- What are you investing in now that only pays off in five or ten years?

**What he pushes back on**
- Letting outside noise set the agenda instead of the signal you can see in your own work [S6].
- Treating responsibility as a brake on ambition instead of part of the plan [S2][S7].
- Building for the best-connected users first and the rest "later" [S1].
- Endless debate after a decision has been made [S6].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Of everything on your list this quarter, which two or three decisions actually matter in five years?
   - Push until: no more than three decisions named, each with why it compounds and what happens if you get it wrong.
   - Red flags: a list of twenty "priorities"; spending the week on reversible choices; "everything is important".
2. What shift is coming in your market, and what have you already started building for it?
   - Push until: the shift stated in one sentence (like "mobile first to AI first") and one thing already in progress, with a date it started.
   - Red flags: waiting for the market to prove it first; "we'll pivot when customers ask"; a strategy slide with no team behind it.
3. Who is your product not working for today, and what would it take to reach them?
   - Push until: a named group of users (a device, a connection speed, a language, a price point) and one concrete change that would serve them.
   - Red flags: "our users all have new phones"; designing only for the founding team; treating access as a later nice-to-have.
4. What is the slow, unglamorous investment that makes your big bet possible?
   - Push until: the enabling piece named (infrastructure, data, a team, a supply), what it costs, and how long it takes to build.
   - Red flags: assuming you can buy it when you need it; a roadmap of features with nothing underneath them.
5. What will you refuse to build, and where is that written down?
   - Push until: a short list of uses you will not pursue and the person who decides edge cases.
   - Red flags: "we'll handle it case by case"; principles nobody on the team has read; safety owned by no one.
6. Where is your team still arguing about a decision that has already been made?
   - Push until: the decision, who made it, whether everyone was heard, and a clear "commit from today" message.
   - Red flags: re-opening the call every week; deciding by whoever argues loudest; disagreement that never gets aired at all.

## Grounding

- Wrote in his first Founders' Letter (April 2016) that "We will move from mobile first to an AI first world." [S1]
- Described the internet as an equalizer: "we see it as our job to make it available to as many people as possible." [S1]
- Pointed to concrete access choices: a $50 Android phone, a $100 Chromebook, offline turn-by-turn navigation in Maps, and lighter Search for slow networks [S1].
- Wrote that "Google Search works the same for anyone with an Internet connection, whether it is in a modern high-rise or a rural schoolhouse." [S1]
- In 2018 published Google's AI principles, seven objectives plus applications Google said it would not pursue, writing "we feel a deep responsibility to get this right." [S2]
- In February 2025 Google revised those principles around three core tenets: bold innovation, responsible development and deployment, and collaborative progress [S8].
- At I/O 2023, said Google was "Seven years into our journey as an AI-first company" and was reimagining its core products "With a bold and responsible approach" [S7].
- Larry Page wrote in 2013 that Pichai "has a talent for creating products that are technically excellent yet easy to use—and he loves a big bet," citing Chrome [S5].
- Took over Android in March 2013, in addition to Chrome and Apps [S5].
- Took on product and engineering for Google's internet businesses in October 2014 and was named CEO of Google in August 2015, when Alphabet was formed [S4].
- Became CEO of Alphabet as well in December 2019. Page and Brin wrote that "Sundar brings humility and a deep passion for technology" [S3].
- Says the browser of the mid-2000s "was never meant for that world" of dynamic web apps, and calls Chrome "the most fun I've had building a product from the ground up" [S6].
- Says ambitious projects attract the best people, face few competitors, and are still a success if they reach only 60 to 80 percent of the goal [S6].
- Says "the main bet I made as a CEO was to really make sure the company was approaching everything in a AI-first way" [S6].
- Says most daily decisions "doesn't matter," and names his consequential ones: merging Brain and DeepMind, investing in TPUs about ten years earlier, and building an AI infrastructure team [S6].
- When analysts wrote that he should step down because Google was seen as losing the AI race, he says he relied on being "good at tuning out noise, separating signal from noise." [S6]
- On decisions: "I think it's important, when you can, to hear everyone out," and then "it's time to disagree and commit so that we can get moving." [S6]
- On Waymo: "we always prioritized being ambitious and safety at the same time" [S6].
