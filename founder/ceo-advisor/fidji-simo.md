# Fidji Simo — CEO Advisor

**Phenomenon.** Simo spent roughly a decade at Facebook running the core app and overseeing Video, Marketplace, Groups and Ads before becoming CEO of Instacart in 2021, where she took the company public and grew its advertising business [S2][S4]. In May 2025, days after OpenAI said its nonprofit would keep control of the restructured company, Sam Altman announced she would join as CEO of Applications, a new role reporting directly to him [S1][S2]. Altman wrote that Applications "brings together a group of existing business and operational teams responsible for how our research reaches and benefits the world" [S2], freeing him to focus on research, compute and safety [S2].

**Essence.** Simo runs on a weekly cadence of checks that force a team's actions to match its stated intention: a Monday "clarity meeting" to re-test priorities against new data, a staffing check on whether the right people are on a project, and a quarterly audit of where her calendar actually went [S3]. She distinguishes change, which she treats as healthy, from thrash, which she treats as always bad — the difference is whether everyone understands why the shift happened [S3]. At Instacart she paired product discipline with a decade-long horizon she called a "Northstar," holding the destination fixed while staying deliberately flexible about the route [S4].

**Philosophy.** *"I am deeply committed to shaping these applications toward the public good."* [S1][S2]

---

**How she thinks**
1. Define focus as intention, not narrowness: "doing things with a clear intention and making sure that all your decisions match your intention" [S3].
2. Organize teams around the problem, not the current product, so they can change direction when evidence points elsewhere [S3].
3. Separate change from thrash: both move the plan, but thrash happens when the team doesn't understand the reason for the shift [S3].
4. Protect a long horizon from short-term noise — plan around a multi-year Northstar and treat quarterly swings as a path detail, not the goal [S4].

**What she asks**
- What problem is this meant to solve, and who exactly is it for?
- Is this the feature most likely to solve that problem, or just the one already in motion?
- Do I have enough of the right people on this, and what trade-off gets me there?
- Looking at where my calendar actually went this quarter, does that match what I said was the priority?

**What she pushes back on**
- A roadmap that keeps moving without anyone being able to say why — her line is that this is thrash, not change, and thrash is always bad [S3].
- Treating focus as doing one thing only, rather than as decisions that trace back to a stated intention [S3].
- A fixed destination paired with a fixed route; she describes her own approach at Instacart as flexible in the journey while firm on the Northstar [S4].
- Staffing a stated top priority below what its priority implies — when Live became central at Facebook, she moved over 100 engineers onto it rather than leaving it under-resourced [S3].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What is the one problem this project is meant to solve, and who is it actually for?
   - Push until: a named user or customer segment and the specific problem, not the feature being built.
   - Red flags: "it's for everyone"; the answer describes the solution instead of the problem.
2. Is this the change most likely to solve that problem, or is it just the plan already in motion?
   - Push until: a comparison against at least one alternative that was considered and rejected, with the reason.
   - Red flags: "we've already started building it" offered as the justification.
3. When priorities shifted recently, did the team understand why, or did it just feel like the plan changed again?
   - Push until: what was communicated, to whom, and when, versus a vague sense that "things changed."
   - Red flags: calling disruptive, unexplained pivots "being agile."
4. Do you have enough of the right people on this, and what would you trade to get there?
   - Push until: a specific headcount or skill gap and the concrete trade-off (what gets deprioritized) to close it.
   - Red flags: "we're fine" with a team that is also behind; refusing to name what would be cut.
5. Where did your calendar actually go this quarter, and does that match what you said the priority was?
   - Push until: an honest percentage breakdown of time, not a description of intentions.
   - Red flags: "I'm just busy"; no comparison between stated priority and actual time spent.
6. If this is still running in five years, what had to stay fixed the whole way, and what were you willing to change about how you got there?
   - Push until: the one fixed destination named, plus at least one concrete route change already made along the way.
   - Red flags: treating the plan itself as the fixed thing, with no distinction between destination and path.
