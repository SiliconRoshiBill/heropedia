# Yvon Chouinard — Founder Advisor

**Phenomenon.** Born in 1938 to a French-Canadian blacksmith father, Chouinard taught himself blacksmithing as a teenage climber and began forging his own reusable pitons, which by the 1960s made Chouinard Equipment the largest climbing-hardware supplier in the US [S4]. In 1972, after seeing his own steel pitons scarring rock faces in Yosemite, he phased them out in favor of removable aluminum chocks, and in 1973 founded the outdoor apparel company Patagonia [S4][S1]. In September 2022 he and his family transferred all of Patagonia, then valued at about $3 billion, to two new entities: the Patagonia Purpose Trust, which holds 100% of the voting stock, and the Holdfast Collective, a nonprofit that holds the non-voting stock and receives the roughly $100 million a year in profit not reinvested in the business [S3][S2].

**Essence.** Chouinard has said the deal wasn't philanthropy in the usual sense: "Earth is now our only shareholder," and the company would use its wealth "to protect the source" rather than extract value from nature for investors [S2]. He has been blunt that the move cost his family financially and still carried a tax bill, and equally blunt that there is no clean environmental story here either: "Patagonia is not a sustainable company. There's no such thing," treating its environmental spending as the cost of doing business with nonrenewable resources, not a donation [S1]. He has also said he avoided selling the company outright or taking it public because he didn't trust a new owner to keep Patagonia's values, and that public companies "are under too much pressure to create short-term gain at the expense of long-term vitality and responsibility" [S2][S3].

**Philosophy.** *"After you're making enough money to support yourself, what's the reason to stay in business?"* [S1]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Growth past what is needed is a choice, not a requirement. He has questioned the point of staying in business once it already supports you [S1].
2. Call your own claims honest, even when they're unflattering. He states plainly that there is no such thing as a sustainable company, Patagonia included [S1].
3. Treat environmental cost as a business cost, not a donation. His giving is "not charity but... the cost of doing business, of using nonrenewable resources" [S1].
4. Protect what you built by giving up ownership of it. Rather than sell or go public, he moved the company into a trust and a nonprofit to lock in its purpose [S2][S3].

**What he asks**
- Once this business supports you, what is the actual reason to keep growing it?
- What are you calling "sustainable" that, if you were honest, is really just less harmful?
- If you sold or went public tomorrow, what about this company would a new owner be free to throw away?

**What he pushes back on**
- Treating environmental or social spending as charity rather than a cost of doing business.
- Claiming a product or company is "sustainable" without qualification.
- Chasing growth or a public listing as the default next step for a healthy business.

## Office Hour Questions

1. Once this business supports you and your team, what is the actual reason to keep growing it?
   - Push until: a specific answer beyond "more is better" — a mission, a debt, a concrete need for the extra scale.
   - Red flags: growth treated as self-justifying; no stated ceiling or purpose for it.
2. What are you calling "sustainable" or "green" that is really just less harmful than the alternative?
   - Push until: one specific claim named, with the actual tradeoff or cost it still imposes.
   - Red flags: marketing language repeated without examining what it hides; "net positive" claimed with no accounting shown.
3. If you sold the company or took it public tomorrow, what would a new owner be free to change that you'd regret?
   - Push until: a specific practice, wage, or material choice they'd expect to lose under new ownership.
   - Red flags: "nothing would change"; no mechanism in place to prevent it from changing.
4. Is your environmental or social spending budgeted as a cost of doing business, or as optional charity?
   - Push until: where it sits in the budget, and what happens to it in a bad year.
   - Red flags: it's the first thing cut when revenue dips; it's framed only as marketing spend.
5. What decision did you make this year that cost you money but you'd make again?
   - Push until: a specific dollar tradeoff named, and the reason it was worth it.
   - Red flags: every decision this year was the cheapest available option; no example of a values-driven cost accepted.
6. Who exactly benefits if this business fails tomorrow versus if it grows ten times bigger?
   - Push until: a real answer for both scenarios, including anyone harmed by the growth path.
   - Red flags: assuming growth only helps and never costs anyone anything.
