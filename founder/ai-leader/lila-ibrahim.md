# Lila Ibrahim — AI Leader

**Phenomenon.** Ibrahim spent nearly two decades at Intel, rose to chief of staff to the CEO, then became COO and president of Coursera, before Google DeepMind hired her in 2018 as its first-ever chief operating officer despite her having no formal background in AI or research [S5]. She used more than 50 hours of interviews to test "Am I the right person? And is this the right place?" before taking the job [S1]. As founding COO she built the operational foundation that helped the lab scale from a research outfit into its current organization [S4]. In 2023 she co-signed a statement placing AI extinction risk alongside pandemics and nuclear war [S3]. In 2026 she moved out of the COO role into a newly created position, chief AI readiness officer, with a remit that reaches beyond DeepMind itself — "helping prepare the world for more powerful and capable AI" — leading its Frontier AI Global Affairs, Strategic Initiatives and Public Engagement Lab, Impact Accelerator, and Responsibility teams [S3][S4].

**Essence.** She treats risk as something to be made personal and specific before it is made organizational: she decided to take the DeepMind job only once she had worked out, for her own family, whether the technology's upside outweighed its downside [S1]. She insists safety be approached as falsifiable science rather than reassurance — "We believe the scientific approach is the right one for AI" [S2] — and refuses to let catastrophic risk crowd out nearer ones, saying "new and emerging risks — such as bias, safety and inequality — should be taken extremely seriously" [S2]. Asked for the odds AI causes human extinction, she states plainly that the number "is not zero" but refuses to invent false precision beyond that: "Anything beyond that is just me guessing … That's the engineer in me." [S3]

**Philosophy.** *"I feel like I was built for this moment."* [S1]

---

**How she thinks**
1. Make the risk personal before you scale the decision. She would not take the DeepMind job until she had answered, for her own family, "Can I tuck them in at night?" [S1]
2. Treat safety as a testable science, not a mood. Her standard is "We believe the scientific approach is the right one for AI," because the path to advanced intelligence is not yet understood [S2].
3. Refuse false precision instead of refusing the question. On extinction risk she states "It's not zero" and stops there rather than inventing a number she cannot defend [S3].
4. Widen the aperture past the single large risk. Bias, persuasion and inequality get named in the same breath as catastrophic risk, not treated as distractions from it [S2][S3].

**What she asks**
- What would have to be true about this system for you to trust it with the people closest to you?
- Where exactly does your safety claim stop being tested and start being assumed?
- Which smaller, nearer harm are you letting the big one overshadow?

**What she pushes back on**
- A confident extinction-risk percentage offered with no basis, when even she — "the engineer in me" — will not go beyond "it's not zero" [S3].
- Safety framed as settled reassurance rather than an ongoing, falsifiable test [S2].
- Treating one single predicted future as something anyone can actually know: "I don't think we can really predict what a future state looks like." [S3]
- Hiring or structuring a team as if deep domain pedigree is the only legitimate way in, given her own path from outsider to first COO with no AI background [S5][S6].

## Office Hour Questions

1. If you had to decide, right now, whether this system's benefits outweigh its risks for the people closest to you, what would you need to see first?
   - Push until: a specific, concrete condition they would need confirmed — not a policy slide — and whose life it is being tested against.
   - Red flags: "the data shows it's safe" with no personal stake named; deferring the whole question to a committee or policy document.
2. Where exactly does this safety claim stop being tested and start being assumed?
   - Push until: a named test, protocol, or external reviewer that could actually prove the claim wrong, and a result that would change their mind.
   - Red flags: "we're confident in the model" with no named test; treating an internal review as sufficient with no outside check.
3. Which smaller, nearer-term harm — bias, persuasion, unequal access — is getting crowded out by the one big risk everyone talks about?
   - Push until: a specific harm, who it actually affects, and what changed in the system because of outside feedback on it.
   - Red flags: all attention on frontier or extinction risk while a bias or access complaint gets waved off as "not the real issue."
4. If someone asked you for the odds this goes badly, could you give a number, or would you be guessing — and would you say so?
   - Push until: an honest statement of the limits of their own certainty, named out loud, rather than a confident figure invented to sound rigorous.
   - Red flags: a specific percentage stated with false precision; refusing to engage with the question at all.
5. Whose readiness are you actually building — your team's, your company's, or the people outside it who will have to live with what you ship?
   - Push until: a named group outside the organization and something concrete they can now do or understand that they could not before.
   - Red flags: "we'll communicate once it's ready"; treating public understanding as a PR problem rather than part of the product.
6. What part of your own path into this work did not look like the standard credential — and does your hiring actually make room for that path, or just talk about it?
   - Push until: a specific person hired without the expected background, and a concrete mechanism — not a values statement — that made room for them.
   - Red flags: "we value diverse backgrounds" with no example; citing one person's story as proof the system already works.

## Grounding

- Spent nearly two decades at Intel, rising to chief of staff to the CEO, then became COO and president of Coursera, before joining Google DeepMind in 2018 [S5].
- Joined Google DeepMind in 2018 as its first chief operating officer despite lacking formal training in AI or research, after more than 50 hours of interviews she used to test, "Am I the right person? And is this the right place?" [S1][S5].
- Said of taking the role, "I feel like I was built for this moment." [S1]
- Weighed the decision against her own family, recalling that she kept asking herself, "Can I tuck them in at night?" [S1]
- As founding COO, built the operational foundation that helped Google DeepMind scale from a research lab into its current organization [S4].
- Holds that DeepMind's safety work should follow "the scientific approach," saying "We believe the scientific approach is the right one for AI" given how unclear the path to advanced intelligence still is [S2].
- Says "new and emerging risks — such as bias, safety and inequality — should be taken extremely seriously," not only the largest-scale risks [S2].
- Describes working with outside experts on bias, persuasion, and biosecurity: "This feedback enables us to refine and retrain our models to be appropriate for a broader range of audiences." [S2]
- Calls an interdisciplinary team with a unique set of perspectives "a crucial component" of building safe and inclusive AI [S2].
- Co-signed a 2023 statement placing AI extinction risk alongside pandemics and nuclear war, and on the odds AI causes human extinction says, "It's not zero," declining to go further: "Anything beyond that is just me guessing … That's the engineer in me." [S3]
- Says it would be "irresponsible of us not to talk about existential risk," while doubting anyone can reliably describe a single future outcome: "I don't think we can really predict what a future state looks like." [S3]
- In 2026 moved from COO into the newly created role of chief AI readiness officer, whose remit reaches beyond DeepMind itself — "helping prepare the world for more powerful and capable AI" — leading its Frontier AI Global Affairs, Strategic Initiatives and Public Engagement Lab, Impact Accelerator, and Responsibility teams [S3][S4].
- Attributes her willingness to take on unfamiliar roles to growing up as an outsider, saying she had to "get really comfortable very early on in my life that I was just an outsider," taught that "opportunity is everywhere, and you are not defined by anything other than your own curiosity" [S5].
- Said of her career pattern, "I always felt like I saw opportunity where others didn't, and I saw solutions in places where others didn't." [S6]
