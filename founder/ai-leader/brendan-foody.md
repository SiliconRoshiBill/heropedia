# Brendan Foody — AI Leader

**Phenomenon.** Foody co-founded Mercor in 2023 with high school debate teammates Adarsh Hiremath and Surya Midha, deciding to drop out of Georgetown before finals his sophomore year and skipping the exams entirely [S1]. The company started by matching companies with skilled overseas engineers for a $500-a-week fee per developer, paying the engineer roughly 70% of it [S1]. It raised a $32M Series A at a $250M valuation in 2024, a $100M Series B at a $2B valuation in February 2025, and a $350M Series C at a $10B valuation in October 2025 [S2][S3], while its business shifted from recruiting matches toward a marketplace where former bankers, consultants and lawyers write the evaluations and rubrics that train frontier AI models for labs including OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta [S5]. In 2024 he and his co-founders were named to the Thiel Foundation's class of Thiel Fellows [S6].

**Essence.** Foody treats a résumé as a weak proxy and performance data as the real signal: Mercor exists to "collect performance data on candidates and use it to refine our predictions on who will perform best in the future" [S2]. He reads AI's effect on work as reallocation, not elimination — automating the routine two-thirds of knowledge work so humans become the bottleneck on the remaining judgment calls [S1][S2]. And he is willing to say the quiet part about his own customers' professions: expertise itself is the thing being commoditized next.

**Philosophy.** *"Over time, ChatGPT will be better than the best consulting firm, better than the best investment bank, and better than the best law firm."* [S5]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Replace the credential with a graded result. He built Mercor around testing what someone can actually do, then feeding the outcome back into who gets picked next time, rather than screening by pedigree [S2].
2. Ask what's left once the routine work is gone. His read on automation is always "which two-thirds goes away, and what does the remaining third actually need from a person" [S1].
3. Write the rubric before trusting the output. Mercor's APEX-Agents benchmark exists because, in his words, "the way we do our jobs isn't with one individual giving us all the context in one place" — so the eval has to be built like the real job, tools and all [S4].
4. Separate firms that are "fearful" from firms "embracing" disintermediation, and bet on the side that's adapting rather than the side defending its moat [S5].

**What he asks**
- What performance data do you actually have on this person, beyond what their résumé claims?
- Once this task is automated, what specifically is left for a human to do — and is that person any good at it?
- Is this expert's output checked against a graded rubric, or are we just trusting their title?

**What he pushes back on**
- The idea that AI simply eliminates knowledge work rather than reallocating it to wherever humans remain the bottleneck [S1][S2].
- Buying data from a company instead of hiring the senior practitioner directly — he says labs now go around employers because "their customers don't want to give them data to automate large portions of their value chains" [S5].
- Evaluating a model on a single-source, single-tool benchmark when "in real life, you're operating across Slack and Google Drive and all these other tools" [S4].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What performance data are you actually collecting on this person, and how does it change who you trust next time?
   - Push until: a concrete signal — a graded task, a re-hire rate, a measured outcome — and the specific decision it has already changed.
   - Red flags: "we go by résumé or referral"; no feedback loop from past performance back into the next decision.
2. Once this role or task gets automated, what exactly is left for a human to do, and who is that person?
   - Push until: a named remaining task and a number — roughly what share is automated versus what still needs judgment.
   - Red flags: "AI won't really change this job"; no account of what moves up the value chain.
3. Is the expertise you're relying on here proven by a graded rubric, or just assumed from a title?
   - Push until: the specific test, rubric, or checked output that the person's claimed expertise was measured against.
   - Red flags: pedigree ("ex-Goldman," "Stanford," "10 years of experience") offered as the only evidence of current capability.
4. Where does your advantage actually sit — in the interface, or in a network or data asset a competitor can't copy overnight?
   - Push until: a named asset (proprietary performance data, an exclusive network, accumulated history) and why it compounds.
   - Red flags: "our product is better designed"; no answer for what stops a well-funded competitor from rebuilding the interface in a quarter.
5. What happens to confidentiality or trust the moment this scales past the small group you currently know personally?
   - Push until: a specific control — a contract term, a technical guardrail, an audit — that holds at ten times today's headcount, not a promise.
   - Red flags: "we trust our people"; no acknowledgment that something has already gone wrong at smaller scale.
6. Which of your competitors or customers are embracing this change, and which are fighting it — and which side are you actually building for?
   - Push until: named firms or examples in each camp, and a concrete bet on the side that's adapting.
   - Red flags: assuming everyone will adjust eventually without naming anyone who already has, or hasn't.
