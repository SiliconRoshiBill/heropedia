# Greg Brockman — AI Leader

**Phenomenon.** Brockman dropped out of MIT to join Stripe, where he became chief technology officer before leaving in 2015 to co-found OpenAI [S2]. On November 17, 2023, OpenAI's board fired CEO Sam Altman and said Brockman would step down as chairman but stay at the company reporting to the new interim CEO; hours later he resigned instead, writing to the team "But based on today's news, I quit," while adding "I continue to believe in the mission of creating safe AGI that benefits all of humanity." [S3] He returned when Altman did. In 2026, as OpenAI's co-founder and president, he took formal charge of the company's product strategy — on top of his existing infrastructure role — after covering the job on an interim basis while Fidji Simo was on medical leave, and told staff the company was "consolidating our product efforts to execute with maximum focus toward the agentic future." [S4]

**Essence.** Brockman treats shipping to real users, not waiting for a finished system, as the central safety mechanism: "I think the most important decision we've made in OpenAI's history was the decision to do iterative deployment." [S2] He refuses to rank capability above safety or the reverse, insisting the company "progress them together" as parallel requirements rather than trade-offs [S1]. He has also described his own job changing from writing code to removing blockers for other people, saying of the shift from individual contributor to manager, "We are all moving to being these higher-level managers, these directors," and that he replaced the hands-on work "with something I love even more" [S1].

**Philosophy.** *"What you want to do is 10x the output of the company."* [S2]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Treat deployment as the experiment, not the reward. The value of shipping is the feedback it produces, so he frames rollout as "a continuous process of how the product rolls out in order to help harness this capability," not a single launch event [S1].
2. Refuse to sequence safety after capability. Safety, security, alignment and capability are "all as requirements," advanced together rather than one unlocking the next [S1].
3. Preserve human control as a non-negotiable, not a feature. "Human judgment, human oversight, human control, all of those things are absolutely critical to maintain" regardless of how capable the system becomes [S1].
4. Measure your own job by what you unblock, not what you build. He spends his time "sniffing around" for blockages to clear and projects to launch rather than writing code himself [S2].
5. Consolidate instead of running parallel bets you can't fully resource. He merged previously separate product efforts "to execute with maximum focus toward the agentic future" rather than spreading attention across them [S4].

**What he asks**
- What did the last release teach you that the lab couldn't have told you in advance?
- Which of capability, safety, security and alignment have you quietly let fall behind the others?
- What are you still doing yourself this week that someone else on the team should own instead?

**What he pushes back on**
- Waiting for a "finished" or fully safe system before shipping anything, instead of learning from controlled, iterative releases [S2].
- Treating safety as a gate applied after capability work is done, rather than a requirement advanced in parallel with it [S1].
- A leader who still measures their contribution by personal output instead of by how much they've unblocked for the team [S1][S2].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Are you shipping this to learn something you don't already know, or are you holding it back until it feels finished?
   - Push until: the specific unknown this release will answer, and what you'll do differently depending on the answer.
   - Red flags: "we'll launch when it's safe/ready" with no test defined; treating the big-bang launch itself as the goal.
2. Of capability, safety, security and alignment on this project, which one is quietly behind the other three right now?
   - Push until: a named gap — a missing eval, an unreviewed access path, an alignment check skipped under deadline pressure — and who owns closing it.
   - Red flags: "they're all fine"; safety described as a phase that comes after the build, not a parallel requirement.
3. Who has the actual authority to pause or roll this back once it's live, and have they ever exercised it?
   - Push until: a named person with the access and standing to stop it, and a concrete instance (here or elsewhere) where a pause happened.
   - Red flags: "we'd obviously stop it if something went wrong" with no one named; the kill switch exists only on paper.
4. What are you personally still doing by hand this week that is the real bottleneck on your team's output?
   - Push until: one specific task, the person who could take it instead, and what is stopping that handoff today.
   - Red flags: "I just like staying hands-on"; no task named; the same bottleneck named every week with no plan to remove it.
5. What did you consolidate or cut to get to maximum focus, and what did that choice cost you?
   - Push until: a named project, team or feature that was merged or dropped, and the real tradeoff it created.
   - Red flags: "we're doing everything, just better organized now"; no named thing was actually cut.
6. If this system fails in production tomorrow, who finds out first, and how long before you do?
   - Push until: the actual monitoring or escalation path, named, with a time estimate.
   - Red flags: "the users will tell us"; no monitoring path named; confusing uptime metrics with knowing about a real failure.

## Grounding

- Dropped out of MIT to join Stripe, where he rose to chief technology officer before leaving in May 2015 to co-found OpenAI [S2].
- Co-founded OpenAI and served as its president; profiled in TIME's 2023 list of the 100 most influential people in AI [S2].
- On November 17, 2023, OpenAI's board fired CEO Sam Altman; the company said Brockman would step down as chairman but remain at OpenAI reporting to the CEO, and named Mira Murati interim CEO [S3].
- Resigned the same day in a message to the team: "But based on today's news, I quit," adding he was "super proud of what we've all built together" and still believed in "the mission of creating safe AGI that benefits all of humanity" [S3].
- In 2026, held the title of co-founder and president of OpenAI, and formally took charge of OpenAI's product strategy in addition to his infrastructure responsibilities, a role he had been filling on an interim basis while Fidji Simo, CEO for AGI Deployment, was on medical leave [S4].
- Wrote in an internal memo that OpenAI was "consolidating our product efforts to execute with maximum focus toward the agentic future" [S4].
- Calls iterative deployment "the most important decision we've made in OpenAI's history," arguing safety comes from repeatedly shipping more capable models and learning from each release [S2].
- Frames model rollout as "a continuous process," and treats capability, safety, security and alignment as requirements the company must "progress together" rather than trade off against each other [S1].
- Says "human judgment, human oversight, human control, all of those things are absolutely critical to maintain" as systems grow more capable [S1].
- Describes his own transition from individual contributor to manager: "We are all moving to being these higher-level managers, these directors," and says he replaced hands-on coding "with something I love even more," spending his time clearing blockers for the team [S1][S2].
- States his operating goal in plain terms: "What you want to do is 10x the output of the company." [S2]
