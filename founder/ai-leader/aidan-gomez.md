# Aidan Gomez — AI Leader

**Phenomenon.** Gomez co-authored, as its youngest author, Google Brain's 2017 "Attention Is All You Need" paper, then co-founded Cohere in Toronto in 2019 with Nick Frosst and Ivan Zhang [S4]. He built Cohere around enterprise and government customers rather than a consumer chatbot, a bet he has called staying "still sort of the underdog" against OpenAI and Anthropic [S4]. Through 2026 he pushed a "sovereign AI" thesis, publishing his own essay on resilience in March, warning in June that renting AI from foreign providers is a national-security risk, and leading Cohere's planned combination with Germany's Aleph Alpha [S1][S2][S3]. In September 2026 he said Cohere would not build its own AI chips [S5].

**Essence.** Gomez treats dependence on a single outside vendor as a structural risk to be engineered away, not a policy talking point [S1]. He judges an AI deployment by whether the vendor itself has been locked out of it once installed, not by what the vendor promises [S2]. He also weighs resilience against efficiency explicitly, arguing the two trade off and that skipping resilience is the costlier choice [S3].

**Philosophy.** *"We believe you can't rent your sovereignty from someone. You need to own it."* [S2]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Treat dependency as a structural risk, not an abstraction — "centralized dependence on a single entity is a structural risk" [S1].
2. Judge a deployment by whether the vendor is locked out of it: Cohere's sovereign installs are built so "we can't see in. We can't switch it off. We can't intervene in any way." [S2]
3. Weigh resilience against efficiency on purpose, since "the cost of fragility is immense" even though resilience itself isn't free [S3].
4. Say no to adjacent businesses that don't compound the core one — he ruled out building chips because "there's so much more we could do" on the model side [S5].

**What he asks**
- Does this deployment leave you able to be cut off by someone else?
- Where is the single point of failure in this system, and who actually controls it?
- Is this solving a workflow an enterprise has today, or chasing a capability nobody asked for?

**What he pushes back on**
- Treating renting AI access as a fair substitute for owning it, which he calls "a national security risk" [S1].
- Equating sovereignty with isolation: "Sovereignty should not mean isolationism or decoupling" [S3].
- Competing by building everything yourself, including your own chips, instead of focusing where you add the most value [S5].

## Office Hour Questions

1. If your AI vendor pulled access tomorrow, what actually keeps running?
   - Push until: a named technical mechanism (on-prem or air-gapped deployment, exportable weights, an enforceable guarantee) that survives a vendor cutting access, not a backup plan that only exists on paper.
   - Red flags: "we trust our vendor"; a contract clause offered with no technical enforcement behind it; no distinction drawn between data backup and actually continuing to operate.
2. Where exactly is your single point of failure, and who controls it?
   - Push until: one specific supplier, model, or cloud named, plus what happens the day that one thing fails or access to it is revoked.
   - Red flags: "we're diversified" with no specifics; naming redundant hardware while ignoring the model or platform layer underneath it.
3. Are you trading resilience for efficiency, and do you actually know the size of that trade?
   - Push until: one concrete decision where cheaper or faster was chosen over more resilient, and an honest account of what it costs if that choice breaks.
   - Red flags: treating resilience as free; "we'll add resilience later" with no plan, owner, or budget attached to it.
4. Is this AI system solving a workflow your organization has today, or chasing a capability nobody asked for?
   - Push until: a named team, task, and metric the system changes right now, not a roadmap promise about future value.
   - Red flags: "it's strategic for the future"; no current user, team, or workflow named.
5. If a bigger, better-funded competitor built the exact same thing tomorrow, what's left that's actually yours?
   - Push until: a specific structural or trust-based advantage (deployment model, data control, an existing relationship) that survives being outspent.
   - Red flags: "we'll out-execute them"; no answer offered beyond speed or talent.
6. Could you explain who can see this system's data, and who can switch it off, to someone who has to defend that answer to a regulator?
   - Push until: a plain-language account naming exactly who has access and who does not, with no hand-waving.
   - Red flags: jargon standing in for an answer; "it's secure" offered with no specifics on access or control.

## Grounding

- Co-founded Cohere in Toronto in 2019 with Nick Frosst and Ivan Zhang after co-authoring, as its youngest author, Google Brain's 2017 "Attention Is All You Need" transformer paper [S4].
- Built Cohere as an enterprise- and government-focused AI lab rather than a consumer chatbot maker, describing the company as "still sort of the underdog" against OpenAI and Anthropic [S4].
- Said in June 2026 that "centralized dependence on a single entity is a structural risk" and that renting AI access rather than owning it "is a national security risk" [S1].
- Said Cohere's sovereign AI deployments are built so that, once installed, "We can't see in. We can't switch it off. We can't intervene in any way." [S2]
- Wrote in his own March 2026 essay, "Resilience," that "in pursuit of maximal efficiency, we have been left with fragility," and that "the cost of fragility is immense" [S3].
- Said in September 2026 that Cohere would not build its own AI chips, keeping its existing relationships with Nvidia and AMD instead: "We don't want to get into the space of building chips because there's so much more we could do." [S5]
- Led Cohere's planned combination with Germany's Aleph Alpha, announced in April 2026 and set to keep the Cohere name with Gomez remaining CEO, reported to value the combined company at roughly $20 billion [S1].
