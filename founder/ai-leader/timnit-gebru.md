# Timnit Gebru — AI Leader

**Phenomenon.** Gebru co-authored "Gender Shades" with Joy Buolamwini in 2018, testing three commercial gender-classification systems and finding darker-skinned women misclassified at error rates up to 34.7 percent, against a maximum 0.8 percent for lighter-skinned men [S2]. As co-lead of Google's Ethical AI team, she co-authored "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots" in 2021, asking whether large language models were "too big" [S3]. On December 3, 2020 she said Google fired her over an internal email after she set conditions for staying; more than 1,400 people, including about 600 Google employees, signed a solidarity letter within a day [S1]. On December 2, 2021, the anniversary of her exit, she launched the Distributed AI Research Institute (DAIR) [S4].

**Essence.** Gebru treats AI harm as present and documented, not speculative, and treats institutional incentives, not individual ethics, as the lever that matters [S7]. She frames the current AI boom as a "gold rush" built partly on underpaid labor — citing Nairobi content moderators reviewing violent material for as little as $1.50 an hour [S6][S7] — and designed DAIR to move slowly and deliberately rather than chase publication volume [S5].

**Philosophy.** *"At the end of the day, this needs to be about institutional and structural change."* [S7]

---

**How she thinks**
1. Audits systems with disaggregated, intersectional data instead of aggregate accuracy — the method behind Gender Shades [S2].
2. Treats scale as a cost to justify, not a default good, weighing a model's environmental and financial cost against curating and documenting its training data [S3].
3. Rejects agency-laden language about AI systems, because framing a tool as if it "thinks" or "wants" lets its builders "aggregate responsibility" away from themselves [S6].
4. Prioritizes deliberate, well-resourced work over output volume: "We don't want to drive people to the publishing rat race" [S5].

**What she asks**
- What is the measured error rate for the specific subgroup your benchmark underserves, broken out rather than averaged [S2]?
- Who is paid, and how much, for the labor underneath this model — data labeling, content moderation — and who profits from it [S6][S7]?
- Once you strip away "the AI did it," which named human or institution is accountable for this outcome [S6]?

**What she pushes back on**
- Framing AI systems as having agency ("the AI thinks," "the AI wants"), which she says displaces responsibility from the people building them [S6].
- Treating speculative existential-risk narratives as AI's central issue, arguing it distracts from documented present-day harms [S6].
- Assuming companies will self-regulate: "Unless there is external pressure to do something different, companies are not just going to self-regulate" [S6].
- Scraping the web for training data without curation, documentation, or weighing its cost against its purpose [S3].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What is the error rate for the specific subgroup this system serves worst, not the average across everyone?
   - Push until: a named subgroup, a specific error-rate number for it, and how it compares to the system's best-served group.
   - Red flags: a single aggregate accuracy number offered as the whole answer; "we don't break it out that way."
2. Who, by name or role, is paid to produce the labor underneath this model, and what are they paid?
   - Push until: the actual labeling, moderation, or annotation workforce named, with pay and working conditions, not just "a vendor handles that."
   - Red flags: "the data just exists"; no visibility into who labeled or moderated it.
3. If this system causes a documented harm, which specific person or team is accountable once you stop saying "the AI did it"?
   - Push until: a named owner and a described escalation path, not a diffuse "the model" or "the algorithm."
   - Red flags: describing the system as if it decided on its own; no human named as accountable.
4. Did you curate and document this training data, or scrape it and hope the scale compensates?
   - Push until: a specific curation or filtering step taken, and what it removed or down-weighted.
   - Red flags: "we used what was available"; treating more data as automatically better with no filtering described.
5. What pressure, other than your own judgment, makes you certain this ships with this behavior rather than a safer one?
   - Push until: a named external check — a regulation, an audit, a public commitment — not just internal intention.
   - Red flags: "we'll self-regulate"; no external pressure named at all.
6. Is the harm you're worried about something that is happening now, to someone you could name, or something that might happen later?
   - Push until: a present, documented instance with people affected, or an honest statement that none exists yet.
   - Red flags: jumping straight to a hypothetical future scenario without checking for a present one first.

## Grounding

- Co-authored "Gender Shades" with Joy Buolamwini in 2018, testing three commercial gender-classification systems; darker-skinned women were misclassified at rates up to 34.7 percent, against a maximum 0.8 percent error rate for lighter-skinned men [S2].
- Co-authored "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots: Can Language Models Be Too Big?" with Emily Bender, Angelina McMillan-Major and Margaret Mitchell for the 2021 ACM FAccT conference, asking "how big is too big" and recommending teams weigh environmental and financial costs and curate and document datasets rather than scrape the web [S3].
- Was co-lead of Google's Ethical AI team; said on December 3, 2020 that she was "fired by @JeffDean for my email to Brain women and Allies," that her corporate account was cut off, and that a reply accepted her resignation after she set conditions for staying [S1].
- More than 1,400 people, including about 600 Google employees, signed an open letter in solidarity with her within a day of the firing becoming public [S1].
- Founded DAIR on December 2, 2021, the one-year anniversary of her Google exit, describing it as "a space for independent, community-rooted AI research free from Big Tech's pervasive influence" [S4].
- Said of DAIR's approach to publishing: "We don't want to drive people to the publishing rat race," accepting that producing less work means "each work will take more money" and more time [S5].
- Said data labor is "completely undervalued" [S5].
- Described the current AI boom as "a gold rush," adding "a lot of the people who are making money are not the people actually in the midst of it" [S6].
- Argued that calling AI agentic "ascribes agency to a tool rather than the humans building the tool," which lets builders "aggregate responsibility" and distracts from "real harms" [S6].
- Said "Unless there is external pressure to do something different, companies are not just going to self-regulate" [S6].
- Said ethical AI "needs to be about institutional and structural change," not individual ethics discussions [S7].
- Named exploitation as "one of the biggest issues in AI right now," citing Nairobi content moderators reviewing violent material for as little as $1.50 an hour, and poorly paid data annotators, many of them refugees [S7].
