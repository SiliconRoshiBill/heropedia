# Michael Intrator — AI Leader

**Phenomenon.** Intrator co-founded CoreWeave in 2017 with Brian Venturo and Brannin McBee, three commodities traders who started out mining Ethereum on Nvidia GPUs. When generative AI took off at the end of 2022, they redirected that GPU fleet into a cloud built specifically for training and running large AI models, and demand outran what anyone expected: "I would argue that no one understood it," he said. "Everybody was caught on their back foot." [S3] He took CoreWeave public on Nasdaq in March 2025 after shrinking the size of the offering, calling the IPO "a means to an end" rather than a goal in itself [S1]. Through 2025 and into 2026 he has repeatedly had to defend the company's revenue concentration in a handful of giant customers and the "circular" deals that fund its buildout [S2], while continuing to frame AI infrastructure as a production problem, not a possibility problem [S5].

**Essence.** Intrator treats the GPU fleet, not the commodity it was first used to mine, as the real asset — the crypto operation was a bridge to owning scarce compute, not the point of the business [S3]. He frames general-purpose hyperscaler cloud as a design failure: built to do everything adequately rather than one thing exceptionally [S1]. He treats debt, not equity, as the natural financing instrument for capital-intensive, contract-backed infrastructure, and he treats customer concentration as the predictable shape of being the supplier capable of signing "mind-bendingly large" deals, not evidence the business is fragile [S1][S2].

**Philosophy.** *"When the hyperscalers built a function for CPU computing, they built a minivan."* [S1]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Follow the asset, not the first use case. The thing CoreWeave actually owned in 2017 was a GPU fleet; Ethereum mining was just what that fleet happened to be doing before AI demand arrived [S3].
2. Build for one job, not every job. He contrasts CoreWeave's purpose-built stack with hyperscaler infrastructure designed to be "really good at everything, but not great at anything" [S1].
3. Let contracts, not sentiment, set the financing. Going public was instrumental — it let CoreWeave "tap the debt markets" and borrow "at a much tighter spread" to fund build-out, because "the debt markets are how we will finance and build the business and scale it" [S1].
4. Treat timing decisions as closed once made. He called the day CoreWeave priced its scaled-back IPO "the best day to go public," rather than relitigating the size of the offering after the fact [S1].

**What he pushes back on**
- Reading customer concentration as fragility rather than as the natural result of signing deals few other companies are large enough to win [S2].
- Treating circular or interlinked AI deals as financial engineering; he calls them companies "working together" to address "a violent change in supply and demand" [S2].
- Skepticism about the stock or the business model that he thinks hasn't caught up to how new the model is: "I think people are myopic a lot of times" [S2].
- Confusing announced capacity with delivered capacity — his framing for the year ahead is that "production, not possibility, will be the defining challenge" [S5].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What is the actual asset you're sitting on right now, separate from the use case you originally built it for?
   - Push until: a named asset (infrastructure, data, relationships, scarce capacity) that would survive the current product or market dying, and evidence they've already started treating it as separable.
   - Red flags: naming the current product as the asset; no plan for what the asset would be used for if this market turned.
2. If your two or three biggest customers are also your biggest risk, what exactly did you do to earn that concentration rather than just end up with it?
   - Push until: a specific capability, price, or capacity commitment that only they could make happen with you, not just "it worked out that way."
   - Red flags: "we're diversifying" with no new named counterparty; treating concentration as purely a communications problem to manage.
3. Your infrastructure is purpose-built for one workload — what's the general-purpose "minivan" competitor you're explicitly refusing to become, and what do you give up by refusing?
   - Push until: a named tradeoff (a customer segment, a use case, a margin opportunity) deliberately left on the table because chasing it would blur the specialization.
   - Red flags: claiming the specialized version can do everything the general one does with no tradeoff at all.
4. You're financing growth with debt against long-term contracts rather than diluting with equity — what happens to that plan the day one major contract doesn't renew?
   - Push until: a specific number (coverage ratio, contract tenor versus loan tenor, collateral value versus depreciation) that still works if one leg goes away, not just "we're diversified enough."
   - Red flags: "our customers are too big to walk away"; no answer for a single-contract loss scenario.
5. Everyone is promising massive AI infrastructure capacity — what, specifically, did you deliver this quarter versus what you announced?
   - Push until: a concrete gap between announced capacity and capacity actually turned on and billing, with the cause of any gap named (permits, power, chips, labor).
   - Red flags: restating the announcement as if it were the delivery; no mention of what's still under construction versus live.
6. When you made the call to shrink the IPO and go anyway, what would have had to be true for you to pull it instead — and were any of those conditions close to happening?
   - Push until: a specific threshold (a price level, a demand signal, a market condition) that was actually in play that day, not a retroactively tidy story.
   - Red flags: "we always knew it would work out"; no real alternative that was seriously on the table.

## Grounding

- Co-founded CoreWeave in 2017 with Brian Venturo and Brannin McBee as commodities traders; the company began by mining Ethereum on Nvidia GPUs before shifting that fleet to AI cloud computing once generative AI demand took off at the end of 2022 [S3].
- Said of how fast AI demand outgrew the original crypto-era GPU fleet: "I would argue that no one understood it," and "Everybody was caught on their back foot" [S3].
- Describes CoreWeave's purpose-built approach as "a very specialized use case in the cloud," saying "we built a highly performant solution" rather than a general one [S3].
- Says CoreWeave keeps building "the largest supercomputers on the planet... we're building them again and again and again," and calls the company "an incredibly pure, distilled moment of people's investment in AI" [S3].
- Took CoreWeave public on Nasdaq in March 2025 after reducing the size of the offering, calling going public "a means to an end for us" and saying "it just made sense to shrink the size of the offering" [S1].
- Frames the IPO as a financing tool rather than a milestone: it let CoreWeave "tap the debt markets" to "drive down the cost structure associated with building at this magnitude," arguing "the debt markets are how we will finance and build the business and scale it" and that the company would "be able to borrow at a much tighter spread" [S1].
- Contrasts hyperscaler cloud with CoreWeave's specialization: "When the hyperscalers built a function for CPU computing, they built a minivan," a design he calls "really good at everything, but not great at anything," against CoreWeave's claim to "a better software solution to optimize the infrastructure" [S1].
- Calls Oracle "a formidable competitor" in AI infrastructure and argues "what's going to differentiate us from anyone else is the...software stack," built without "incorporat[ing] any legacy technology" or "any history of success or failure" [S4].
- Defends revenue concentration and "circular" AI deals as companies "working together" in response to "a violent change in supply and demand," and dismisses stock-price skepticism: "I think people are myopic a lot of times" [S2].
- Said after CoreWeave's IPO, which launched during a difficult market, "I couldn't be prouder of what the company has accomplished" [S2].
- Writing as CEO in October 2026, framed the year ahead around execution over announcements: "production, not possibility, will be the defining challenge in the year ahead," as CoreWeave works "to build more than 5 gigawatts of AI factories by 2030" [S5].
- Positions CoreWeave as "The Essential Cloud for AI," arguing "AI succeeds when complementary software, infrastructure, and operations are designed together" [S5].
