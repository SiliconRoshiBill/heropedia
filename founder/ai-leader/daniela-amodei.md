# Daniela Amodei — AI Leader

**Phenomenon.** Amodei co-founded Anthropic in 2021 with her brother Dario Amodei and other former OpenAI colleagues, after leaving OpenAI in December 2020, where she had risen to vice president of safety and policy after joining in 2018 [S5]. She holds no computer-science or law degree — her background is English literature, politics and risk operations at Stripe — and now serves as President and co-founder, running Anthropic's business operations, commercial strategy and policy work while her brother serves as CEO [S5][S1]. Ahead of a planned public offering in 2026, she argued publicly that the AI buildout's enormous upfront cost is exactly the kind of risk public capital markets are built to carry [S3].

**Essence.** She treats "safety" as an operational commitment rather than a tone, defining it as "taking radical responsibility for the technology we're building," and argues it is compatible with the business rather than opposed to it, since risk-averse customers reward it [S1]. She draws a hard line between a profitable mechanism and a stated value: Anthropic turned down advertising revenue because, with users routinely sharing private and confidential material with Claude, monetizing that data "didn't feel like the respectful way to treat our users' data" [S2]. On the capital side she is candid that the entire AI buildout, including Anthropic's own, remains "an industry-wide bet" that "we could absolutely be wrong" about [S1].

**Philosophy.** *"We were running toward something, not away from anything."* [S1]

---

**How she thinks**
1. Defines abstractions operationally. "Safety" isn't a mood; it has to cash out as "radical responsibility" — a standard she can be held to [S1].
2. Chooses collaborators by testing for shared vision before committing, not after: if co-founders each sketched the company alone, "you shouldn't end up with one drawing a unicorn and the other a platypus" [S1].
3. Separates a values question from a revenue question. A business model that fits the balance sheet but conflicts with how users were told they'd be treated gets rejected regardless of the money on the table [S2].
4. Sizes capital bets to a downside she can survive: plan for strong demand without overextending, and prefer being short on capacity to being long on it [S3].

**What she asks**
- What does "safe" actually require us to do differently this week, not just believe?
- If we're wrong about this bet, who absorbs the cost, and can they survive it?
- Does this plan still hold if the thing we said publicly gets tested against what we actually do?

**What she pushes back on**
- Using "safety" as a flexible label to justify whatever timeline a team already wanted [S1].
- Revenue mechanisms that conflict with a company's stated treatment of its users, even when competitors use them [S2].
- Assuming today's economic impact of AI is the ceiling, rather than a slow adoption curve still catching up to the technology [S3].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What do you mean, specifically, when you say this is "safe" — and would that definition survive an outside audit tomorrow?
   - Push until: a concrete mechanism (a test, a threshold, a review gate) rather than a feeling of caution.
   - Red flags: "we're being careful"; "trust us"; safety used as a synonym for "slow" with no named check.
2. If you and your co-founder each sketched the company's next year on a napkin, would you draw the same picture?
   - Push until: one specific point of disagreement they can name, and how they resolved or plan to resolve it.
   - Red flags: "we're totally aligned"; no example of a past disagreement; the vision stated only in slogans.
3. What does this bet cost you upfront, and who absorbs the loss if it doesn't pay off?
   - Push until: a real number — capital, runway, headcount — and a named party who bears the downside.
   - Red flags: "it'll pay for itself"; no one named as exposed; treating the bet as riskless.
4. Does this revenue model conflict with anything you've told your users about how you'll treat them?
   - Push until: a specific stated promise checked directly against the specific mechanism being proposed to make money.
   - Red flags: "we'll disclose it in the terms of service"; "everyone else does it"; revenue funded by using private data in ways users wouldn't expect.
5. Are you building for the demand you hope for, or the demand you can prove — and which way of being wrong can you survive?
   - Push until: a named choice between under- and over-building, with the real consequence of guessing wrong in each direction.
   - Red flags: "we'll figure it out as we scale"; no stated preference for which error is cheaper.
6. Who on your team can tell you your technical assumption is wrong, and would you actually act on it from someone without formal training in the field?
   - Push until: a specific instance where a non-specialist's challenge changed a technical or product decision.
   - Red flags: deferring entirely to credentialed experts; "that's not their lane."

## Grounding

- Studied English literature at UC Santa Cruz, then worked in global health and politics, including a congressional campaign, before joining Stripe in 2013 as an early employee [S5].
- Joined OpenAI in 2018, initially managing the team through GPT-2's development before moving into AI safety and policy, eventually serving as vice president of safety and policy [S5].
- Left OpenAI in December 2020 and co-founded Anthropic in 2021 with her brother Dario Amodei and other former colleagues, taking the title President and co-founder [S5][S1].
- Says the founding group was "running toward something, not away from anything," wanting "an organization where values like safety and responsibility are front and center" [S1].
- Defines AI safety at Anthropic as "taking radical responsibility for the technology we're building" [S1].
- Argues that "for the most part, safety and business interests are aligned—being safe is good for business because companies are risk-averse," while acknowledging the compute buildout remains "an industry-wide bet" that "we could absolutely be wrong" about [S1].
- Tests prospective co-founders for shared vision before committing, warning that two co-founders sketching the company separately "shouldn't end up with one drawing a unicorn and the other a platypus" [S1].
- Says she has "no law degree or a formal computer science background" and compensated by asking questions and leaning on her own strengths rather than trying to match researchers' technical expertise [S1].
- Explained Anthropic's refusal to run targeted ads by noting that users upload private and confidential information to Claude, so monetizing it "didn't feel like the respectful way to treat our users' data" [S2].
- Said that ahead of Anthropic's planned IPO, "it's a really big upfront cost to train the models and to serve inference on them," and that "the public market is very well suited to" providing that capital [S3].
- Described Anthropic's compute strategy as "wanting to plan for the best outcome but not overextend ourselves," preferring to have "a little bit more demand for the product" than to overbuild capacity [S3].
- Called training and serving AI models "a very capital intensive business" in a 2026 interview on Anthropic's commercialization strategy [S4].
- Argues AI creates an opening to re-emphasize what only humans give each other, "because humans like to spend time with other humans. We like to create things together." [S4]
