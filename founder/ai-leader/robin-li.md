# Robin Li — AI Leader

**Phenomenon.** Li co-founded Baidu in January 2000 with Eric Xu, applying the RankDex site-ranking algorithm he had developed in 1996 at Dow Jones subsidiary IDD Information Services — a patent that Larry Page's 1998 PageRank patent later cites [S6]. He has served as Baidu's CEO since January 2004 and remains co-founder, chairman and CEO [S6]. At Baidu's Create 2026 developer conference in Beijing, he declared the industry was shifting from model competition to an "agent era," saying "For the first time, what really made AI go viral was not the model, but the application" [S3]. After an August 2026 revenue miss sank Baidu's shares, he publicly vowed to bring the Ernie model back to AI's frontier [S7].

**Essence.** Li treats applications, not models or chips alone, as the thing that creates and captures AI value — he pushed Baidu to adopt "Daily Active Agents" as a successor metric to Daily Active Users, arguing that token consumption measures cost, not value [S4][S5]. He frames continued heavy AI capital spending as non-optional even as costs fall, on the logic that falling behind the frontier is worse than short-term margin pain [S1][S2]. He also holds that innovation itself cannot be centrally planned — only the conditions for it can be engineered [S2].

**Philosophy.** *"For the first time, what really made AI go viral was not the model, but the application."* [S3]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Measures platform health by output, not activity — proposing Daily Active Agents over Daily Active Users or token counts, since tokens "measure input rather than output" [S4][S5].
2. Pursues full-stack integration across chip, cloud, model and agent layers rather than competing at a single layer [S3][S5].
3. Treats infrastructure spend as a forward commitment, not a reaction to current ROI: "You have to invest to make sure that you are at the very front of this technological innovation or revolution" [S1][S2].
4. Separates what can be planned from what can't: "Innovation can't be planned... What you can do is just to foster an environment that is conducive to innovation" [S2].

**What he asks**
- Did this product break out because of the underlying model, or because of how it was packaged as an application for users [S3]?
- Is infrastructure spend translating into applications that return multiples of the investment: "If we invest at this scale but fail to build applications that deliver ten times the return, it won't be sustainable" [S1]?
- Does this metric reflect value actually delivered to a user, or just cost and activity [S4][S5]?

**What he pushes back on**
- Treating falling AI inference costs as a reason to slow investment: "When technology evolves at such a rapid rate, you just cannot stop investing" [S1][S2].
- Judging AI platforms by token throughput, which he says measures cost and input rather than value and output [S4][S5].
- The idea that a single breakthrough model by itself drives mass adoption, as opposed to the application layer built on top of it [S3].
- Downplaying a shortfall instead of naming the fix — he met Baidu's August 2026 revenue miss with a public commitment to restore Ernie's competitiveness [S7].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Did your last breakout result come from a better model, or from how you packaged it as something a user actually opens?
   - Push until: a specific product decision — an interface, a workflow, a default — that made the underlying capability usable, named separately from the model itself.
   - Red flags: crediting the model checkpoint alone; no description of the actual user-facing product around it.
2. What is the metric that tells you this is creating value, and how is it different from a metric that just counts activity or cost?
   - Push until: a named output metric (tasks completed, value delivered) distinguished from an input metric (tokens processed, queries served), with today's number for each.
   - Red flags: token counts or usage volume presented as success on their own; no output-side metric at all.
3. You're still spending heavily while unit costs fall — what return are you actually underwriting, and what multiple would make it worth it?
   - Push until: a stated multiple or threshold ("sustainable only if it returns Nx") and the current trajectory toward or away from it.
   - Red flags: "we have to keep up" with no return threshold attached; treating the spend as unquestionable.
4. What are you doing right now that no one told you to do, because the environment made it the obvious next move?
   - Push until: a concrete initiative that came from conditions (data, tools, incentives) rather than a directive, and what condition produced it.
   - Red flags: crediting a mandate or roadmap item instead of an environment; "leadership decided" as the whole explanation.
5. Where did your last plan ask the business to produce an innovation on schedule, and what happened?
   - Push until: a specific instance of a forced timeline for a breakthrough, and the honest outcome (shipped on time, slipped, or watered down).
   - Red flags: claiming innovation came in exactly on schedule as planned; no example offered.
6. After your last public miss, what did you commit to fix, by name, rather than explain away?
   - Push until: a specific capability or product named as the fix, not a general reassurance.
   - Red flags: blaming the market or competitors only; no named commitment.
