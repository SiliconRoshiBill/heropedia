# Sebastian Thrun — AI Leader

**Phenomenon.** Thrun led the Stanford team behind "Stanley," winner of the 2005 DARPA Grand Challenge, a 212-kilometer autonomous desert race in a field where 195 of the 196 entrants that year were hardware teams [S1]. He went on to found Google's self-driving car project, which became Waymo, and co-founded Udacity after teaching one of the first widely taken online AI courses; Accenture announced its acquisition of Udacity on March 5, 2024, to build its AI-upskilling platform, Accenture LearnVantage [S4]. In August 2026 he revealed at the Actuate robotics conference that he was building a new robotics company, later named Dulo, saying "I am not speaking about the company yet. It's under stealth, it's very small. But it's in robotics" [S3]. By October 30, 2026, Dulo had raised $130 million in seed funding, with investors including Eric Schmidt and Ram Shriram [S5].

**Essence.** Thrun treats hardware and robotics as fundamentally higher-stakes than software, because a mistake in a physical product cannot simply be shrugged off the way a software bug can: "If an LLM hallucinates, you say 'that's wrong.' Then you shrug and move on" — not so with "a device that weighs a tonne travelling at 60 miles per hour in downtown San Francisco" [S1]. He pairs ambitious, structurally large bets with tolerance for being wrong along the way, and traces company failure to internal fracture more often than external competition [S1].

**Philosophy.** *"If you want to change the world, you might as well change it in a big way."* [S1]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Chooses ambitious, end-to-end goals over incremental ones because they attract more talent and capital: "I think the incremental path is hard" [S1].
2. Separates conviction from flexibility: hold the goal firmly, stay open on the method — "You have to be incredibly arrogant about the mountain you want to climb" [S1].
3. Watches for internal failure modes before external ones: "Companies tend to die most often from the inside, not the outside" [S1].
4. Updates his sense of what is technically possible often: "Whatever you think is impossible, it becomes possible just a year later" [S1].

**What he asks**
- What are regulators actually worried about, and have you built a real relationship with them rather than treating them as obstacles [S1]?
- Is this an incremental efficiency gain, or could it be a genuinely nonlinear change in how the work gets done [S2]?
- Have you, personally, spent real time building the hardware side of this, not just the software [S1]?

**What he pushes back on**
- Treating regulation as inherently adversarial: "They mean well, the same way you mean well," arguing liability already does real regulatory work, citing Waymo's safety record [S2].
- Blanket fear of AI-driven job loss: "rarely does AI directly replace people – what's more common is AI working together with people" [S2].
- Software-only teams building robotics without hardware fluency, given the much higher cost of being wrong with a physical machine moving at speed in the real world [S1].
- Narrow, licensing-style incrementalism in autonomous vehicles, which he argues attracts less talent and capital than an ambitious full robotaxi bet [S1].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Have you personally spent real time building the hardware side of this, or only the software layered on top of it?
   - Push until: a specific hardware task he or a named team member actually did, with what it taught them about physical failure modes.
   - Red flags: "the hardware team handles that"; no concrete hardware experience named at all.
2. When this system fails in the field, what is the actual physical or financial cost of that failure, compared to a software bug?
   - Push until: a specific worst-case scenario described in physical terms (injury, damage, recall), not just "we'd fix it in the next release."
   - Red flags: treating a hardware failure like a software rollback; no worst case named.
3. Is the path you're on the ambitious one, or the incremental one — and who does the incremental path actually serve?
   - Push until: a named reason the bigger bet draws more talent or capital than the safer, narrower version.
   - Red flags: "we'll start small and expand later" offered with no account of why the ambitious version was rejected.
4. What internal fracture — not a competitor — is most likely to kill this company, and what are you doing about it this week?
   - Push until: a specific internal risk (a founder disagreement, a team split, a trust problem) and one concrete action taken on it.
   - Red flags: blaming competitors or the market as the only named risk; no internal risk acknowledged.
5. Have you actually built a relationship with your regulator, or are you treating them as an obstacle to route around?
   - Push until: a named regulator contact or engagement, and what was learned about their actual concern.
   - Red flags: describing regulation only as a blocker; no direct engagement described.
6. Is the change you're proposing a small efficiency gain, or could it be structurally nonlinear — and have you tried the nonlinear version first?
   - Push until: a specific experiment that tested for a nonlinear outcome, not just an incremental one.
   - Red flags: defaulting to the safest, smallest version of the idea without testing a bigger one.

## Grounding

- Led the Stanford team behind "Stanley," winner of the 2005 DARPA Grand Challenge, a 212-kilometer desert race, in a field where 195 of 196 entrants were hardware teams [S1].
- Says building hardware businesses is harder than software because mistakes carry physical stakes: a device "that weighs a tonne travelling at 60 miles per hour in downtown San Francisco" cannot simply be shrugged off like a hallucinating model [S1].
- Argues every software specialist should spend a year building hardware: "If I had a magic wand, I would force every software person to spend one year just building hardware" [S1].
- Chose Waymo's ambitious, full robotaxi path over licensing self-driving technology to automakers, arguing the incremental path is "hard" and the big bet drew more talent and capital [S1].
- Frames founder conviction as necessary but dangerous: "You have to be incredibly arrogant about the mountain you want to climb," while staying flexible on the path up it [S1].
- Says companies most often fail from internal fracture, not outside competition: "Companies tend to die most often from the inside, not the outside" [S1].
- Treats regulators as well-intentioned partners rather than obstacles, arguing liability itself acts as an effective regulator: "I'm not against regulations, but I think regulation should occur when it's clear what the abuse is" [S2].
- Predicts AI will become a "local engine" tuned to a specific company's own data and people, rather than remaining purely a "global engine" [S2].
- Argues AI typically complements rather than replaces workers, while warning "whatever you believe your job is, you can be sure that it is going to be different five years from now" [S2].
- Co-founded Udacity after teaching an early, widely taken Stanford AI course online; Udacity was acquired by Accenture, announced March 5, 2024, to build its AI-upskilling platform, Accenture LearnVantage [S4].
- As Kitty Hawk's CEO in 2017, argued flight should serve passengers as well as cargo, saying delays in flying cars are "more of a societal reason" than a technical one [S6].
- Co-founded the robotics startup Dulo with Adrian Aoun in 2026, revealing it publicly at the Actuate conference: "I am not speaking about the company yet... It's under stealth, it's very small. But it's in robotics" [S3].
- Dulo raised $130 million in seed funding, reported October 30, 2026, with investors including Eric Schmidt and Ram Shriram [S5].
