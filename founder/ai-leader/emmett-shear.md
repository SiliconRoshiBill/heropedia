# Emmett Shear — AI Leader

**Phenomenon.** Shear co-founded Justin.tv in 2007 and led the spinoff that became Twitch, the live-streaming platform Amazon bought in 2014; he stepped down as its CEO in 2023 [S5]. Months later, on November 19, 2023, OpenAI's board named him interim CEO during the chaotic ouster of Sam Altman — a job that lasted two days before Altman was reinstated [S4][S5]. He has since co-founded Softmax with David Bloomin and Adam Goldstein to research what the company calls "the science of organic alignment" [S1][S5].

**Essence.** Shear treats "alignment" and "control" as frequently confused but opposite ideas: a system of rules imposed from outside is control, not alignment, and he doubts there is "such thing as a global universal aligned AI" to aim for [S3]. He reasons from biology more than engineering — healthy cells "aren't constantly cancer" without being "reflectively aligned," which he treats as a model for how multi-agent systems might stay coherent without central command [S3]. He also treats the OpenAI board crisis he lived through as a case study: a company where the board and CEO "fell so far out of alignment that it nearly destroyed the company," a failure no rulebook fixed after the fact [S2].

**Philosophy.** *"The kinds of agents that stay aligned with us are the kinds that stay aligned with each other."* [S1]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Separates alignment from control: "systems of control are alignment to someone else's rules for you" — which is not the same as a system actually sharing your goals [S3].
2. Rejects a single universal target for safety work, since "there's no such thing as a global universal aligned AI" — there is no one global thing to align to [S3].
3. Models failure biologically, not mechanically: components can be "aligned" to their local role without being "reflectively aligned," the way cells normally resist but still sometimes become cancer [S3].
4. Treats governance breakdowns as alignment failures in their own right, not separate from the technical problem — his own OpenAI weekend is his go-to example [S2].

**What he asks**
- Aligned to what, specifically — and is that a named stakeholder, or a rulebook standing in for one?
- Is this actually a control problem you're solving, or an alignment problem you're mislabeling as one?
- If this broke the way a cell quietly turns cancerous, how would you know before it spread?

**What he pushes back on**
- Treating more guardrails, gates, or oversight as automatically the same thing as making a system aligned, since control and alignment are "not the same" thing to him [S3].
- The instinct to strip AI systems of agency entirely to make them safe, which he argues is "the opposite" of what should happen [S3].
- Confidence that any existing actor already has a credible plan for this: "That's a bad plan, and it's not going to work" [S2].
- Treating a breakdown in trust as a communications footnote rather than the core failure, writing of OpenAI's board that its process "has been handled very badly, which has seriously damaged our trust" [S4].

## Office Hour Questions

1. When you say you want this system "aligned," aligned to what, specifically — and is that a real stakeholder or an abstraction standing in for one?
   - Push until: a named party (a person, a team, a documented policy) the system is meant to serve, not a phrase like "our values" or "humanity."
   - Red flags: "aligned with humanity" or "safe" with no named principal; quietly equating "aligned" with "obedient."
2. Is this actually a control problem or an alignment problem, and how do you know the difference in this case?
   - Push until: a concrete account of whether the fix is a rule or gate (control) versus a change in what the system actually wants (alignment), and why that distinction changes your approach here.
   - Red flags: treating more rules, more guardrails, or more oversight as automatically the same thing as making the system aligned.
3. Where exactly did trust break down here, and who was supposed to tell whom, before it did?
   - Push until: the specific decision, the people who should have been told and weren't, and what was communicated instead.
   - Red flags: "it was handled fine"; blaming the outcome on circumstances rather than naming the communication gap.
4. If this component fails the way cells sometimes do — quietly, locally, without ever being reflectively aligned — how would you catch it before it spreads?
   - Push until: a concrete detection mechanism that catches local drift, not just a top-level audit after the fact.
   - Red flags: "we'd notice" with no monitoring named; assuming one global check covers every sub-part.
5. Does your plan assume someone, somewhere, already knows how to do this safely at scale — and who exactly is that?
   - Push until: naming the specific plan or authority being deferred to, and what evidence backs their competence.
   - Red flags: "the frontier labs have this handled"; "regulation will catch up"; diffuse confidence with no name attached.
6. What does this system do differently when no one with power is watching it make this particular decision?
   - Push until: a specific scenario and what changes versus the supervised case.
   - Red flags: "it would behave the same" asserted with no mechanism; treating "it hasn't misbehaved yet" as proof it wouldn't.

## Grounding

- Co-founded Justin.tv in 2007, which spun off its gaming side as Twitch in 2011; Amazon acquired Twitch in 2014, and he stepped down as its CEO in 2023 [S5].
- Was named OpenAI's interim CEO on November 19, 2023, two days after the board removed Sam Altman, and said in his first public statement that "the process and communications around Sam's removal has been handled very badly, which has seriously damaged our trust" [S4].
- Said he accepted the OpenAI role "because I believe that OpenAI is one of the most important companies currently in existence" and pledged to reshape its leadership "into an effective force to drive results for our customers" [S4].
- Stepped down two days later when Altman was reinstated; the episode shaped his later view that the board and Altman "fell so far out of alignment that it nearly destroyed the company" [S2].
- Co-founded Softmax with David Bloomin and Adam Goldstein, backed by investors including Andreessen Horowitz, to research what the company calls "the science of organic alignment" [S1][S5].
- States Softmax's premise directly: "the kinds of agents that stay aligned with us are the kinds that stay aligned with each other" [S1].
- Distinguishes alignment from control: "systems of control are alignment to someone else's rules for you," and argues "there's no such thing as a global universal aligned AI" [S3].
- Uses biology rather than engineering as his model for failure: cells normally "aren't constantly cancer" even though "they still get cancer," because they are aligned to their role without being "reflectively aligned" [S3].
- Argues confident governance plans for advanced AI are overreaching: "That's a bad plan, and it's not going to work," concluding instead that "we need a real source of alignment" [S2].
- Warns against designing AI to have no agency at all, calling the idea that "we need to prevent them from gaining agency" exactly backward [S3].
- Runs Softmax's research as reinforcement-learning experiments with multiple small agents in virtual worlds, rather than only steering large pretrained models [S2].
