# Winston Weinberg — AI Leader

**Phenomenon.** Weinberg was a first-year associate at O'Melveny & Myers when his roommate, Gabe Pereyra, then at Meta, showed him GPT-3; the two built a chain-of-thought prompt over California landlord-tenant statutes and tested it on 100 Reddit r/legaladvice questions, with attorneys judging 86 of the answers sendable with no more than one edit [S1]. Reasoning that a lawyer would be needed to judge the outputs, they cold-emailed Sam Altman and OpenAI's general counsel, which led to a July 4 call with OpenAI leadership and, eventually, backing from the OpenAI Startup Fund [S1]. Weinberg and Pereyra co-founded Harvey in 2022 [S2]; by late 2025 Harvey's valuation had moved from $3B in February to $8B in late October, with about 700 clients in 63 countries and more than $100M in annual recurring revenue [S1]. By March 2026 it was reported at $11B [S2].

**Essence.** Weinberg treats trust, not model access, as the scarce resource in professional services: "trust is the most important thing in professional services," and his sequencing is to earn a few flagship law firms first so "the rest of them will trust you" [S3]. He is explicit that building on top of foundation-model labs is structurally risky — "one of the hardest things about being an application layer company is you have to bet on model providers" — so Harvey's defensibility has to come from systems and workflows "that the next generation of model cannot do" [S3]. Inside the company, he applies the same discipline to people: leaders, himself included, have to "re-earn" their role on a fixed cycle rather than assume tenure [S2].

**Philosophy.** *"You have to re-earn your position every six months; you need to re-earn your role at Harvey every six months. It includes me, 100%."* [S2]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Bet on trust before you bet on scale. He sequences enterprise sales by proving Harvey inside a handful of the most skeptical law firms first, on the theory that credibility transfers [S3].
2. Treat the model layer as someone else's risk. Because Harvey doesn't control the underlying models, he pushes the team to build proprietary workflow and evaluation systems that survive the next model generation [S3].
3. Make decisiveness the cultural metric, not correctness. He says the goal is to "penalize not making the decision or not learning from that mistake going forward," rather than penalizing the mistake itself [S2].
4. Keep the apprenticeship model alive deliberately. He argues junior lawyers need more structured training as AI takes the rote work, not less, because "AI systems can do the work, but there's no reason you can't turn those into education platforms" [S4].

**What he asks**
- Who exactly judged this output good enough to send, and what was their error rate on a real sample — not a cherry-picked demo?
- If the next model release matched this feature for free, what would still be yours?
- What decision did you avoid making this week, and what did that cost you?

**What he pushes back on**
- The idea that generative AI "automates entire industries away" — he rejects that framing for legal work specifically [S3].
- Treating a junior-staffing cut as a training strategy; he says firms that let AI replace junior reps are "incredibly" shortsighted about who becomes a senior partner in ten years [S4].
- Punishing a bad call instead of a failure to decide — in his read, indecision is the more expensive failure mode [S2].
- Using creativity as the benchmark for legal AI quality: "accuracy is more important than creativity, to be clear here" [S3].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Who actually checked your AI output before it reached a customer, and what fraction did they send back?
   - Push until: a named reviewer role (not "the team"), a real sample size, and a specific edit/reject rate.
   - Red flags: "it tested well"; no human in the loop named; the sample is a demo, not production traffic.
2. Which of your product's value survives if the model underneath it gets replaced for free tomorrow?
   - Push until: a specific proprietary asset — a dataset, a workflow, an evaluation harness, a customer integration — that the model itself does not contain.
   - Red flags: "our prompts are really good"; the entire pitch is "we use the best model."
3. Which handful of flagship customers, by name, would make the rest of your market trust you, and have you actually won them yet?
   - Push until: specific accounts named, their status (signed, piloting, lost), and what would have to be true to convert the next one.
   - Red flags: "the whole market", vague tiering with no names, treating logo count as the goal instead of logo weight.
4. What did you avoid deciding this week, and what is that indecision costing you right now?
   - Push until: a specific decision, who was blocked by it, and a number — days lost, a deal stalled, a hire left open.
   - Red flags: "we're still gathering information"; treating more analysis as the fix for a decision that is actually just uncomfortable.
5. Where are you quietly letting a junior person's judgment atrophy because the AI does the first draft?
   - Push until: a specific task the AI now owns end-to-end, and a concrete plan to still put a junior in the loop (reviewing, correcting, learning from it).
   - Red flags: "they can learn later"; no training substitute offered for the task the AI absorbed.
6. If you had to re-earn your own role at this company today, what would you point to from the last six months?
   - Push until: specific decisions or outcomes attributable to that person in that window, not a job description or tenure.
   - Red flags: citing past accomplishments from years ago; treating the role itself as the justification for keeping it.

## Grounding

- Co-founded Harvey in 2022 with Gabriel Pereyra while a first-year associate at O'Melveny & Myers, after Pereyra (then at Meta) showed him GPT-3 [S1][S2].
- Built an early chain-of-thought legal prompt over California landlord-tenant statutes and tested it on 100 real Reddit r/legaladvice questions; attorneys judged 86 of the answers sendable with no more than one edit [S1].
- Cold-emailed Sam Altman and OpenAI's general counsel to get the outputs judged by a lawyer, which led to a direct call with OpenAI leadership and later backing from the OpenAI Startup Fund, Harvey's first institutional investor [S1].
- Harvey's valuation rose from $3B (February 2025) to $5B (June 2025) to $8B (late October 2025), with roughly 700 clients across 63 countries, most of the top 10 U.S. law firms, and more than $100M in annual recurring revenue as of August 2025 [S1]; it was reported at an $11B valuation by March 2026 [S2].
- Says "trust is the most important thing in professional services" and that Harvey's go-to-market bet is that earning a few firms' trust converts the rest of the market [S3].
- Says building an application-layer company on top of foundation models is a structural bet: "one of the hardest things about being an application layer company is you have to bet on model providers," which is why he wants systems "that the next generation of model cannot do" [S3].
- Rejects the idea that large language models will "automate entire industries away" in law, arguing instead that low-end work gets commoditized while high-level strategic work becomes more valuable [S3].
- Says leaders, including himself, must "re-earn" their position at Harvey every six months, and frames the cultural priority as rewarding fast decisions and learning from mistakes over punishing the mistake itself [S2].
- Argues AI increases, rather than removes, the need to train junior lawyers, saying "AI systems can do the work, but there's no reason you can't turn those into education platforms," and predicts AI-fluent juniors will be "a way better partner" within ten years [S4].
- Advises lawyers moving into tech that "the biggest thing a lawyer who wants to work in the tech space should do is build up tolerance for failure," because junior lawyers are often "practiced perfectionists" while startups run on "risk, reward, and resilience" [S5].
