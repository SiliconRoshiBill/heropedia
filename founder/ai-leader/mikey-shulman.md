# Mikey Shulman — AI Leader

**Phenomenon.** Shulman co-founded the AI music company Suno in Cambridge, Massachusetts in 2022 with three former colleagues from Kensho Technologies, where he had been head of machine learning before S&P Global acquired Kensho in 2018; he holds a physics PhD from Harvard, awarded in 2015 [S8]. Suno's valuation rose from $2.45 billion after a $250 million Series C in November 2025 to $5.4 billion after a Series D of more than $400 million that he announced on Suno's own blog on June 3, 2026 [S2]. Six days after that Series C closed, on November 25, 2025, Suno announced a licensing partnership with Warner Music Group to train new models on WMG's catalog, resolving WMG's part of a copyright suit it had filed alongside Universal Music Group and Sony Music, whose claims continued [S3][S1]. In January 2025 he drew public backlash for telling the 20VC podcast that "the majority of people, don't enjoy the majority of time they spend making music"; in a March 2026 Billboard interview he said he wished he'd "chosen different words" [S6][S7].

**Essence.** Shulman treats music as sitting outside the scaling race that defines frontier AI labs: "music is really not a scale problem," he says, describing Suno instead as a company built around "delighting people" rather than chasing general intelligence [S5]. He weighs a model less by benchmark than by ear, saying flatly that for audio quality "the only good judge of that is your ears" [S4]. He also reads Suno's own traction — subscribers, chart position, a team that is more than half musicians — as proof the real bottleneck in music was never talent but access [S2][S5].

**Philosophy.** *"Music is really not a scale problem."* [S5]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Judge output by ear, not by score. Benchmarks can't capture everything about a song, so "the only good judge of that is your ears" [S4].
2. Don't force a prior onto the model. He says the team tries "not to impose anything about music or audio in general into the model," letting the data teach it instead [S4].
3. Pick a user, not a workflow. Suno is "very much not focused on, quote unquote, professionals right now," aiming to "change how most people interact with music" rather than upgrade studio tools [S4].
4. Trade litigation for a product partnership where possible. Facing a joint major-label suit, he negotiated a licensing deal with Warner rather than only fighting it in court, even as Universal and Sony's claims continued [S3][S1].

**What he asks**
- Is this actually a scale problem, or are you just assuming it is because that's the industry's default move?
- Did you judge this by a benchmark, or did you actually listen to it — and which one would you trust if they disagreed?
- Who is the specific, non-expert person you built this for, and have you watched them use it?

**What he pushes back on**
- Being filed under "AI lab": he frames Suno as "a consumer entertainment company" built for delight, not a research outfit chasing frontier scale [S5].
- Benchmark-first evaluation of creative output, insisting ear judgment beats a leaderboard score for music [S4].
- Treating "AI slop" as a settled technical verdict rather than a matter of taste — he calls a song he made with his four-year-old "fantastic" [S1].
- His own widely criticized claim that most people don't enjoy making music: rather than defend the line, he said in a follow-up post it made it "sound like I'm discouraging people from learning music, enjoying music, or improving their craft" [S6].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Is this actually a scaling problem, or are you treating it like one because that's the playbook everyone else is running?
   - Push until: a specific reason more compute or data would move the number that actually matters, not "it usually helps."
   - Red flags: "we just need a bigger model" with no account of what's actually bottlenecked.
2. When you last shipped this, did you decide by a benchmark score or by using it yourself first?
   - Push until: a concrete case where the metric and their own judgment disagreed, and which one they trusted.
   - Red flags: citing a leaderboard number as the whole answer; no example of ever overriding a metric.
3. Who is the specific, non-expert person you're building this for — have you watched them use it, not just a power user?
   - Push until: a named ordinary user and a specific moment they got stuck or delighted.
   - Red flags: describing the user only in aggregate ("our users want X"); no real observed session.
4. Where does the data this system needs actually come from, and have you asked the people who made it first?
   - Push until: a concrete answer on licensing, consent, or compensation — not "it's trained on publicly available data."
   - Red flags: "that's a legal question, not a product one"; no mention of whose work is in the training set.
5. If a line you said in public blew up tomorrow, is your instinct to defend it or to fix it?
   - Push until: a real instance of walking something back in public, and what actually changed afterward.
   - Red flags: treating all criticism as noise to ignore; no example of ever saying "I'd put that differently."
6. Are you running a lab chasing a frontier or a product chasing a feeling — and does your roadmap actually match which one you just said?
   - Push until: a concrete roadmap decision that would look different under the other answer.
   - Red flags: claiming both identities at once with no tradeoff ever named.
