# Nat Friedman — AI Leader

**Phenomenon.** Friedman co-founded Ximian and later Xamarin before Microsoft acquired Xamarin, then served as CEO of GitHub from 2018 through 2021 [S1]. In 2017 he and Daniel Gross launched AI Grant, a program that handed cash plus cloud and compute credits directly to AI researchers without traditional credentialing [S5], and the two went on to run the venture firm NFDG, whose holdings have included a stake in Safe Superintelligence [S3]. In June 2025, Meta moved to hire both Gross and Friedman as part of its AI push and took a stake in NFDG in the process [S6][S3]. Mark Zuckerberg's internal memo announcing Meta Superintelligence Labs (MSL), reported June 30, 2025, said Friedman would "partner with Alex [Wang] to lead MSL, heading our work on AI products and applied research," after a year already spent on Meta's internal AI advisory group [S4].

**Essence.** Friedman invests and operates like someone who thinks markets and organizations default to being wrong-sized and wrong-paced. He distrusts consensus as a signal — "I have learned to trust my enthusiasm" over the crowd [S2] — and on his own site calls the efficient market hypothesis "a lie," preferring to model the world as roughly 500 consequential people rather than 8 billion anonymous ones [S1]. At GitHub he ran the same way: on his first day as CEO he had the team "pick one thing from this list and fix it by the end of the day and ship that, just one thing," rather than plan first [S2]. He treats headcount the same way he treats data — as something to cut toward signal, not pad for comfort: "smaller teams are better," with "faster decisions, fewer meetings, more fun" [S1].

**Philosophy.** *"I just fundamentally don't believe the world is efficient."* [S2]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Assume the market is wrong before assuming it's right: "the efficient market hypothesis is a lie," and the best outcomes live precisely where it fails [S1].
2. Bias to speed over planning — "Slow is fake" — and prove it by shipping one concrete fix the same day rather than scoping a roadmap first [S1][S2].
3. Keep teams small and the bar high: "smaller teams are better," and he'd rather pay fewer top people more than carry mediocre hires for political reasons [S1].
4. Put resources straight into the hands of builders: AI Grant and NFDG moved cash, cloud credits and compute directly to individual researchers and founders, skipping conventional diligence [S5][S3].

**What he asks**
- If you could only fix one thing today and ship it by end of day, what would it be — and why isn't it already shipped?
- Where does this plan assume the market is efficient, that someone else would already be doing it if it worked?
- Does the user still open this a week after the demo, or did the demo do all the convincing?
- How many people here are on this team because the work needs them, versus because splitting the work felt fair?

**What he pushes back on**
- Treating a strong demo as proof of value: "Every AI product has a fantastic demo," so he looks past it to whether the median new user keeps coming back [S2].
- The assumption that bigger teams are safer: he argues most tech companies are substantially overstaffed and that mediocre people have no place on a team [S1].
- The idea that acquisitions preserve value by default — "most acquisitions are destructive of value" — which is why he ran GitHub to prove it still "cares about developers" after Microsoft bought it [S2].
- The cultural taboo against micromanagement, which he calls harmful, arguing strong individuals should be fully empowered to use their own judgment even at the cost of some mistakes [S1].

## Office Hour Questions

1. If you could only fix one thing today and ship it by the end of the day, what would it be — and why isn't it already shipped?
   - Push until: one concrete, scoped fix, named, with the actual blocker identified (review, approval, politics) rather than "the backlog."
   - Red flags: a long list of priorities with none chosen; blaming process instead of naming who or what is actually in the way.
2. Where in this plan are you assuming the market is efficient — that someone else would already be doing this if it worked?
   - Push until: a specific reason the opportunity is still open (access, timing, taste, information) rather than "no one's thought of it."
   - Red flags: treating a lack of competitors as proof the idea is bad, or as proof it's great, with no actual mechanism given either way.
3. People loved the demo. Do they still open the product a week later?
   - Push until: real retention or repeat-usage numbers, separate from first-session reaction, and a clear account of what drives any drop-off.
   - Red flags: citing the demo reaction itself as the success metric; no data past day one.
4. How many people are on this team because the work genuinely needs them, versus because splitting it up felt fair?
   - Push until: a role-by-role account of what each person uniquely unblocks, and an honest answer about what shrinks if the weakest one leaves.
   - Red flags: "everyone's busy" offered as justification; refusal to name who adds the least.
5. Is this your enthusiasm, or the room's enthusiasm?
   - Push until: a specific, personal reason the builder is pulled toward this, independent of whether anyone else is excited yet.
   - Red flags: consensus, hype, or investor interest cited as the reason to proceed; no personal conviction offered.
6. If this team had to get five times smaller tomorrow, whose judgment would you trust to keep running it alone?
   - Push until: a named person and a believable account of what they'd actually do with that much ownership.
   - Red flags: "that's not how orgs work" as a dodge; no one named, or everyone named out of fairness rather than capability.
