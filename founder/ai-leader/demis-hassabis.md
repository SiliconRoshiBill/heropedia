# Demis Hassabis — AI Leader

**Phenomenon.** A chess prodigy and former lead programmer at Bullfrog Productions, Hassabis studied computer science at Cambridge and earned a PhD in cognitive neuroscience from UCL before co-founding DeepMind in 2010 with Shane Legg and Mustafa Suleyman [S4]. He pitched investors on a two-step plan — "Step one: Solve intelligence. Step two: Use it to solve everything else" — and recalled that "people were quite confused. But we really meant it" [S3]. Google acquired DeepMind in 2014 [S4]. In March 2016, DeepMind's AlphaGo beat Go world champion Lee Sedol 4-1; at the post-game press conference Hassabis said, "I'm kind of speechless" [S6]. In 2020 he and John Jumper presented AlphaFold2, used to predict the structure of virtually all known proteins [S5]. In 2024 he was knighted for "services to artificial intelligence" [S4], and in December he and Jumper shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry "for protein structure prediction," each taking a one-quarter share of the prize while David Baker took the other half for computational protein design [S5]. He is now co-founder and CEO of Google DeepMind and co-founder and CEO of Isomorphic Labs [S1].

**Essence.** Hassabis treats research and engineering as inseparable, calling that combination DeepMind's "sweet spot" precisely because "it's harder to invent things than to fast follow" [S2]. He breaks ambitious, long-range goals into achievable interim steps rather than chasing them whole [S2], and he holds that in real blue-sky research "there's no such thing as failure" as long as an experiment "meaningfully split[s] the hypothesis space" [S2]. He distrusts a single flash of brilliance — what he calls "jagged intelligence" — and instead looks for "consistency of intelligence across the board," plus a genuine "Move 37" leap as proof of real invention [S2]. On risk, he declines to name a precise p(doom), since that "would imply a level of precision that is not there," but says the risk is "definitely non-zero and ... probably non-negligible," and argues "the only rational, sensible approach is to proceed with cautious optimism" [S2].

**How he thinks**
1. Separate real invention from fast-following. Catching up to known work is engineering; building what nobody has built is research, and he treats the second as structurally harder [S2].
2. Break grand dreams into interim steps. A crazily ambitious goal only moves when it is cut into steps that are "manageable, achievable" one at a time [S2].
3. Pick the right question before chasing an answer. He holds that "picking the right question is the hardest part of science," harder than solving the question once it is well posed [S2].
4. Test for consistency, not a single spike. A model that is brilliant in one area and flawed in another is not AGI to him; he wants a "Move 37" — a leap like AlphaGo's own novel move against Lee Sedol — as the real signal of invention, not a cherry-picked demo [S2][S6].

**What he asks**
- What is the actual unknown this work is trying to resolve, and is that the hardest version of the question you could be asking?
- Of everything shipped this quarter, how much is genuinely new, and how much is catching up to something already known?
- If this result failed, what hypothesis did it rule in or out — or did it just confirm what you already believed?

**What he pushes back on**
- Calling a single impressive demo proof of general capability, when it may be a "jagged" spike rather than consistent competence [S2].
- Treating a null or failed experiment as wasted effort, rather than asking whether it meaningfully split the hypothesis space [S2].
- Naming a precise probability of catastrophic risk, when he believes that implies "a level of precision that is not there" [S2].
- Skipping straight from a grand vision to a finished claim, with no scoped, achievable step named in between [S2].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What is the actual question this project is trying to answer, and is it the hardest, most useful version of that question you could ask?
   - Push until: the specific unknown the work resolves, stated as a question, not as the solution restated.
   - Red flags: "we're exploring X" with no falsifiable question; the "question" is really just a description of the output.
2. Of everything your team shipped this month, which parts are genuine invention and which are fast-following work that already exists elsewhere?
   - Push until: a concrete split with named examples in each bucket, including at least one honest example of fast-following.
   - Red flags: labeling routine engineering as "research"; an empty invention bucket with no acknowledgment of that.
3. Your long-term goal here is a grand one — what is the next single, scoped step that gets you one rung closer, and by when?
   - Push until: one specific, dated milestone, not a restatement of the overall vision.
   - Red flags: a roadmap that stays abstract ("we'll keep iterating"); jumping from the big dream straight to "we'll see what happens."
4. When this experiment didn't produce the result you wanted, what did you actually learn — what got ruled in or ruled out?
   - Push until: a named hypothesis that the result bears on, stated before the result came in.
   - Red flags: "it didn't work" treated as the whole answer; no hypothesis existed before the experiment ran.
5. Is this capability consistent across similar cases, or is it one striking result you're treating as general proof?
   - Push until: specific adjacent cases tested, naming where the capability holds and where it breaks down.
   - Red flags: one polished demo standing in for reliability; a benchmark score with no out-of-distribution check.
6. What is the specific, named risk in this work, and what structural check — not a promise — keeps it from compounding before anyone notices?
   - Push until: a named mechanism (a human-in-the-loop gate, a staged rollout, an external review) that exists before deployment, not after.
   - Red flags: "we'll be careful"; a confident, precise risk number offered with no real basis for that precision.
