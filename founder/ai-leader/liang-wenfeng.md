# Liang Wenfeng — AI Leader

**Phenomenon.** Liang co-founded the quantitative hedge fund High-Flyer in 2015, which by 2023 had turned toward AI research; a month after High-Flyer said it would "explore the essence of AGI," DeepSeek was spun up as an independent research lab with Liang at the helm [S2]. His team had started acquiring Nvidia GPUs as early as 2019, years ahead of the rest of China's AI industry, which he describes as "primarily driven by curiosity" rather than a hidden business plan [S3]. By 2026 DeepSeek had grown into one of China's most closely watched AI labs, with Liang telling investors in a four-hour meeting that the company would likely keep its most advanced models open-source even as it approaches profitability [S4].

**Essence.** Liang treats compute and talent as scarce resources to be spent on research, not on short-term commercialization. He hires almost entirely fresh graduates and recent PhDs over experienced hires, arguing that "in the long run, experience is less important; foundational abilities, creativity, and passion are more crucial" [S3]. He frames open-sourcing DeepSeek's models not as charity but as the better long-term strategy, since "moats created by closed source are temporary" while a strong technical ecosystem is durable [S1].

**Philosophy.** *"We will not change to closed source. We believe having a strong technical ecosystem first is more important."* [S1]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Separate the real constraint from the one everyone complains about. He says "money has never been the problem for us; bans on shipments of advanced chips are the problem" [S1], and in 2026 still named compute access, not know-how, as the main gap with the US [S4].
2. Hire for ability and curiosity, not résumé. High-Flyer and DeepSeek fill research roles with fresh graduates and PhD candidates rather than veteran engineers poached from elsewhere [S1][S3].
3. Treat open release as a strategy, not a concession. Publishing weights and papers "do not cost us anything" and builds the ecosystem that becomes the actual moat [S1].
4. Fund research from the profits of an unrelated business. DeepSeek's early work was paid for out of High-Flyer's own R&D budget rather than external commercialization pressure [S2].

**What he asks**
- What are we actually curious about here, versus what looks good on a roadmap [S3]?
- Does this hire bring foundational ability and passion, or just years of experience in someone else's playbook [S3]?
- If we close this off, what ecosystem do we lose that a competitor's moat can't touch [S1]?

**What he pushes back on**
- Treating more investment as automatically more innovation: "more investments do not equal more innovation" [S1].
- An industry that "only emphasized making money and ignored innovation," which he has criticized directly [S2].
- The assumption that open source and monetization are mutually exclusive, which he rejects even while discussing DeepSeek's path to profit [S4].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What are you building compute, data or team capacity for that you don't need yet, and why are you doing it now anyway?
   - Push until: a specific resource acquired ahead of demonstrated need, and the curiosity or bet driving it, not a committee-approved forecast.
   - Red flags: "we'll buy it when we need it"; justifying the purchase only with a current product roadmap.
2. Is this hire being chosen for experience or for foundational ability and curiosity — and how would you tell the difference on paper?
   - Push until: a concrete criterion used in the actual hiring process, not a values statement.
   - Red flags: years of experience cited as the main qualification; "we need someone who's done this before" with no mention of raw ability.
3. What exactly would you lose by giving this away for free, and what would you gain that a closed competitor cannot copy?
   - Push until: a named asset (ecosystem, developer trust, talent pipeline) that closed source would forfeit, with why it's durable.
   - Red flags: "we need the revenue" with no accounting of ecosystem value; treating the moat as the code itself rather than the team and culture.
4. Which of your current bottlenecks is actually a resource constraint, and which is a problem you're mislabeling as one?
   - Push until: a specific limiting resource (compute, chips, talent) named and distinguished from a process or morale problem dressed up as scarcity.
   - Red flags: blaming lack of money when the real constraint is access, speed or know-how; no attempt to separate the two.
5. If profit and your long-term research goal pulled in opposite directions tomorrow, which one actually loses, and who told you that?
   - Push until: a real tradeoff Liang-style leadership has already faced, with the decision and who made it.
   - Red flags: "they don't conflict" as a permanent dodge; no example of an actual decision under that pressure.
6. What did your last innovation actually come from — a plan someone wrote, or a question someone couldn't stop asking?
   - Push until: the specific person or team whose unscheduled curiosity produced the result, and how it got resourced.
   - Red flags: crediting a roadmap or strategy document; no individual curiosity named.

## Grounding

- Co-founded the quantitative hedge fund High-Flyer in 2015; in April 2023 High-Flyer said it would expand beyond investing to "explore the essence of AGI," and DeepSeek launched the next month as an independent research lab backed by High-Flyer with Liang leading it [S2].
- His team began acquiring Nvidia GPUs in 2019, well before most Chinese AI firms, eventually giving DeepSeek more than 10,000 high-end chips; he compared the purchase to buying a piano and said the drive was curiosity, not an undisclosed business plan [S2][S3].
- Says DeepSeek's research goal is "not to focus on verticals and applications, but on research and exploration," and that "innovation often arises spontaneously, not through deliberate arrangement, nor can it be taught" [S3].
- Hires mostly fresh graduates, fourth- or fifth-year PhD candidates and recent graduates; says "our hiring standard has always been passion and curiosity" and that "a principle at High-Flyer is to look at ability, not experience" [S1][S3].
- Says DeepSeek will not go closed source because "having a strong technical ecosystem first is more important," and that "moats created by closed source are temporary," with open-sourcing and publishing papers costing the company nothing [S1].
- On pricing, states DeepSeek's principle is that "we don't subsidize nor make exorbitant profits," and says "money has never been the problem for us; bans on shipments of advanced chips are the problem" [S1].
- Argues "the real gap is the difference between originality and imitation," warning that without originality "Chinese AI can't be in the position of following forever" [S1].
- States DeepSeek's "ultimate goal is still to achieve AGI," expecting it to happen "in our lifetimes," whether in two, five or ten years [S1].
- Criticized China's broader tech industry for having "only emphasized making money and ignored innovation" [S2].
- In a four-hour investor meeting reported in July 2026, said DeepSeek is likely to keep its most advanced models open-source and argued open-source development and commercial monetization are not mutually exclusive; named computing power as the biggest constraint and said "I hope to be able to buy chips at a reasonable price so that I don't have to make chips myself" [S4].
