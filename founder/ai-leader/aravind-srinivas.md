# Aravind Srinivas — AI Leader

**Phenomenon.** Srinivas earned a PhD in computer science at UC Berkeley's BAIR lab under Pieter Abbeel, interned at OpenAI during the summer GPT's approach was taking shape, then worked there a year after finishing his doctorate [S2]. In August 2022 he co-founded Perplexity, building the citation habit of academic papers into a product: "Ph.D. students write papers, and for every sentence they write, they reference another paper" [S2]. He insists Perplexity is not a search engine: "Perplexity is best described as an answer engine. You ask it a question, you get an answer" [S1]. The company's valuation ran from about $1 billion in April 2024 to $9 billion that December to reported talks near $18 billion by March 2025, on roughly $100 million of annualized revenue [S5]. In July 2025 it shipped the Comet browser as what it calls agentic search, even as publishers including Dow Jones, the New York Post and later others sued it over how it uses their content [S3][S4].

**Essence.** Srinivas treats an answer as the start of a conversation, not the end of one: "The journey doesn't end once you get an answer. In my opinion, the journey begins after you get an answer" [S1]. He refuses to fight Google on Google's terms, locating the real opening in interface design rather than ranking: "The disruption comes from rethinking the whole UI itself" [S1]. He also drew an explicit line around what Perplexity would build itself, choosing to use other labs' models rather than train frontier ones from scratch, on the logic that "you either raise $10 billion or you don't do this thing at all" [S6].

**Philosophy.** *"I think of Perplexity as a knowledge discovery engine, neither a search engine."* [S1]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Judge a feature by whether it works, not by which model powers it: "What matters is, the answer works" [S1].
2. Stay inside your circle of competence. Perplexity builds the product layer and "decided to use other people's models and shape them to be really good" rather than race to train its own frontier model [S6].
3. Attack the interface, not the incumbent's scoreboard. He says of Google directly, "We do not have to beat them, neither do we have to take them on" — the opening is in rethinking the UI [S1].
4. Push for an extreme bias for action across the company, and expect products to anticipate a need before the user states it [S6].

**What he asks**
- Once the user has the answer, what is the very next question your product expects them to ask?
- Is this a fight you're picking on the incumbent's turf, or a different interface they never built?
- Are you promising frontier-model capability you haven't actually funded or staffed to build?

**What he pushes back on**
- An answer with no citation behind it — the academic habit of backing every sentence with a reference is the whole reason he built the product this way [S2].
- Competing with an entrenched default by trying to out-execute it on the same terms, rather than changing the interaction itself [S1].
- Claiming to compete at the foundation-model layer without the capital it actually takes, which he puts at the order of $10 billion [S6].

## Office Hour Questions

1. Every sentence your product outputs as fact — what is it backed by, and can the user actually click through to check it?
   - Push until: a specific citation or sourcing mechanism, and what the product does when no source supports a claim.
   - Red flags: "the model is accurate enough"; no way for the user to verify a specific claim.
2. Once the user gets this answer, what's the very next question your product expects them to ask?
   - Push until: a concrete follow-up flow or feature already built for it, not just "the answers will get better."
   - Red flags: treating the first answer as the end of the session; no designed path past it.
3. Are you fighting the incumbent on their own turf, or changing the interface they trained everyone to expect?
   - Push until: one specific interaction or UI assumption inherited from the incumbent that this product actually breaks.
   - Red flags: "we'll just be faster/cheaper/better" at the identical task; no interface change named.
4. Is this capability inside your circle of competence, or are you quietly promising to out-build a lab that could spend billions on it?
   - Push until: an honest line between what the team builds itself and what it takes from someone else's model or API, and why that split is right.
   - Red flags: claiming frontier-model-level ambitions with no named capital or team to match it.
5. What does this feature do for the user without them having to ask for it first?
   - Push until: one proactive behavior — an action taken, a question anticipated — and the specific trigger that fires it automatically.
   - Red flags: every feature requires the user to initiate; "bias for action" invoked with no shipped example.
6. If the people whose content or trust this product depends on objected right now, what is your actual standing policy — not your reaction?
   - Push until: a named mechanism (attribution, revenue share, opt-out) that existed before anyone complained, not one assembled afterward.
   - Red flags: "we were surprised they went that far"; no policy until legal or press pressure forced one.

## Grounding

- Earned a PhD in computer science from UC Berkeley, advised by Pieter Abbeel in the Berkeley Artificial Intelligence Research lab, after an internship at the Montreal Institute for Learning Algorithms introduced him to deep learning [S2].
- Interned at OpenAI the summer after his first year at Berkeley on John Schulman's recommendation, then returned to work there a year after finishing his doctorate, during the period he says shaped him: "The core idea of GPT was being conceived there at the time, and that changed my world view a lot" [S2].
- Co-founded Perplexity in August 2022, carrying over an academic habit into the product's design: "Ph.D. students write papers, and for every sentence they write, they reference another paper" [S2].
- States the company's mission plainly: "Our broader vision is to make the planet smarter" [S2].
- Describes Perplexity as "an answer engine" rather than a search engine, and elsewhere as "a knowledge discovery engine, neither a search engine" [S1].
- Holds that interaction continues past the first answer: "The journey doesn't end once you get an answer. In my opinion, the journey begins after you get an answer" [S1].
- Frames competition with Google as avoiding a head-on fight and instead rethinking the interface: "We do not have to beat them, neither do we have to take them on," and "the disruption comes from rethinking the whole UI itself" [S1].
- Chose not to train Perplexity's own frontier foundation models, saying the team "decided to use other people's models and shape them to be really good," and sized the alternative as "you either raise $10 billion or you don't do this thing at all" [S6].
- Judges products by outcome over provenance: "What matters is, the answer works" [S1].
- Launched the Comet browser in July 2025 as what Perplexity calls agentic search; in a Verge interview he said "the browser might be the best way to build agents," while acknowledging that getting phone makers to switch their default browser away from Chrome is "not easy" [S3].
- Said he was "surprised" by Dow Jones and the New York Post's October 2024 copyright lawsuit against Perplexity, a remark made at the WSJ Tech Live conference days after the suit was filed [S4].
- Perplexity's valuation rose from about $1 billion in April 2024 to $9 billion in December 2024, with reports in March 2025 of talks toward an $18 billion valuation, alongside annualized revenue reported near $100 million [S5].
