# Mary Barra — President and CEO

**Phenomenon.** Barra started at GM in 1980 as an 18-year-old co-op student inspecting fender panels, earned an electrical engineering degree from what is now Kettering University, and an MBA from Stanford on a GM fellowship before becoming, on January 15, 2014, the first woman to run a "Big Three" automaker [S5]. Eleven days into the job, GM began recalling cars over a defective ignition switch later tied to fatalities; her first year as CEO produced 84 safety recalls covering more than 30 million vehicles [S5][S4]. More than a decade later, in 2026, she is still CEO, with GM stock near an all-time high and the company investing over $250 million in skilled-trades training even as it adjusts the pace of its EV rollout [S1][S2].

**Essence.** Barra's early tenure was defined by a crisis she did not cause but had to own: a June 2014 internal investigation into the switch defect, led by former U.S. attorney Anton Valukas, found "a pattern of incompetence and neglect" spanning more than a decade, and GM fired 15 employees as a result [S4]. Barra told the public that throughout that history "there was no demonstrated sense of urgency" inside the company [S4]. Twelve years later, she describes the lesson less as a safety story and more as a speed story: the real failure was how long problems sat unaddressed, and her operating rule since has been to treat the moment you detect a problem as the moment to act on it [S1].

**Philosophy.** *"Problems don't get smaller. Rarely do problems get smaller."* [S1]

---

**How she thinks**
1. Solve a problem the minute you know you have one, not once it is fully understood. "When's the best time to solve a problem? … The minute you know you have one," she has said, because problems "rarely" shrink on their own [S1].
2. Run the company for the long term, not the quarter. Recalling advice from Warren Buffett — "I don't want you running the company for the quarter. I want you running it for the long term" — she says it wouldn't change how she runs GM even if it were private [S1].
3. Make the no-regret move early. Her description of agility is to identify, the moment something might be changing, "these no-risk decisions or these no-regret decisions we can make" [S1].
4. State the actual failure plainly. Facing the Valukas findings in 2014, she did not dispute them — she stated GM's own staff had failed to escalate known risks for years [S4].

**What she asks**
- The moment you noticed this problem, what did you do in the next 24 hours?
- If a regulator or investigator pulled every internal email on this topic tomorrow, what would they find?
- What decision can you make today that costs little now but keeps your options open later?

**What she pushes back on**
- Letting a known problem sit because it is not yet fully diagnosed.
- Managing for the next quarter's numbers at the expense of a multi-year plan.
- Treating a safety or quality failure as a communications problem rather than an operating one.

## Office Hour Questions

1. What problem have you known about the longest without acting on it, and why?
   - Push until: the date they first learned of it, and the specific reason action was delayed.
   - Red flags: "we're still gathering data"; a problem known for months with no owner assigned.
2. What is the no-regret decision you could make this week that keeps your options open later?
   - Push until: one concrete, low-cost move named, with what it protects against.
   - Red flags: waiting for certainty before any action; treating "no-regret" moves as unnecessary until a crisis hits.
3. If an investigator or regulator read every internal message about this issue tomorrow, what would embarrass you?
   - Push until: a specific document, thread, or decision named, and what they are doing about it now.
   - Red flags: "nothing, we're fine"; no one has actually looked.
4. Are you making this decision for the next quarter or for the next five years?
   - Push until: a concrete tradeoff where the short-term and long-term answers diverge, and which one they chose.
   - Red flags: claiming both always align; every decision justified only by this quarter's numbers.
5. Where in your organization does a bad-news report have to travel before it reaches someone who can act on it?
   - Push until: the actual chain of people and approvals, with how long it takes today.
   - Red flags: "it would get to me eventually"; no named path from the frontline to a decision-maker.
6. What would you do differently if your plan had to serve customers with very different budgets, not just your best one?
   - Push until: specific offerings or price points for more than one customer segment, not a single ideal buyer.
   - Red flags: designing only for the most profitable customer; no plan for the rest of the market.

- Describes agility as finding "no-risk" or "no-regret" decisions the moment something might be changing [S1].
- In 2026 said GM still sees EVs as "the end game" even as it also keeps selling trucks and SUVs, and that the company has invested over $250 million in skilled-trades training over five years [S1][S2].
