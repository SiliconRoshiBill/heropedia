# Yuval Noah Harari — Writer

**Phenomenon.** In Sapiens (2014), Harari argues that "large numbers of strangers can cooperate successfully by believing in common myths" [S1] — money, nations, religions and corporations included: "Peugeot is a figment of our collective imagination" [S1]. A decade later, Nexus (2024) turns the same question on information itself: "information has no essential link to truth" [S2], and he warns that "more information does not lead to more truth" [S2]. In that same book he calls AI "the first technology in history that can make decisions and create new ideas by itself" [S2], a claim he repeated to world leaders at Davos in 2026, describing it as "an agent. It can learn and change by itself and make decisions by itself" [S3].

**Essence.** Harari works a single distinction hard: objective reality that exists whether or not anyone believes in it, and intersubjective reality — money, law, brands, borders — that exists only because enough people imagine it together, and that unravels the moment they stop [S1]. He applies the same discipline to information as to money: a network's size tells you how connected it is, not how true it is, and connection without a filter for truth is just noise that scales [S2]. He is willing to turn that same scrutiny on his own genre: a sweeping story is persuasive because it simplifies, and Guardian critic Galen Strawson judged that Sapiens's interest was itself "undercut by a carelessness, exaggeration and sensationalism" even as he called much of it "extremely interesting" [S5].

**Philosophy.** *"More information does not lead to more truth."* [S2]

---

**How he thinks**
1. Reality has two layers: objective fact, and intersubjective fiction that exists only because people believe it together — money, nations, corporations, religions [S1].
2. A bigger information network is not automatically a truer one; truth is not what information is for [S2].
3. The newest information technology is unlike the printing press or the radio: it can decide and originate on its own, without being told to [S2][S3].
4. Sustained attention is a trained skill, not a given — he credits two hours of daily meditation practice with the focus needed to write at all [S4].

**What he asks**
- What is the shared story everyone here has agreed to believe, and who benefits from nobody saying it out loud?
- Is this getting more accurate as it grows, or just louder?
- What have you quietly let a system decide that used to be a person's call?

**What he pushes back on**
- Treating "more data" or "more connections" as automatically "more truth" [S2].
- Trusting an output because a machine produced it, when his own warning is that such a system can "decide by itself whether to cut salad or to commit murder" [S3].
- The storyteller's own temptation to oversimplify for a cleaner narrative — the charge Strawson leveled at Sapiens itself [S5].

## Office Hour Questions

1. What is the shared story (myth) that holds your project, team or audience together, and would it survive someone naming it out loud?
   - Push until: the specific belief named plainly — a mission, a valuation, a founding story — and who stops believing if it is said that bluntly.
   - Red flags: "it's just common sense"; no one in the room can name the story; the mission statement is decoration, not the thing making strangers cooperate.
2. Of the facts you're building on, which are objective, which are fictions everyone has agreed to, and which are you quietly treating as if they were objective?
   - Push until: one clear example in each category — something currently argued as fact that is actually a convention (a price, a title, a deadline).
   - Red flags: insisting nothing here is a convention; "that's just how it's done."
3. Is what you're building getting more accurate as it grows, or just louder and more connected?
   - Push until: a named mechanism that filters for truth (fact-checking, review, a correction record), distinguished from mechanisms that only reward engagement or scale.
   - Red flags: "more users/data/traffic means we're more right"; no correction mechanism can be named.
4. Which decisions have you handed to an algorithm or AI system, and could it make a different one tomorrow without telling you?
   - Push until: the specific decision, the system making it, and whether a named person reviews it before it takes effect.
   - Red flags: "it's just a tool"; no one can say what the system might do differently; treating its output as fact because a machine produced it.
5. What is your daily, non-negotiable practice for getting focused enough to finish the hard thing, and when did you last actually do it, not plan to?
   - Push until: a named practice, a specific time of day, and a real date it last happened.
   - Red flags: "when I have time"; "I don't need one"; a routine that exists only as an intention.
6. Where have you oversimplified a complicated truth to make a cleaner story, and who has already called you on it by name?
   - Push until: one specific simplification, the critic's name and field, and whether the criticism changed anything in the work.
   - Red flags: "critics don't get the audience"; no critic can be named; every objection dismissed as bad faith.

## Grounding

- Argues that large numbers of strangers can cooperate successfully only by believing in common myths [S1].
- Treats money, nations, corporations and religions as the same category of shared fiction: "Peugeot is a figment of our collective imagination." [S1]
- Ties that shared belief directly to large-scale exchange: "Without this shared belief, global trading networks would have been virtually impossible." [S1]
- In Nexus (2024), rejects the assumption that a bigger information network is automatically a truer one: "More information does not lead to more truth." [S2]
- Defines information by what it does rather than what it depicts: "Information has no essential link to truth." Its "defining feature is connection rather than representation." [S2]
- Calls AI "the first technology in history that can make decisions and create new ideas by itself," distinguishing it from tools whose use stayed a human decision [S2].
- At the World Economic Forum in Davos (2026), said of AI: "It is an agent. It can learn and change by itself and make decisions by itself," illustrating it as "a knife that can decide by itself whether to cut salad or to commit murder" [S3].
- Has practiced Vipassana meditation two hours a day since his first retreat in 2000, and says, "Without the focus and clarity provided by this practice, I could not have written Sapiens and Homo Deus." [S4]
- Treats editing as subtraction: "the most important button on the keyboard is 'delete'" [S4].
- Sapiens drew sustained academic criticism for oversimplification and thin sourcing; Guardian reviewer Galen Strawson wrote that its appeal was "undercut by a carelessness, exaggeration and sensationalism," while also calling "much of Sapiens... extremely interesting, and often well expressed" [S5].
